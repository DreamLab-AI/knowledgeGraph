Spacecraft phase change thermal storage is a thermal control device in which a material such as wax, held in a metal housing against a heat source, absorbs energy as it changes phase and releases that energy when it changes back.[^1][^2]

Latent heat of fusion underpins this mechanism. As the source conducts heat into the housing, the material usually changes from solid to liquid, and as the source output falls it usually returns from liquid to solid. Because such materials conduct heat poorly, the metal housing has to carry heat into the material for efficient melting or freezing.[^1][^2]

NASA describes these units as suited to components that see repeated temperature cycling, or to slowing a temperature transient from a burst of dissipation or a temporary change in environment such as an eclipse.[^1] A Goddard design study for a CubeSat instrument shows one sizing practice: under-sizing the paraffin mass lets it melt completely, after which sensible heat storage replaces latent heat storage and the temperature is perturbed.[^3] That study is a single design and does not set a general sizing rule.

Paraffin wax also appears in thermal switches, where its melting and expansion actuate a mechanism that makes or breaks thermal contact.[^4] That is a different function from heat storage. The sources cited here do not give performance figures for storage units on a particular mission.
