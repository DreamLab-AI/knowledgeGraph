A spacecraft radiator is a thermally conductive surface arranged to emit internally generated heat towards a cold radiative sink. Equipment may mount directly to its panel or connect through a spreader, strap, heat pipe or fluid loop. The transport path moves heat to the radiator; the exposed surface releases it by infrared radiation.[^1][^2][^3]

## Radiative balance and sizing

Radiator sizing starts with the heat to reject, permitted equipment and panel temperatures, usable area, surface emissivity and the time-varying radiative environment. Emission rises with the fourth power of absolute surface temperature. Raising rejection temperature can reduce required area, but the attached equipment, fluid and interfaces must tolerate that operating point.

Deep space is only one part of the view. Absorbed sunlight, planetary infrared radiation, reflected sunlight and radiation from other spacecraft surfaces depend on attitude, orbit, shielding and view factor. The net balance also includes parasitic heat loss from surfaces not designated as radiators.[^1] A quoted areal rejection value is therefore a rated point, not a transferable property of a panel.

A useful external finish has high infrared emissivity and low solar absorptivity. White coatings and second-surface silvered fluorinated ethylene propylene are established examples; MetOp uses silvered FEP on its radiator areas.[^1][^6] Contamination, ultraviolet exposure, charged particles and atomic oxygen can change optical properties. Hot-case sizing should use justified beginning- and end-of-life values and the applicable orientation rather than a pristine coupon figure.[^3]

## Heat paths and integration

Direct mounting reduces interfaces but can constrain equipment location and expose it to panel gradients. Remote equipment adds conductance through joints, straps, pipes or pumped lines. Contact pressure, surface finish, filler, bondline and fasteners can consume the temperature-drop budget before heat reaches the radiating face.[^1][^3]

Heat pipes or fluid passages may attach to a panel face or be embedded within it. Embedding can improve spreading and save structural mass, while making pressure containment and workmanship part of the radiator. It complicates manufacture, inspection, integration and repair; exposed fluid paths also add micrometeoroid risk.[^3]

A body-mounted panel competes with solar cells, antennas, sensors and payload apertures for area. A deployable radiator can expose both faces or add area after launch, but adds hinges, latches, flexible thermal transport, launch restraint and deployment failure modes. Joints must preserve conductance through thermal cycling and motion. A rendering or breadboard supports only its recorded development stage.[^1][^3]

## Control, failure and verification

Changing payload duty cycle, eclipse state or attitude can move a fixed radiator between hot and cold extremes. Louvres, shutters, variable-emittance surfaces, heaters or regulated fluid transport can reduce that range, with extra mass, power, mechanisms or control faults. A failed-open variable radiator can overcool hardware; a failed-closed state can remove hot-case capacity.

Other faults include degraded coating, contamination, a lost deep-space view, poor spreading, detached straps, heat-pipe dry-out, fluid leakage, blocked flow, deployment failure and impact damage. Redundant paths need separation and enough remaining area for the required load. Thermal-vacuum and thermal-balance testing should reproduce relevant sink boundaries, dissipation and configuration, then correlate the analytical model; a chamber result does not directly reproduce every orbital view factor.

## Mission and UK evidence

BepiColombo illustrates mission-specific shielding. ESA describes fins that shield a radiator and system testing that revealed underperforming heat pipes, prompting extra pipes.[^5] Airbus describes Solar Orbiter radiators kept in shadow and connected by pyrolytic-graphite straps; the Stevenage team also performed contamination-control bake-out work.[^8] These are documented mission arrangements, not universal radiator rules.

ESA's modular scalable-radiator activity assembled cold plates, loop-heat-pipe evaporators and up to four parallel panels and reported a 100–500 W test range.[^4] Airbus publishes HiPeR panel figures for embedded heat pipes, but labels the sheet non-contractual.[^9] Both sources need their stated conditions and maturity attached to any numerical claim.

UK company SmartIR reports that two variable-emittance test articles were deployed in low Earth orbit in January and March 2025. The company also describes continuing survivability and thermal-control tests and movement towards a qualified module.[^7] This supports a manufacturer-reported in-orbit demonstration, not independent proof of a generally qualified production radiator.
