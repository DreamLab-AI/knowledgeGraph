Theoretical astrophysics is the branch of astrophysics that builds mathematical and computational models of astronomical systems to explain observations and to predict phenomena not yet observed.[^1]

Harvard and Smithsonian's Center for Astrophysics describes theory as the means of connecting observational data into a fuller understanding, and says the field includes mathematical models of astronomical systems together with templates for fitting new results as they arrive.[^1] Toronto's Canadian Institute for Theoretical Astrophysics describes numerical simulation as one of the key components of modern astrophysics, naming hydrodynamic codes for gas flows and N-body codes for systems of many gravitationally interacting particles.[^2]

A review of core-collapse supernova mechanisms illustrates the practice. It reports progress from more sophisticated simulation tools for neutrino transport and from improved microphysics, and it also records open questions about which explosions the models can reproduce.[^3] That example shows theory working alongside observation, but a single review does not define the field.[^3]

Descriptions retrieved here come from two institutes and a review article. They describe working practice and scope, not a formal definition, and groups that emphasise analytic methods over simulation may describe the field differently.[^1][^2]
