Spaceborne lidar is the use of a laser pulse and a sensitive receiver on a spacecraft to measure the distance to a surface, or the vertical structure of the surface and the objects on it, from the time taken for backscattered light to return.[^1][^2]

NASA describes a lidar as transmitting a light pulse and using a receiver to measure the backscattered or reflected light, with distance obtained from the time between transmitted and backscattered pulses and the speed of light. A laser altimeter applies this to the height of the platform above the surface, from which topography is determined, using precise knowledge of the spacecraft orbit.[^1][^2]

Two designs illustrate the range. GEDI is a full-waveform lidar on the International Space Station that measures the three-dimensional structure of the surface. ICESat-2 carries a single instrument, ATLAS, that collects elevation data with six laser beams and is sensitive enough to detect individual photons.[^3][^4]

NASA notes that laser altimeters generally have a smaller footprint, and so finer spatial resolution, than radar altimeters, and require less power. The sources are mission descriptions and do not establish achieved accuracy, and a lidar is an active instrument, so it is distinct from passive optical imaging.[^1][^2][^3][^4]
