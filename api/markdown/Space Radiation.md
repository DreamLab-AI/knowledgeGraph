Space radiation is the mixed field of ionising particles encountered beyond the protection available at Earth's surface. It includes galactic cosmic rays, particles accelerated by solar events, trapped radiation belts and secondary particles produced when primary radiation strikes a spacecraft, habitat or human tissue.[^1]

## Exposure and health risk

Radiation hazard, dose and risk describe different things. The hazard is the radiation field and its capacity to cause harm. Absorbed and weighted dose quantify exposure and account, in different ways, for energy deposition and radiation type. Risk estimates the probability and severity of a health outcome using dosimetry, radiobiology and epidemiological models. A dose limit is therefore an operational and policy constraint, rather than a boundary below which biological harm is impossible.

High-energy particles can damage DNA and other cell structures. Research considers cancer, cataract, cardiovascular and central-nervous-system effects, alongside acute injury from a severe solar particle event.[^1] The biological effectiveness of high-linear-energy-transfer particles is difficult to infer from terrestrial evidence. A UKHSA-hosted peer-reviewed assessment notes that direct human evidence for the relative effectiveness of the relevant high-energy fields is sparse, so protection models necessarily contain uncertainty.[^2]

Low Earth orbit receives more radiation than Earth's surface but remains partly sheltered by the magnetosphere. Lunar and interplanetary crews spend more time beyond that protection. ISS measurements cannot simply be multiplied by mission duration to estimate a Mars exposure: particle spectra, solar activity, trajectory, shielding and secondary-particle production all change the result. ESA's 2026 Mars visualisation is explicitly based on a trajectory model and measurements from the Liulin-MO telescope, not a crewed Mars mission.[^3]

## Protection and limits

Controls include mission timing, monitoring and forecasting, personal and area dosimeters, shielding, spacecraft layout and a more heavily shielded refuge for solar particle events. These measures can reduce exposure, but no cited system removes the risk from galactic cosmic rays.

NASA's current human-system standard requires each crew member's career effective dose from spaceflight radiation to remain below 600 mSv and applies the limit to all ages and sexes. It also requires exposure to be kept as low as reasonably achievable.[^4] This is a NASA standard, not a universal safe-dose threshold. A 2026 National Academies assessment found that representative Mars scenarios can exceed the present career limit and identified continuing gaps in neutron dosimetry, high-energy-particle biology, shielding effectiveness and solar-event forecasting.[^5]
