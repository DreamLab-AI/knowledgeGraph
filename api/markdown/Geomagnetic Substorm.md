A geomagnetic substorm is a sudden release of energy by the magnetosphere, the protective magnetic bubble surrounding Earth, that occurs in the magnetic fields of near-Earth space.[^1]

NASA states that some of the energy and particles released reach the upper atmosphere and create the Northern and Southern lights. The THEMIS page also says that substorms start small, spread across large regions of the magnetosphere within minutes, and typically accompany the most intense space weather.[^1]

Substorms vary a good deal. A 2019 study of Van Allen Probe observations describes substorms as occurring either as isolated events or in sequences of compound substorms, and reports differences in ring current energy content between the two cases.[^2]

NOAA describes the magnetosphere as highly dynamic, with regions such as the plasmasphere, the ring current and the radiation belts. The NASA page says that before THEMIS launched, scientists knew that substorms caused auroras but not what initiated the substorms, so the initiation mechanism should be treated as a research topic and not as settled by these sources.[^3][^1]
