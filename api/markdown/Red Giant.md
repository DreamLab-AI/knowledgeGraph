A red giant is a large, luminous, cool star that has left the main sequence after exhausting the hydrogen in its core.[^1][^2]

NASA describes a main-sequence star below eight times the Sun’s mass collapsing at the core when core hydrogen runs out. Squeezing the core raises its temperature and pressure until helium fuses to carbon, while hydrogen fusion moves into the outer layers and makes them expand.[^1] A university textbook gives a similar sequence in which the core contracts and fusion continues in a shell around it, so the star becomes larger, redder and more luminous. It describes more massive stars as becoming red supergiants instead.[^2]

NASA notes that a red giant would appear more orange than red. It can become unstable, pulsate and eject part of its atmosphere, and after all the outer layers have blown away the core remains as a white dwarf.[^1] The textbook adds that some red giants are large enough that their outer atmosphere would reach the orbit of Mars or beyond if one replaced the Sun.[^2] That size is an illustration for the larger cases and not a typical radius.

Both sources describe the route for stars like the Sun, so the details differ for other masses. Neither establishes an exact boundary for the label, and the NASA mass limit applies to the path described, not to every star that is called a giant.[^1][^2]

[^2]: OpenStax, *Astronomy 2e*, section 22.1, “Evolution from the Main Sequence to Red Giants”. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/22-1-evolution-from-the-main-sequence-to-red-giants
