Geometric calibration is the determination, for an Earth observation instrument and its platform, of the geometric model that links each detector to a viewing direction, so that image positions can be placed on the Earth's surface.[^1][^2]

The USGS describes it as driven by geometric characterisation of spacecraft, instrument and image product, and as involving calibration of the spacecraft and instruments.[^1] The instrument line of sight is derived from static focal plane characteristics combined with telemetry.[^1] For Sentinel-2, an automatic correlation against a reference image supplies ground control points that refine the viewing model for changes in attitude, position or thermoelastic deformation.[^2]

Calibration is separable from the correction applied to each scene. Geometric correction uses telemetry and external resources to remove small random errors in the inputs.[^1] The Landsat systematic product applies only ephemeris-based corrections, whereas the precision and terrain product is orthorectified with ground control points and a digital elevation model.[^3] NASA lists geometric calibration coefficients among the ancillary information annotated on reconstructed instrument data.[^4]

Landsat and Sentinel-2 practice is all the retained sources describe. They do not establish a mission-independent calibration procedure, and no accuracy figure is stated here because the accuracies in those sources belong to particular missions and processing tiers.
