Suborbital spaceflight is a flight in which a spacecraft follows a non-orbital trajectory and returns without completing an orbit around Earth.[^1437][^1453][^1457]

Flight regime identifies the trajectory; it does not specify the vehicle's shape, the people aboard or the mission's purpose. A suborbital arc may reach the upper atmosphere or space by a chosen convention, coast briefly, and then descend. It does not acquire a continuing Earth orbit merely by crossing a named altitude; velocity and the complete path matter.[^1437][^1453][^1457]

There is no universally agreed altitude at which airspace ends and outer space begins. The FAI retains 100 km as a practical Kármán-line convention for its records, while the US FAA uses 50 statute miles for its human-spaceflight recognition list and says that it does not define where space begins. These conventions serve different purposes and are not a universal legal boundary.[^1431][^1442][^1457]

UK regulation uses its own activity and capability tests. The Space Industry Act covers sub-orbital activities involving a rocket or other craft capable of operating above the stratosphere, as well as a crewed or passenger-carrying balloon capable of reaching it. The CAA describes the relevant stratosphere level as about 47 km in its large-rocket guidance and around 50 km in its legislation overview. These are rounded regulatory descriptions, not substitutes for the Act's wording.[^1430][^1432][^1433]

Suborbital missions may carry people, research equipment or other payloads, and may be experimental or commercial. Human carriage and commercial purpose are attributes of the mission; neither defines the trajectory. The FAA describes commercial human spaceflight in the US, but this does not establish UK operational status or licensing. A short suborbital flight can support high-altitude research or brief microgravity work, but duration, acceleration, microgravity quality and return loads depend on the vehicle and flight profile.[^1430][^1437][^1457]

For UK activity, the applicable licence depends on the activity and where it is carried out. The Space Industry Act applies to space and sub-orbital activities carried out in the UK. Separate Air Navigation Order permission can apply to specified large rockets that cannot exceed the stratosphere. Do not infer a UK licence category from the Kármán line alone.[^1432][^1433]
