An imaging spectrometer is an instrument that records images in many narrow, contiguous spectral bands, so that each pixel has its own spectrum.[^1]

Data form a spectral cube with two spatial dimensions and one spectral dimension, and a reflectance curve can be plotted for each pixel. eoPortal says such spectra are compared with libraries of known material spectra to identify surface materials. It also describes imaging spectroscopy as the wider term that includes hyperspectral imaging.[^1]

Scanning designs differ. The airborne AVIRIS is described as a whiskbroom system with scanning foreoptics that acquires data across the track. On Deep Space 1, the Miniature Integrated Camera Spectrometer combined cameras with ultraviolet and infrared imaging spectrometers that shared one telescope and operated in push-broom mode, so that the instrument had to sweep across the target body.[^2][^3]

None of the cited sources sets a band count or bandwidth that separates an imaging spectrometer from a multispectral imager, and instrument specifications vary. Radiometric calibration and atmospheric correction are needed before spectra can be compared, and the cited pages do not cover them.[^1][^2][^3]
