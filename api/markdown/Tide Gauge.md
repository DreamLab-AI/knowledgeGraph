A tide gauge is an instrument that records changes in sea level relative to a datum, a height reference, using a mechanical, acoustic or radar sensing method.[^1][^2]

The IOC manual lists four measuring technologies in common use: a stilling well with a float, pressure systems, acoustic systems and radar systems. It describes the float in a stilling well as measuring sea level directly, whereas pressure and acoustic systems work through an indirect parameter such as pressure or sound. NOAA describes recorders that send an acoustic signal down a sounding tube and time the reflection from the water surface.[^1][^2]

Gauge heights are tied to a land benchmark. The manual says measurements must be made relative to a fixed and permanent local tide gauge bench mark, and the Global Geodetic Observing System says tide gauges therefore record relative sea level, so local land movement is part of what they record. It proposes co-locating a GNSS receiver to separate land movement from the sea-level signal.[^2][^3]

The National Oceanography Centre notes that such instruments are more properly called water level recorders because they can also be used in lakes and rivers.[^4] The retained sources describe the instrument and its datum practice. They do not describe how gauge records are combined with satellite altimetry, so that comparison is not covered here.
