- ### Definition
  - [[Autonomous Behavior]] is the property of an agent, robotic system, or AI-driven software that enables it to act purposefully in an environment without moment-to-moment human direction. The agent perceives environmental state through its [[Perception System]], maintains an internal goal representation, selects actions via a decision policy (rule-based, learned, or planned), and adjusts its strategy based on feedback from action outcomes. The degree of autonomy is characterised by the breadth of situations handled, the temporal horizon of planning, and the degree to which human oversight is required or sought.

- ### Relationships
  - Autonomous Behavior requires a [[Perception System]] to sense environmental state and a [[Planning Module]] to select action sequences towards goals. These capabilities together enable the creation of [[Autonomous Agent]]s capable of [[Autonomous Task Execution]]. The concept is central to [[Agentic AI]] systems and is studied through [[Behavioral Modeling]]. [[Goal]] representations define the objective state the agent pursues, while [[Feedback Loop]]s connect action outcomes to policy adjustment.

- ### Content
  - The theoretical foundations of autonomous behaviour derive from cybernetics (Wiener, 1948), which framed goal-directed behaviour in terms of feedback-controlled systems. Early robotics research in the 1960s and 1970s — exemplified by Stanford's Shakey robot — demonstrated deliberative planning in simple environments using symbolic AI planners that constructed action sequences from world-state descriptions. The limitation of these "sense-plan-act" architectures was brittleness under incomplete or noisy perception, motivating Rodney Brooks's subsumption architecture (1986), which produced reactive behaviours via layered stimulus-response rules without internal world models.

  - Contemporary autonomous behaviour systems integrate reactive and deliberative components in hierarchical or hybrid architectures. The BDI (Beliefs-Desires-Intentions) model formalises internal goal structures for cognitive agents. Reinforcement learning provides a mechanism for learning behaviour policies from environmental interaction, replacing hand-coded rules with learned mappings from state to action. Deep reinforcement learning (combining neural perception with RL policy optimisation) has produced systems capable of mastering complex games, robotic manipulation, and locomotion across varied terrain. Large language models add a new layer — enabling agents to reason about goals in natural language and decompose high-level objectives into executable action sequences.

  - Autonomous behaviour is deployed across a wide spectrum: industrial mobile robots navigating warehouses without pre-defined paths, autonomous vehicles managing complex traffic scenarios, drone swarms conducting coordinated surveillance or delivery missions, software agents executing multi-step research and code-generation workflows, and financial trading systems executing strategies across millisecond timescales. Each application domain imposes different constraints on latency, safety margins, interpretability of decisions, and the acceptable scope of unilateral action.

  - In 2024–2025, autonomous behaviour capabilities are advancing rapidly due to the convergence of improved foundation models, better simulation environments for policy training, and increasing sensor and compute availability. Simultaneously, the safety and alignment implications of highly autonomous systems are receiving intense scrutiny: AI safety researchers study goal misgeneralisation, reward hacking, and specification gaming — failure modes that emerge when an autonomous agent optimises a proxy metric rather than the intended human objective. Regulatory frameworks for autonomous vehicles, medical devices, and agentic AI systems are being developed to mandate human-oversight interfaces, explainability requirements, and certification processes for behaviour under edge cases.
