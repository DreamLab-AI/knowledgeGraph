- ### Definition
  - Mobile robotics is the discipline of constructing and programming autonomous robots capable of self-directed movement through physical environments to accomplish spatially-distributed tasks. It integrates mechanical design of locomotion systems (wheeled, tracked, legged, aerial, aquatic), sensor-based [[Sensor Fusion]] for world perception, simultaneous localisation and mapping (SLAM) for state estimation, [[Path Planning]] for collision-free route generation, and motion control for execution of planned trajectories. The field spans service robots operating in human-shared indoor environments, field robots navigating outdoor terrain, and [[Autonomous Vehicle]] platforms on public roads and in airspace.

- ### Relationships
  - Mobile robots rely on [[Sensor Fusion]] to combine LiDAR, camera, IMU, and ultrasonic sensor data into coherent environment representations. [[Path Planning]] algorithms (A*, RRT*, D* Lite) compute collision-free routes through the environment model. [[Computer Vision]] enables semantic understanding of scenes — detecting obstacles, recognising landmarks, tracking humans. Mobile robotics underlies [[Logistics Automation]] through autonomous mobile robot (AMR) platforms in warehouses, and the principles directly extend to [[Autonomous Vehicle]] systems for road transport. [[Reinforcement Learning for Robotics]] provides a pathway to learning locomotion policies and adaptive navigation behaviours without hand-coded controllers.

- ### Content
  - The foundations of mobile robotics were established by Shakey the Robot at SRI International (1966–1972), the first robot to use AI for real-world navigation and task execution. Shakey combined symbolic planning, vision-based feature detection, and graph-search navigation in a physically mobile platform, demonstrating the feasibility of autonomous goal-directed robot action. The 1980s and 1990s saw the development of probabilistic approaches to robotics — Kalman filters and particle filters for localisation under sensor noise — codified in the probabilistic robotics framework of Thrun, Burgard, and Fox. The DARPA Grand Challenge (2004, 2005) and Urban Challenge (2007) catalysed autonomous outdoor navigation on vehicles, demonstrated by teams from Stanford, CMU, and others.

  - Simultaneous Localisation and Mapping (SLAM) is the core technical problem of mobile robotics: a robot must build a map of an unknown environment while simultaneously estimating its own position within that map, using only onboard sensor data. Extended Kalman Filter SLAM (EKF-SLAM), particle filter SLAM (FastSLAM), and graph-based SLAM approaches have each been dominant at different periods. Modern SLAM systems — ORB-SLAM3, RTAB-Map, LIO-SAM — fuse visual, LiDAR, and inertial measurements to produce accurate maps in real time, enabling operation in GPS-denied environments such as indoor warehouses, underground mines, and planetary surfaces.

  - Wheeled AMRs (Autonomous Mobile Robots) are the dominant commercial instantiation of mobile robotics in 2024, with platforms from Fetch Robotics, Mobile Industrial Robots (MiR), Geek+, and 6 River Systems deployed in millions of warehouse square metres globally. Legged robots — Boston Dynamics Spot, ANYbotics ANYmal — address unstructured terrain inaccessible to wheeled platforms, finding applications in industrial inspection, search and rescue, and construction site monitoring. Aerial mobile robots (drones) represent the highest-growth segment, with applications spanning precision agriculture, infrastructure inspection, and parcel delivery. Humanoid mobile robot platforms (Boston Dynamics Atlas, Agility Digit, Figure 02) are transitioning from research to early industrial deployment.

  - By 2024–2025, mobile robotics is experiencing a step change driven by the integration of deep learning and foundation models. Neural [[Computer Vision]] — particularly instance segmentation and 3D object detection with transformer architectures — has superseded classical feature-based perception in most commercial platforms. Large navigation models trained on diverse robot trajectory datasets are demonstrating zero-shot generalisation to new environments, reducing the need for environment-specific map building. Physical AI models such as RT-2 and OpenVLA combine language understanding with motor policy generation, enabling instruction-following navigation and manipulation. Safety certification of mobile robots for human-shared spaces (ISO 3691-4, IEC 61508) remains a bottleneck for broader deployment in public-access environments.
