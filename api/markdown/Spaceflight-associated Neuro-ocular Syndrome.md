Spaceflight-associated neuro-ocular syndrome (SANS) is the name NASA gives to a collection of eye and brain changes seen in astronauts, including optic nerve swelling, folds in the retina and flattening of the back of the eye.[^1][^2]

NASA describes the proposed explanation as chronic weightlessness moving fluids such as blood and cerebrospinal fluid toward the head.[^1] The NASA risk entry lists the underlying mechanisms, including the role of intracranial pressure, among its major knowledge gaps.[^2] A 2026 consensus paper describes SANS as a set of clinical features that appear inconsistently between astronauts and says the processes behind them may be multifactorial.[^3]

NASA's risk entry names optic disc oedema, chorioretinal folds, globe flattening and hyperopic shifts in refractive error as hallmark signs, and reports that 70% of crew members show one or more of them.[^2] The 2026 paper reports at least one feature in 81% of NASA astronauts.[^3] The two figures come from different documents, and the sources do not say whether they use the same crew population or sign definitions, so they should not be merged into one rate.

NASA says some astronauts have only mild changes while others have clinically significant outcomes, that risk is higher on longer missions, and that the long-term health outcome is unknown.[^1] Supplements, mechanical measures against headward fluid shifts and head-down-tilt bed rest studies are described as research directions.[^1] The retained sources do not establish a validated countermeasure.
