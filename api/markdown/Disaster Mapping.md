Disaster mapping converts satellite imagery and other geospatial information into products for preparedness, emergency response and recovery. Depending on the request, a product may show the pre-event setting, the observed extent of a hazard, exposed assets, or an interpreted grade of damage. Each map is tied to an area, an acquisition time and the evidence available to its producer.

## Activation and production

The International Charter Space and Major Disasters gives authorised government and emergency-response bodies access to participating satellite resources without charge. The UK Space Agency is a Charter member and works with UK operators and public bodies during activations.[^1] An authorised user submits a request to a continuously staffed operator. An emergency officer identifies suitable satellites, participating operators task them where possible, and a project manager coordinates interpretation and delivery by value-adding specialists. Ground verification can be included when available. Activations typically close within several weeks, depending on the scale and evolution of the event.[^2]

The 24-hour contact point describes service availability, not the delivery time of a suitable image or finished map. A satellite must be able to acquire the affected area, data must reach the processor, and analysts must produce and check the requested information.

Copernicus Emergency Management Service Rapid Mapping provides standardised products on demand, normally on an hours-to-days timescale after a disaster. An activation can contain several areas of interest and several products for each area. Completed products are delivered to the requester and normally published unless the activation is sensitive.[^3] A map is therefore an interpreted event product, rather than a continuously updated view or an exhaustive record of conditions on the ground.

## Quality and observation limits

Copernicus service providers and the European Commission's Joint Research Centre apply checks for completeness, thematic and positional accuracy, topology, attributes and consistency. A product that fails is rejected and revised, with a replacement required within 24 hours.[^4] These controls test the product against defined criteria. They cannot recover damage hidden from the sensor or prove that every classified feature has been independently inspected in the field.

Acquisition conditions shape both latency and completeness. Optical imagery can be blocked by cloud or smoke, while radar works through cloud and darkness but has its own terrain, vegetation and interpretation limits. The Charter's 2022 operations report records cases in which the affected region's size or cloud cover prevented a requested data type from meeting user needs. User feedback also called for faster post-disaster radar imagery.[^5]

## UK contribution and programme status

The Charter reached its 1,000th activation in 2025. The UK Space Agency reports that the UK contributed to more than 60 disaster incidents that year and supplied high-value Earth-observation products for 53 activations.[^6] These counts show operational participation; they do not measure the accuracy of every map.

One dated example came from flooding in Scotland in November 2022. The UK Space Agency reported that RADARSAT Constellation Mission imagery helped the Scottish Environment Protection Agency assess flood extent and water levels over large areas for warnings and rescue planning.[^8] Results and delivery times from that case should not be assumed for a later event.

CEOS places disaster observation in the wider cycle of risk reduction, response and recovery. Its Working Group on Disasters coordinates agencies and runs pilots and demonstrators as well as other activities.[^7] A coordinated pilot demonstrates a method or working arrangement; it becomes an operational capability only when the responsible service documents that status.
