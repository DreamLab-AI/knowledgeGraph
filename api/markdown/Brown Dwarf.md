A brown dwarf is a substellar object more massive than a planet but too light to sustain the hydrogen fusion of a main-sequence star.[^1]

NASA describes brown dwarfs as not technically stars, with masses generally between 13 and 80 times that of Jupiter. They emit almost no visible light, although a few have been seen in infrared. Some may form in the same way as main-sequence stars, from clumps of gas and dust, and others may form like planets in disks around stars, so the sources do not tie the class to one formation route.[^1]

Its lower boundary is less settled. A published model study states that there is no universally acknowledged criterion separating brown dwarfs from planets, and that a limit near 13 Jupiter masses, based on deuterium fusion, is a reasonable rule of thumb whose value depends on composition and on how much deuterium must burn.[^2] A mass figure should therefore be read as approximate, not as a sharp definition.

These sources do not give formation statistics, temperature classes or detection limits.

[^2]: D. S. Spiegel, A. Burrows and J. A. Milsom, “The Deuterium-Burning Mass Limit for Brown Dwarfs and Giant Planets”, *The Astrophysical Journal* 727, 57 (2011), arXiv:1008.5150 v2. https://arxiv.org/abs/1008.5150
