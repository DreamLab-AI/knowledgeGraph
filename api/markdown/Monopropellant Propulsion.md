Monopropellant propulsion stores and feeds one propellant formulation to the thruster. A catalyst, heat or another initiation mechanism triggers decomposition or reaction, producing hot gas that expands through a nozzle. `Monopropellant` describes the single stored feed: an ionic-liquid blend can contain both fuel and oxidising species within that formulation.[^1][^3]

## Thruster and feed architecture

A pressure-fed system commonly comprises a propellant tank, pressurant or blowdown volume, isolation and flow-control valves, filter, lines, injector, catalyst bed or chamber, heaters, temperature sensing and a nozzle.[^1][^2] It avoids the second tank and matched fuel/oxidiser plumbing of a bipropellant system, but integrated performance still depends on inlet pressure, valve response, thermal state and propellant acquisition.

Hydrazine spacecraft thrusters normally pass the liquid over an iridium-on-alumina catalyst such as Shell 405. Exothermic decomposition produces the hot gases; it is therefore more precise to describe catalytic decomposition than to say simply that hydrazine burns.[^1] The catalyst bed often needs thermal conditioning. Contamination, mechanical degradation, cold starts, pulse history and cumulative throughput can change ignition delay and delivered impulse.

## Performance with conditions

Nammo's UK-made MHT-1N data sheet specifies 1.295 N nominal thrust at 24 bar absolute and more than 228 seconds specific impulse at its 1.38 N maximum thrust.[^10] The same record gives a demonstrated 15 ms minimum on-time, but those figures belong to the stated thruster, pressure, valve and hydrazine configuration.

Its ESA qualification figures include 19.19 kg hydrazine throughput, 36,849 N s total impulse, 87,818 pulses and two cold starts with the catalyst bed between 5 °C and 10 °C. Nammo also records a January 2018 first flight on SSTL's Telesat LEO-1.[^10] These are useful bounded flight and qualification data, rather than general limits for 1 N-class monopropellant systems.

NASA's Green Propellant Infusion Mission tested the ASCENT formulation, formerly AF-M315E, with its compatible tanks, valves and five thrusters in orbit. Planned attitude-control and orbit-lowering manoeuvres demonstrated the projected mission performance.[^3] NASA described a 50% improvement in `gas mileage` over the mission's hydrazine comparison; that result should remain attached to GPIM and not be recast as a universal specific-impulse or spacecraft-mass benefit.

## Propellants, handling and storability

Hydrazine has extensive flight heritage, but it is corrosive, highly toxic and potentially carcinogenic. Loading operations use specialist controls and protective ensembles: ESA's June 2023 Euclid fuelling record documents 140 kg in one central tank and technicians wearing self-contained atmospheric protective suits.[^8] UK Health Security Agency guidance provides current health-effect and incident-response context, while project-specific standards govern spacecraft processing.[^9]

Lower-toxicity formulations such as ASCENT can reduce vapour and personal-protective-equipment burdens relative to hydrazine, but they remain energetic chemicals and require compatible tanks, seals, valves, filters and heaters.[^1][^3] `Green` is therefore a relative handling label, not evidence of harmlessness or a complete lifecycle assessment.

Storability has thermal limits. NASA reports that hydrazine freezes near 1.6 °C; freeze–thaw can rupture confined plumbing, compromise seals and valves, create slush blockage and cause a hard start or misfire.[^6] Temperature sensors alone may not capture the coldest point, so analysis, heaters, insulation and controlled ground handling need uncertainty margin.

## Qualification and failure

Qualification must represent pressure, propellant and catalyst temperature, pulses and long burns, minimum impulse bit, restarts, throughput, valve cycles and environmental loads.[^2] Before GPIM, NASA tested non-flight 1 N and 22 N valves from 125 to 410 psig at multiple temperatures because ASCENT's fluid properties vary with temperature.[^4] Separate vacuum plume work used spectroscopy and imaging, followed by pulse and steady tests, before the successful 13-month flight demonstration.[^5]

Credible failures include leakage, a valve stuck open or closed, filter or injector blockage, catalyst poisoning or breakup, insufficient preheat, thermal hard start and falling performance with contamination. A NASA technical bulletin reports that contaminants not fully controlled by the high-purity hydrazine procurement specification have caused flow decay in small thrusters.[^7] Redundant valves and filtration reduce selected risks but do not remove the need for cleanliness, compatible materials and health monitoring.

## UK capability

The MHT-1N establishes a bounded UK example: it was developed by Nammo UK at Westcott, qualified for ESA's NeoSat platforms and flown on Telesat LEO-1 according to the manufacturer's data sheet.[^10] Those claims concern the named thruster. A new integrated propulsion subsystem still needs qualification for its own tankage, feed, control, thermal design, duty cycle and mission environment.
