Van Allen radiation belts are regions encircling Earth in which energetic electrons and ions are trapped by Earth’s magnetic field.[^1][^2]

NASA describes two donut-shaped regions, with an inner belt reaching from about one to eight thousand miles above the equator and an outer belt from about 12 to 25 thousand miles. NOAA’s Space Weather Prediction Center gives electron belts in Earth radii instead: an inner belt from about 1.5 to 3 and an outer belt from about 3 to 10, measured from Earth’s centre in the equatorial plane, with a slot region between them attributed to losses through interaction with whistler waves. It places a separate belt of high-energy protons at about 1.1 to 3 Earth radii. The two agencies use different reference distances and units, so their figures are not directly comparable.[^1][^2]

NOAA describes the belts as highly dynamic, changing over minutes to years, and says their motion is controlled largely by magnetic and electric fields shaped by interaction between the solar wind and Earth’s magnetic and plasma environment. NASA’s Van Allen Probes returned the first data showing a transient third belt, which can form during intense solar activity. NOAA adds that much remains uncertain about how belt particles are energised, transported and lost.[^2][^3]

Energetic protons and galactic cosmic rays can alter the electronic state of sensitive devices on satellites, according to NOAA, and high-energy electrons can damage cables and chips through deep dielectric charging. NOAA also states that MeV electrons reaching the upper atmosphere can deplete ozone. The retained sources describe Earth’s belts only; they do not address the origin of the name or belts at other planets.[^2]
