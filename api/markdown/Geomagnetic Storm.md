A geomagnetic storm is a major disturbance of Earth's magnetosphere that occurs when there is a very efficient exchange of energy from the solar wind into the space environment surrounding Earth.[^1]

NOAA attributes storms to variations in the solar wind that change the currents, plasmas and fields in the magnetosphere. The largest storms are associated with coronal mass ejections, and high-speed solar wind streams can also create favourable conditions, generally for storms that are less intense but may deposit energy over a longer interval.[^1]

A storm also changes the radiation belts and the ionosphere, and heats the thermosphere. Its size is characterised with indices: the disturbance storm time index has been used historically, and the planetary Kp index is the basis for the NOAA geomagnetic storm scale used to describe space weather that can disrupt systems on Earth.[^1]

A storm differs from a substorm, which the NASA THEMIS mission describes as a sudden release of energy by the magnetosphere. NOAA says the magnetopause shields Earth from the solar wind only to some extent, and that energy, mass and momentum are transferred into the magnetosphere. The sources cited here do not give a threshold for calling an event a storm outside the NOAA scale, and the BGS page only covers monitoring and hazards in general terms.[^2][^3][^4]
