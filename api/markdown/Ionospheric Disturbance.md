An ionospheric disturbance is a general term for a change in the ionosphere, such as enhanced lower-layer ionisation after a solar flare or density variations during a geomagnetic storm, that disturbs radio propagation.[^1][^2]

In the flare case, NOAA says the increased X-ray and EUV radiation arrives at the speed of light, so ionisation of the lower layers on the sunlit side occurs at the same time as the event is observed. The added ionisation can degrade or fully absorb HF radio signals.[^1]

In the storm case, NOAA says storms change the ionosphere and heat it, and that local heating creates strong horizontal variations in ionospheric density that can modify the path of radio signals and create errors in GPS positioning. Total electron content is modified by solar EUV radiation, geomagnetic storms and atmospheric waves that propagate up from the lower atmosphere, and depends on local time, latitude, season and solar activity.[^2][^3]

Small-scale structure produces scintillation, the rapid modification of radio waves by structures in electron density along the signal path. NOAA quantifies it with an amplitude index and a phase index, says it is more prevalent at low and high latitudes, and says severe scintillation can prevent a GPS receiver from locking on to the signal.[^4]

These pages are NOAA descriptions, not a single classification scheme, and they give no thresholds that separate a disturbance from ordinary variation. Regular day and night differences follow solar irradiance and are not by themselves a disturbance.[^1][^5]
