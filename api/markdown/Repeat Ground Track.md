A repeat ground track is an orbit geometry designed to bring the subsatellite path back over the same Earth-fixed locations after a specified cycle.

A repeat condition relates the satellite’s nodal period to Earth rotation over an integer cycle of orbital revolutions and Earth days. For mission design, use nodal period and include the orbit-plane precession caused by Earth’s nonspherical gravity. Altitude, inclination and eccentricity affect this relation. A first-order integer-orbits-per-day sketch omits those perturbations and does not set a precise reference track.[^1276][^1283]

A recurring ground-track curve does not by itself mean the spacecraft returns to the same complete state. The orbit may cross the same locations at a different time of day or with different along-track phase, attitude, lighting and observation conditions. Swath width and satellite phasing can also provide revisit opportunities before the full repeat cycle is complete.[^1277][^1278]

Operational missions maintain a reference pattern within stated tolerances and can manoeuvre to correct drift. Envisat, for example, used a 35-day repeat cycle and specified a maximum deviation of plus or minus one kilometre from its reference ground track. The requirement describes that mission rather than a universal definition of repeatability.[^1277][^1279]

Peer-reviewed research held by the University of Surrey examines high-precision repeat-track design and maintenance, including higher-order zonal gravity, atmospheric-drag decay and uncertainty in orbit measurements, control and decay-rate estimates. It supports treating repeatability as an orbit-maintenance problem with a defined model and tolerance, rather than as an exact property that persists without control.[^1284]
