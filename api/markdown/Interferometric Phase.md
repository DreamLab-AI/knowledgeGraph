Interferometric phase is the pixel-by-pixel phase difference between two coregistered SAR acquisitions of the same area, which an interferogram records.[^1][^2]

ESA's SNAP tutorial says an interferogram is formed by multiplying the reference image by the complex conjugate of the secondary image, and that the phase of each pixel then depends only on the difference in travel paths from the two acquisitions to the resolution cell.[^1] NASA adds that interferometry is concerned only with the difference in phase from multiple passes or positions, and that phase is affected by the ground surface, the satellite's orbital position and topography.[^2] ESA explains why this works: if a target is re-acquired from the same point in space, the signal phase should in theory stay the same, so a ground shift between acquisitions alters it.[^3]

Measured phase is not yet a height or a displacement. That tutorial identifies a flat-earth contribution from the curvature of the reference surface, and notes that the phase is ambiguous and known only within the scale of 2π until it is unwrapped.[^1] The NASA SAR Handbook states the goal as measuring surface topography, surface deformation, or both, from phase differences between two or more acquisitions.[^4]

Retained sources do not establish accuracy figures, coherence thresholds or a processing level for interferometric products. They treat the term in the repeat-pass and topographic settings, and sensor-specific wavelengths are not asserted here.[^1][^2]
