
Radio-frequency communication carries information by modulating a radio-frequency electromagnetic carrier for transmission and recovering the information at a receiver.

In a spacecraft system, RF is one physical means of carrying a link. A transmitter creates a modulated signal and an antenna radiates it; a receiving antenna and radio front end detect the signal for demodulation and further processing. Free-space optical or laser communication also uses electromagnetic waves, but is an optical-frequency implementation rather than RF.[^1][^5]

Band names such as S-band are engineering shorthand, not permissions to transmit. ESA gives S-band a broad indicative range of 2–4 GHz and lists typical applications, while the ITU Radio Regulations set out allocations by frequency range, radiocommunication service, direction, Region and footnotes. National administrations apply those frameworks through their own rules and authorisations. A radio specification or familiar band label does not establish a station’s right to use a frequency.[^6][^9]

Allowed use depends on the service, direction, station class, technical parameters and jurisdiction. Ofcom’s current UK guidance lists distinct earth-station licence types; its non-geostationary fixed-satellite-service authorisations cover specified Ku/Ka deployments. These are UK licence conditions for described station types, not a global allocation plan.[^7][^8] Higher frequencies can offer access to wider bandwidths, yet propagation loss, precipitation and atmospheric absorption can also affect availability. Antenna design, elevation, pointing, modulation, power control and link margin matter alongside frequency; ESA’s Q/V-band power-control example concerns high-frequency feeder links rather than a universal threshold.[^9][^10]

RF performance also depends on geometry, distance and receiver design. NASA’s Deep Space Network material documents specialised large apertures and low-noise reception for weak deep-space returns; these are characteristics of that network, not generic specifications for all earth stations. Modulation, synchronisation and channel coding are selected as part of the link design. CCSDS recommendations define particular protocol and coding functions but do not prescribe one universal frequency, waveform, power level or link budget.[^3][^4][^5]

Relative motion changes the received carrier frequency through Doppler shift. Tracking systems can measure that shift as an observable for spacecraft tracking, navigation or radio science; it is not the same operation as decoding telemetry. The shift depends on the radial motion, carrier frequency and link geometry.[^2][^11]
