Star formation is the process by which stars form from the gas and dust of molecular clouds, through the collapse of dense clumps into protostars.[^1]

NASA describes stars forming in large molecular clouds, where gravity causes some clumps to collapse and heat up, giving a protostar.[^1] Theoretical reviews add that dense clouds collapse hierarchically, with bulk kinetic energy turned into turbulence and stars forming from cores fed by filaments.[^2] These are model-based descriptions of a process that is observed only indirectly in the dust-obscured regions where it occurs.

Stars often form in groups. NASA calls batches of recently formed stars stellar clusters, and the review literature treats cluster formation as part of the same collapse process.[^1][^2][^3] The sources cited here do not show that every star forms within a cluster.

A protostar becomes a main-sequence star once hydrogen fusion begins in its nucleus, which NASA ties to the pressure and temperature of the core, and a textbook chapter takes the main sequence to be the stage in which a star burns hydrogen in its nucleus.[^1][^4] The intermediate stages between collapse and the main sequence, and the factors that set the masses of the resulting stars, are not established by these sources.
