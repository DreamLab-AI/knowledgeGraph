Space medicine is the research and clinical practice directed at protecting the health and performance of people who travel in space.[^1][^2]

NASA's Human Research Program states its aim as developing methods to protect astronaut health and performance, using ground facilities, the International Space Station and analogue environments. It also runs a surveillance programme that uses medical monitoring data on astronaut health, although the programme page does not describe its protocols.[^1]

Its problems follow from the conditions of flight. ESA describes bones weakening, muscles shrinking and the heart, blood vessels and immune system changing in microgravity, and notes that these changes can affect astronaut health and mission success. NASA lists a similar set and also treats radiation, isolation and confinement as hazards.[^3][^4]

Agencies also present this work as useful on Earth, and NASA's biological and physical sciences programme says the study of space stressors benefits life on Earth. These are statements of programme purpose and the sources do not establish particular clinical outcomes.[^5][^3]

Space medicine is related to, but not the same as, space physiology. The sources reviewed give no formal definition of either discipline, so this description is drawn from how the agencies describe their programmes.[^1][^2]
