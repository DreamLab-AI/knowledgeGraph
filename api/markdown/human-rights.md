- ### Content

  ## Definition

  Fundamental rights and freedoms inherent to all human beings as recognised in international instruments including the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, which AI systems must respect and protect throughout their lifecycle. Human rights form a foundational component of the OECD's human-centred values principle, establishing that AI development and deployment must occur within the framework of internationally recognised fundamental rights.

  ## Key Rights Relevant to AI

  ### Civil and Political Rights
  - **Right to privacy**: Protection of personal data and private life
  - **Freedom of expression**: Unrestricted communication and information access
  - **Freedom of assembly**: Collective action and organisation
  - **Right to fair trial**: Due process in AI-influenced legal proceedings
  - **Non-discrimination**: Equal treatment regardless of protected characteristics

  ### Economic and Social Rights
  - **Right to work**: Employment free from arbitrary AI-based exclusion
  - **Right to education**: Equitable access to educational opportunities
  - **Right to health**: Healthcare accessibility without algorithmic discrimination
  - **Right to social security**: Fair access to essential services

  ### Emerging Digital Rights
  - **Right to human determination**: Meaningful human involvement in consequential decisions
  - **Right to explanation**: Understanding AI-influenced outcomes
  - **Right to contest**: Challenging automated decisions
  - **Right to digital dignity**: Respect for personhood in digital contexts
  - **Right to algorithmic transparency**: Understanding how AI systems make decisions affecting individuals

  ## Relationship to AI Ethics

  Human rights provide the normative foundation for [[AI Ethics]], specifying which values AI systems must uphold as non-negotiable obligations rather than aspirational goals. Where AI Ethics addresses broad value alignment, Human Rights identifies specific legally codified entitlements that constrain system design, training data collection, deployment contexts, and post-deployment auditing.

  Key intersections:
  - **Fairness**: Non-discrimination rights operationalise fairness requirements in model outputs
  - **Transparency**: Right to explanation underpins explainability mandates (EU AI Act Article 13)
  - **Accountability**: Right to contest requires auditability and meaningful human oversight
  - **Autonomy**: Right to human determination restricts fully automated decision-making in high-stakes contexts

  ## Relationship to Blockchain Governance

  [[Blockchain Technology]] provides mechanisms to operationalise human rights protections in digital contexts:

  - **Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI)**: Decentralised identity systems (ID2020, Civic, WFP Building Blocks) give individuals control over their digital identities, enabling stateless individuals to access services
  - **Financial Inclusion**: DeFi platforms and blockchain remittances provide borderless financial services for unbanked populations, fulfilling economic rights
  - **Censorship Resistance**: Decentralised publishing (Mirror, Arweave) protects journalists and activists exercising freedom of expression
  - **Zero-Knowledge Proofs**: Allow identity verification without exposing sensitive data, protecting privacy rights

  Challenges include regulatory compliance conflicts with privacy rights, and the digital divide limiting access for vulnerable populations.

  ## Relationship to Digital Rights

  [[Digital Rights]] extends human rights frameworks into digital environments:

  - **Bot proliferation and inauthentic content**: AI-generated content and automated accounts undermine freedom of expression and access to genuine information (51% of global internet traffic is bots as of 2025)
  - **Surveillance technologies**: AI-powered surveillance systems (facial recognition, behavioural monitoring) directly implicate privacy rights; EU AI Act prohibits real-time biometric identification in public spaces
  - **Algorithmic manipulation**: Personalisation systems that manipulate user behaviour infringe digital autonomy
  - **Data portability**: Rights over personal data enable individuals to control their digital footprints

  ## Rights-Based Impact Assessment

  Systematic evaluation required for AI systems affecting fundamental rights:

  1. **Affected rights**: Which fundamental rights the AI system may impact
  2. **Affected persons**: Individuals and groups whose rights are at stake
  3. **Nature of impact**: Positive, negative, direct, indirect effects
  4. **Severity**: Magnitude and reversibility of rights impacts
  5. **Mitigation**: Measures to prevent or minimise rights infringements
  6. **Safeguards**: Procedural protections ensuring rights respect

  ## EU AI Act Integration

  Human rights protection is central to:
  - **Prohibited practices** (Article 5): Preventing manipulation, exploitation, social scoring
  - **High-risk classification** (Annex III): Systems affecting fundamental rights receive enhanced scrutiny
  - **Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment** (Article 27): Mandatory for public authority deployers
  - **Transparency obligations**: Enabling rights exercise and contestation
  - **Ban on real-time biometric surveillance**: Untargeted scraping of internet/CCTV for facial recognition databases prohibited (effective August 2024)

  ## Council of Europe AI Treaty (2024)

  The Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (signed by UK September 2024):
  - First legally binding international treaty on AI and human rights
  - Requires signatories to assess need for bans or moratoria on AI incompatible with human rights
  - Mandates iterative risk and impact assessments
  - Emphasises transparency, accountability, and democratic oversight

  ## Challenges

  - **Balancing**: Managing tensions between competing rights (privacy vs security)
  - **Context sensitivity**: Rights frameworks varying across jurisdictions
  - **Technical translation**: Implementing abstract rights in technical systems
  - **Enforcement**: Ensuring practical redress for rights violations
  - **Emerging issues**: Addressing AI-specific rights challenges not contemplated by historical frameworks
  - **Power asymmetries**: Addressing concentration of AI development in few corporations
