A surface radiation environment is the natural radiation environment on or near the surface of a planet or moon, made up of free-space radiation as partly shielded and modulated by the body, together with radiation from naturally radioactive elements in its crust.[^1]

Ontology definitions cover both ionising and non-ionising radiation, such as UVA, UVB and visible light, although the term is often used for biologically damaging ionising radiation such as galactic cosmic radiation and solar particles.[^1] In these definitions, the planet or moon's crust, atmosphere and magnetic field, where present, are named as factors that modify the free-space environment.[^1]

Mars gives a measured example. The Radiation Assessment Detector on the Curiosity rover reported absorbed dose and dose equivalent from galactic cosmic rays and solar energetic particles at the Martian surface over about 300 days during a solar maximum.[^2] A later analysis of the first Martian year of RAD data describes daily and seasonal changes in atmospheric pressure and solar modulation of the galactic cosmic ray flux as influences on the surface dose.[^3]

These results are specific to Mars, to the instrument and to the observation periods. The sources retained here do not give surface dose values for the Moon or other bodies, and the ontology entry is a vocabulary definition rather than a measurement. A surface dose should not be read as a crew mission dose.
