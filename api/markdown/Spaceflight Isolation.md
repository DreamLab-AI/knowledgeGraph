Spaceflight isolation is the condition of crews who are isolated and confined in a small space far from Earth over a long period, together with the behavioural responses that NASA studies as a spaceflight hazard.[^1]

NASA states that exposure to the isolation and confinement of spaceflight can result in decrements in cognitive and behavioural functioning, and that validated countermeasures for exploration-class missions had not yet been developed when the page was written.[^2] That description covers a risk and a gap in countermeasures, but does not give how often or how severely such decrements would occur on exploration missions.[^2]

That evidence report records that no behavioural emergencies had occurred before or during any US spaceflight up to its date, and it names lack of privacy, which analogue studies have associated with impaired well-being, as a major psychosocial stressor.[^3] Such statements reflect the missions and period the report covers, but do not establish the position for longer exploration missions. The report also separates adverse behavioural conditions from a mental disorder, which it describes as a possible outcome if such conditions go undetected or unmitigated.[^3]

Ground habitats are used to study the same conditions. In NASA's HERA habitat, crews of volunteers live inside for 45 days at a time, helping NASA study isolation, confinement and remote conditions in exploration scenarios.[^1] No quantification of how far analogue results transfer to flight is given in these sources.[^1][^3]
