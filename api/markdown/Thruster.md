A thruster is a device that produces thrust on a spacecraft, and the NASA small-spacecraft survey treats it as one component of a complete propulsion system alongside feed systems, pressurisation, propellant management and power processing units.[^1]

NASA Glenn explains that thrust is generated by a rocket engine through the reaction of accelerating a mass of gas, with the gas accelerated to the rear and the vehicle accelerated in the opposite direction.[^2] For engines that carry their own propellant, the page gives thrust as the exhaust mass flow times the exit velocity, plus a term for the pressure difference across the nozzle exit.[^3]

Several classes are covered in the survey: chemical thrusters such as hydrazine, solid motors, and electric propulsion types including electrothermal, electrospray, gridded ion, Hall-effect, pulsed plasma and vacuum arc, and ambipolar. Distinct thrust and specific impulse ranges are given for each class.[^1]

This survey scope statement is not a formal definition of the word, and it addresses small spacecraft only.[^1] The sources do not establish a single typical thrust level for thrusters in general.
