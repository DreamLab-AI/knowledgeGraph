
A space-to-space link is a communications hop between two space-borne endpoints.

Also called a crosslink, it can connect a spacecraft to a relay satellite, another satellite or a nearby peer in a proximity network. The link may use RF or optical communication. “Crosslink” does not imply that endpoints share an orbit, constellation, operator or protocol.[^1][^3][^5]

Direction is defined between the two space endpoints or by a mission’s network roles. A crosslink is not an Earth-to-space uplink or space-to-Earth downlink simply because traffic ultimately travels from or to Earth. CCSDS 401 uses the historical terms “forward” and “return” for space-to-space directions; these depend on the link convention and should be defined where they matter.[^2]

For nearby mission links, CCSDS Proximity-1 specifies a bidirectional radio protocol for communication among spacecraft, probes, landers, rovers and relays. The approved Issue 6 data-link standard opened here is dated July 2020. A later Issue 6.2 item is labelled a draft review document; the end of its review period does not make it an approved standard.[^3][^4]

Optical inter-satellite links can provide relay paths with high data rates, but terminals must acquire, point to and track one another. Performance and availability therefore depend on terminal capability and geometry; a demonstration does not set a service level for every constellation. NASA’s ILLUMA-T/LCRD account documents a two-way optical relay demonstration and delay/disruption-tolerant networking experiments. A relayed route still comprises distinct hops, each dependent on its own coverage, scheduling, protocol support and ground access.[^1][^5]
