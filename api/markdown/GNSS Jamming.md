GNSS jamming is intentional radio frequency interference that prevents a GNSS receiver from locking onto satellite signals, leaving its position and time service degraded or unusable.[^1][^2]

GNSS signals arrive at the receiver with very low power, so a transmitter on the same frequencies can overwhelm them. One research paper describes jamming as the transmission of a high-powered signal towards the target device to disrupt its communication with the satellites, and separates it from unintentional interference such as that from communication or television transmitters.[^2]

Jamming differs from spoofing. Under jamming the receiver typically loses or degrades its solution, whereas spoofing supplies counterfeit signals that can lead to a plausible but wrong one. The same paper reports that in jamming both the automatic gain control and carrier-to-noise indicators fall, while in the spoofing cases it discusses the carrier-to-noise value can stay relatively constant. That is a finding of the cited research, not a general detection rule.[^2]

EASA describes interference as able to occur in any phase of flight, leading to re-routing or diversions. Its recommendations for suspected jamming include verifying aircraft position by non-GNSS means and reporting irregularities. The bulletin behind that page was in its fourth revision when retrieved, and neither source gives a general figure for the range or power at which a jammer defeats a given receiver.[^1]
