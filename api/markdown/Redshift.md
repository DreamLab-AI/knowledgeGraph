Redshift is the apparent shift toward longer wavelengths of light or spectral lines emitted by an object.[^1]

NASA’s glossary separates three forms: Doppler redshift from the motion of sources away from the observer, gravitational redshift as light escapes massive objects, and cosmological redshift from the stretching of space in the expanding universe. The cosmological form is often symbolised as z.[^1] A NASA explainer describes the Doppler case as a star moving away so that its light waves look stretched out and its spectral lines move to longer wavelengths.[^2]

Astronomers measure redshift by comparing the positions of lines in an object’s spectrum with their wavelengths in the laboratory. ESA notes that atomic emission and absorption lines occur at well-known wavelengths.[^3] The NASA/IPAC Extragalactic Database describes the measurement as finding the shift of spectral features of known wavelength and frequency, using individual lines or comparison with template spectra.[^4]

ESA stresses that the shifts seen in distant galaxies are expansion redshifts, not Doppler shifts arising from motion through space.[^3] The database says the two parts generally cannot be separated except for nearby galaxies and members of galaxy clusters, so quoting a single recession velocity from a redshift needs a stated model.[^4] None of the sources used here gives a catalogue of redshift values, and the three forms should not be treated as one effect.[^1][^3][^4]
