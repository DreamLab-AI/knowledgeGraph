A coronal mass ejection, or CME, is an eruption of magnetised plasma from the Sun's corona into interplanetary space. A CME is physically distinct from a solar flare: a flare is a burst of electromagnetic radiation, whereas an ejection is material and embedded magnetic field moving through the solar wind. The two can occur together.[^1]

## Formation and propagation

CMEs are associated with stressed solar magnetic fields, flux-rope structures and magnetic reconnection, although eruption initiation remains an active research subject. Coronagraphs block the bright solar disc so that analysts can estimate an ejection's size, density, speed and direction. Views close to the Sun–Earth line suffer projection ambiguity, which is one reason that side-on observations from the Sun–Earth L5 region are being developed.[^2]

An Earth-directed CME may arrive in roughly 15 hours to several days. This broad range is not a guaranteed warning period: flare radiation and energetic particles can arrive earlier, while the ejection's trajectory and speed can change as it moves through the solar wind.[^1]

## Geomagnetic effects and uncertainty

Impact depends strongly on the orientation of the magnetic field carried by the CME relative to Earth's field. Favourable magnetic coupling transfers energy into the magnetosphere and can drive geomagnetic storms, aurora and currents that disturb spacecraft and ground infrastructure. Operationally, the decisive field orientation is generally unavailable until an upstream monitor samples the approaching structure, leaving much less notice than remote imaging alone might suggest.[^3]

Forecast centres use several model runs to express uncertainty in arrival time and whether an ejection will strike Earth. An evaluation of the Met Office's 24-member operational ensemble found false alarms, arrival errors exceeding a day in some cases, and ensemble spread that did not reliably represent the actual error.[^4] These results describe one evaluated system and event sample; they are evidence for cautious, probabilistic forecasts rather than a universal accuracy figure.
