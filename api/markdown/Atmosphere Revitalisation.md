Atmosphere revitalisation is the set of life support functions that keep the air of a crewed spacecraft breathable, including removing carbon dioxide, recovering and generating oxygen, controlling humidity, removing trace contaminants and filtering particulates.[^1]

On the International Space Station, NASA describes the Air Revitalization System as cleaning the circulating cabin air by removing trace contaminants from electronics, plastics and human off-gassing, together with the carbon dioxide the crew exhales. Carbon dioxide is captured with molecular sieves, materials that separate gases by size.[^2][^3]

Oxygen for the crew comes from an Oxygen Generation System that electrolyses water. Hydrogen from that process can be vented or combined with exhaled carbon dioxide in a Sabatier reactor, which yields methane and water. NASA reports that this reactor recovered only part of the oxygen from carbon dioxide when it was operational, because the available hydrogen was limited.[^2][^1]

These descriptions cover the US segment of the station and one agency's hardware. ESA's MELiSSA programme studies a different, biological approach in which microbiological cells, algae and plants recycle air, water and waste in a closed loop. The cited ESA text presents it as a research concept and does not establish that it is an operational flight system.[^4]
