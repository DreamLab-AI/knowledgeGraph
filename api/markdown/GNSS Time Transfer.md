GNSS time transfer is the use of GNSS satellite signals to synchronise a local clock or to compare clocks at separate sites.[^1]

In one-way mode a timing receiver uses GNSS signals in real time to synchronise a local clock to GPS time or a realisation of UTC, or to calibrate a frequency source. Such receivers normally store one surveyed position rather than computing moving fixes, because the antenna does not move.[^1]

In common-view mode two stations observe the same satellite at the same time, each comparing the received signal with its own clock. The stations exchange data and subtract, so the satellite clock drops out and much, though not all, of the path delay cancels. NIST notes that this works best when the delay fluctuations on the two paths are highly correlated, and that exact cancellation cannot be achieved in practice. Common view needs a means of exchanging data between the stations and requires the same satellite to be observed at the same time.[^1][^2]

Navipedia also describes an all-in-view, or melting-pot, mode that does not need simultaneous observations and suits unattended systems, and a carrier-phase mode that adds precision but carries an ambiguity that pseudorange information resolves. It reports that common-view GPS data have long fed the international time scales computed at the BIPM. The sources opened here do not state current link performance, so no accuracy figure is given.[^1]
