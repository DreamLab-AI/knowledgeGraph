A geostationary orbit is a circular, prograde orbit in Earth's equatorial plane with an orbital period equal to Earth's sidereal rotation. A spacecraft in this ideal orbit remains above one longitude and appears stationary to an observer fixed on Earth.[^1] These conditions make geostationary orbit a strict subset of geosynchronous orbit.

## Geostationary and geosynchronous

A **geosynchronous** orbit requires the matching sidereal period but may be inclined or eccentric. Such a spacecraft returns to the same relative geometry once per sidereal day yet can trace a north-south or figure-of-eight path in the sky. A **geostationary** orbit additionally requires zero eccentricity, zero inclination and motion in the direction of Earth's rotation.[^1] The terms should not be used as synonyms.

Nominal geostationary altitude is about 35,786 km above the equator. Its period is 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds, a sidereal day rather than 24 civil hours.[^2] The quoted altitude is rounded and depends on the Earth-radius convention. The dynamically central quantity is the orbital radius or semi-major axis that gives the sidereal period.

## Station-keeping and operational regions

Exact geostationary geometry is an idealisation. Lunar and solar gravity, Earth's non-spherical gravity field and solar-radiation pressure drive longitude, eccentricity and inclination away from their targets. Operators use station-keeping manoeuvres to remain within allocated longitude and latitude tolerances, consuming propellant and creating manoeuvre-estimation uncertainty.[^3] An operational control box is a tolerance around the ideal; it does not alter the geostationary definition.

Statistical and debris-mitigation documents use wider regions. ESA's 2026 environment report defines a broad GEO analysis bin using perigee, apogee and inclination bounds, and separately identifies the IADC-protected geostationary region.[^4] These are declared operational classifications. Some objects inside them are inclined, eccentric, drifting or inactive and are not mathematically geostationary.

## Uses, limitations and provenance

Apparent fixed position lets a ground antenna point continuously towards one spacecraft. It also supports continuous regional meteorological imaging and broad-area communications.[^2] The long propagation path affects latency and link loss, while equatorial geometry gives poor elevation towards high latitudes and the poles. Suitability therefore depends on coverage, elevation, spectrum, link budget and resilience as well as orbit class.

A geostationary-orbit record should include longitude target or drift state, semi-major axis, eccentricity, inclination, frame, epoch and time system, station-keeping status and uncertainty. Catalogue membership in a GEO bin should preserve the bin's authority and numerical criteria rather than being converted automatically into the strict class.
