Tachocline is the name for the thin rotational shear layer near the base of the Sun's convection zone, where the differential rotation of the convective envelope gives way to the nearly uniform rotation of the radiative interior.[^1][^2]

Helioseismology, which infers the Sun's interior rotation from the oscillations seen at its surface, shows two rotational shear layers: one near the surface and the tachocline near the base of the convection zone.[^1] Work on the layer's properties reports that its jump in rotation rate, its position and its width have all been examined over several solar cycles, and that the change in rotation rate across it does not follow the activity cycle in a simple way.[^2]

These sources describe the layer as thin but fix no single thickness, so this page states no thickness value.[^1][^2] Everything above refers to the Sun. The sources opened do not establish that stars in general have a tachocline of the same character.[^1][^2]

SWEET, an ontology of Earth and space science terms, records the class under the spelling Tacocline as a subclass of Stellar Interior, with a label but no definition.[^3] Tachocline is the conventional spelling, and whether the SWEET spelling is deliberate is not established by the file.[^3]
