A tabular iceberg is an iceberg with a flat upper surface that has detached from an ice shelf, ice tongue or floating tidewater glacier.[^1][^2]

SWEET gives the two defining conditions as a flat upper surface and derivation from an ice shelf, ice tongue or floating tidewater glacier by detachment.[^1] Australian Antarctic Program material describes a tabular berg as a flat-topped iceberg and says most form by calving from an ice shelf and show horizontal banding.[^2] SwissEduc adds that such bergs are typically several kilometres long.[^3]

Tabular is one shape category among several, since the Australian source lists dome-shaped, sloping, pinnacled, dry-docked and blocky forms alongside it.[^2] SWEET’s comment records that newly formed tabular icebergs have nearly vertical sides and flat tops.[^1] That wording ties that profile to newly formed bergs, and the retained sources say nothing about how it changes as a berg ages.

Size appears in the retained sources only as typical or regional description, and they set no numerical threshold separating a tabular iceberg from other flat-topped ice. They also describe the berg from surface observation, not from any particular satellite sensor.
