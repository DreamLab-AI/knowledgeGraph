Stellar evolution is the sequence of changes in a star's properties over its life, from formation in a collapsing clump of gas through a long phase of core fusion to an end state that depends on the star's mass.[^1][^2]

NASA describes each star as having a life cycle, with properties that change as it ages. A forming star first draws energy from heat released by its collapse, and a star that has reached the main sequence fuses hydrogen into helium in its core.[^1] A star in this phase is stable because pressure from its hot interior balances the pull of gravity, a state called hydrostatic equilibrium.[^2]

When the core runs out of hydrogen, the balance shifts and the star leaves the main sequence. A star like the Sun is described as ending as a white dwarf, whereas a massive star can explode as a supernova and leave a neutron star or a black hole.[^1][^3] Material cast out by dying stars enriches the gas from which later generations of stars form.[^1][^4]

These pages are educational overviews. They do not give a single mass boundary between the outcomes or a single timescale, so none is stated here. Stellar evolution also differs from the evolution of galaxies or of the Universe, which the sources do not cover.
