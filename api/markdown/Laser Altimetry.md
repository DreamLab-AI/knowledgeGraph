Laser altimetry is the measurement of surface height by sending pulses of laser light to a surface and timing the photons that return.[^1][^2]

NASA describes the ATLAS instrument on ICESat-2 as carrying out three tasks: sending laser pulses to the ground, collecting the returning photons and recording their travel time. ICESat-2 uses six laser beams, which gives denser coverage than the single beam of the original ICESat. NOAA's general account of active remote sensing uses the same idea, in which a laser is projected at the surface and the time taken to return to the sensor is measured.[^1][^2][^3]

ATLAS is sensitive enough to detect individual photons, and NASA states that this lets it detect both the forest floor and the tops of canopies in all but the densest woods and jungles. Its primary intent is cryospheric elevation, with forest cover, vegetation, coral reefs and ocean waves described as further targets. The sources do not set out how travel times are converted into calibrated heights or what uncertainty results.[^1][^2]

Cloud limits the measurement. NASA says the beams can pass through some wispy clouds while other clouds block them and reflect light back to the satellite. The pages cited here describe one mission, so they do not establish that every laser altimeter shares its beam count, sampling or behaviour. Laser altimetry is also not the same as radar altimetry, which these sources do not cover.[^2]
