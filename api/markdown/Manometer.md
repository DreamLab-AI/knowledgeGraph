A manometer is a device that measures fluid pressure by means of a column of liquid.[^1]

A 1965 NASA Tech Brief on high-pressure measurement describes the difficulty with conventional manometers: for high pressures an inordinately long column of liquid and tube would be required, and liquid could blow out of the open end during pressure surges. The brief proposes a system of short-length U-tube manometers used with a reference pressure that is divided into proportional parts, so that the heights of liquid in the separate U-tubes are added to determine the reference pressure.[^1]

That document is an engineering summary of one design, not a general definition. It does not set out the working fluids, the units, accuracy, or how manometers are calibrated or used with weather or ocean instruments.[^1]

A manometer is an in situ instrument. It belongs in an Earth-observation vocabulary because pressure is measured on site, while remote sensing, as NOAA describes it, obtains information about objects or areas from a distance.[^2] The sources do not discuss manometers in Earth observation specifically, so that placement is a classification rather than a documented use.
