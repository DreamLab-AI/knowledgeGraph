Stellar nucleosynthesis is the formation of heavier atomic nuclei from lighter ones by nuclear fusion inside stars and in stellar explosions.[^1][^2]

NASA describes stars fusing hydrogen nuclei into helium for most of their lives, and says that in massive stars later stages convert carbon into heavier elements such as oxygen, neon and magnesium.[^1] Chandra material describes supernova explosions as fusing lighter elements into heavier ones and as the main means of seeding the galaxy with elements such as carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, silicon and iron.[^2]

Core-collapse and thermonuclear supernovae are described as leaving different element patterns, with core-collapse events richer in oxygen and thermonuclear events producing relatively more iron.[^2] The gas between the stars is described as hydrogen and helium from the Big Bang, enriched with heavier elements produced by fusion in earlier stellar generations.[^3]

These sources are explanatory and do not give reaction networks or yields. Nucleosynthesis in the Big Bang is a separate process that the retained sources mention only as the origin of the hydrogen and helium.[^3]

[^2]: Chandra X-ray Center, “Field Guide to X-ray Sources: Supernovas & Supernova Remnants”. https://chandra.harvard.edu/xray_sources/supernovas.html
[^3]: Chandra X-ray Center, “Stellar Evolution: Cycles of Formation and Destruction”. https://chandra.harvard.edu/edu/formal/stellar_ev/story/index.html
