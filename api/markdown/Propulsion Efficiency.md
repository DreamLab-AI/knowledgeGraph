Propulsion efficiency is a measure of how much thrust a propulsion system obtains from its propellant, expressed for rockets as specific impulse, the equivalent exhaust velocity divided by standard gravitational acceleration, in seconds.[^1]

NASA Glenn describes the engine with the higher specific impulse as the more efficient, because it produces more thrust for the same amount of propellant, and notes that the units are the same in English and metric systems. The page also uses specific impulse to size an engine in preliminary analysis.[^1]

The NASA small-spacecraft survey quotes values by device class rather than as one figure: hydrazine thrusters typically achieve a specific impulse of 200 to 235 seconds for 1-N class or larger thrusters, while pulsed plasma and vacuum arc thrusters are listed at 87 to 3,200 seconds with thrust of 4 to 500 micronewtons.[^2] These are survey ranges for named device classes and not guarantees for a particular product.

Specific impulse measures propellant use and is not an energy-conversion efficiency. A 2002 paper on pulsed plasma thrusters treats thrust efficiency separately and describes these devices as among the least efficient electric thrusters, at about ten per cent.[^3] That figure is dated and the sources do not establish a single comparison across thruster technologies.
