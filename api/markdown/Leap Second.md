A leap second is a second added to Coordinated Universal Time to keep it synchronised with astronomical time.[^1]

UTC is an atomic time scale, whereas astronomical time, UT1, follows the irregular rotation of Earth. NIST states that leap seconds keep the difference between UTC and UT1 to less than 0.9 seconds, and NASA describes the adjustment as the addition or subtraction of leap seconds as necessary. Opened sources do not establish whether any subtraction has occurred.[^1][^2]

IERS Bulletin C, issued every six months, announces leap seconds or confirms that none will occur at the next possible date, which is the end of December or June. Bulletin C 72 of 6 July 2026 states that no leap second will be introduced at the end of December 2026. Later bulletins supersede this status.[^3][^4]

Accumulated leap seconds give the difference between UTC and International Atomic Time. NAIF documents a leap second as an extra UTC name, 23:59:60, at the end of a month, which software must handle explicitly. NASA notes that UTC is used by astronomers, navigators and the Deep Space Network.[^5][^4][^2]
