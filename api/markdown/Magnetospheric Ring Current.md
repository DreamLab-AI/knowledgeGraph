A population of medium-energy charged particles drifts around Earth to form the magnetospheric ring current, with protons drifting in one direction and electrons in the opposite direction.[^1] Because the two species drift oppositely, the population forms a ring of current around the planet.

NOAA describes a ring of westward current in space that produces magnetic disturbances on the ground, and notes that a measure of this current, the disturbance storm time index, has historically been used to characterise the size of a geomagnetic storm.[^2] NASA places the ring current among three populations of charged particles trapped in the inner magnetosphere, alongside the plasmasphere and the Van Allen radiation belts, and separates them by particle energy.[^1] NOAA likewise lists the ring current, the plasmasphere and the radiation belts as regions created by the interaction between the solar wind and Earth’s magnetic field.[^3]

This region should not be confused with the radiation belts or with the cold plasmasphere. The sources cited here give qualitative descriptions, not its strength, location or evolution during any particular storm, and NOAA does not say the disturbance storm time index is the only storm measure in use.[^1][^2][^3]
