An ice giant is a giant planet, of which Uranus and Neptune are the solar system examples, that is mostly composed of hydrogen and helium but also contains water, ammonia and methane.[^1][^2]

NASA explains that these three chemicals are typically found frozen as ices in the cold outer solar system, which is the origin of the name. It adds that the interiors of Uranus and Neptune are warm enough that the so-called ices inside them are not frozen, so the term describes composition rather than physical state.[^1]

NASA’s overview groups the outer giant planets as bodies without hard surfaces, with swirling gases above a core, and separates Jupiter and Saturn as gas giants from Uranus and Neptune as ice giants. Among exoplanets, NASA reports an ice giant found in 2014 about 25,000 light-years away, in an orbit similar to that of Uranus around the Sun, whose composition and atmosphere are poorly known. It also reports water vapour in the atmosphere of HAT-P-11b, a Neptune-size planet described as gaseous with a rocky core.[^1][^2]

Supporting sources give no mass range, ice fraction or interior structure for the class, and both come from one publisher. Exoplanet use of the term, and its overlap with Neptune-like planets, is therefore only partly supported here.[^1][^2]

[^2]: NASA Science, “About the Planets”. https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planets/
