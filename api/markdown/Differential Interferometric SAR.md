Differential interferometric SAR is a technique that compares the phase of radar images of the same area acquired at different times to measure small changes in the position of the ground surface.[^1][^2]

Repeat observations from the same position form the basis of the technique. If a point on the ground moves between two passes, the phase difference is proportional to the movement along the radar line of sight, and the fringes in the resulting interferogram step in units tied to the radar wavelength.[^2] An ESA interview from 2002 describes the same principle for Envisat ASAR and gives centimetre-scale surface deformation as the measurable scale for that instrument.[^1]

In practice the phase also carries contributions from the satellite's orbital geometry and from surface topography, and these have to be modelled and removed before the remaining signal can be read as deformation. The NISAR page describes this for its own L-band data and states a sensitivity to large-scale deformation rates of a few millimetres per year from repeat-pass interferograms.[^2] That figure belongs to the mission and its observing conditions, not to the technique in general.

Both sources name hazards and ground-subsidence monitoring among the uses, including earthquakes, volcanoes and landslides.[^1][^2] Neither source establishes how coherence loss, atmospheric delay or vegetation limit the technique, so those limits are not described here.
