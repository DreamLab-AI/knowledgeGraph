Satellite remote sensing is remote sensing carried out from a satellite, in which a sensor outside the atmosphere acquires information about the Earth's surface without contact by recording reflected or emitted energy.[^1][^2]

Because of their orbits, satellites permit repetitive coverage of the surface on a continuing basis.[^2] Orbits are selected to suit the sensor: geostationary satellites at roughly constant position over the Earth view the same region continuously, while near-polar, sun-synchronous orbits cover each area at a constant local sun time and give consistent illumination for comparing images. Swath is the area imaged along the track, and the tutorial says it generally varies between tens and hundreds of kilometres for spaceborne sensors.[^3]

Satellite sensors may be passive, recording energy emitted or reflected by the target, or active. Active microwave sensors provide their own illumination, and the tutorial notes that radar can penetrate cloud cover and most weather conditions.[^4] Spaceborne imaging radar points its antenna away from nadir at an oblique look angle and illuminates a continuous swath as it moves.[^5]

Mission documents give swath, revisit and radiometric characteristics for their own instruments, for example the Landsat 9 quick facts, and these differ between systems rather than defining the field.[^6] Satellite sensors are restricted to the areas and times their orbits allow, and the tutorial notes that a fault in space is much harder to correct than one on an aircraft.[^3]
