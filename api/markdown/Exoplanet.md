An exoplanet is a planet outside the Solar System. It may orbit a star, orbit more than one star, or travel through space without a host star. The term describes the object, not the method used to detect it.[^1]

## Detection and measurement

Most exoplanets are inferred from their effect on light or motion. The transit method measures periodic dimming as a planet crosses its star, while radial-velocity observations measure the star's line-of-sight motion caused by an orbiting companion. Microlensing detects the temporary magnification produced by gravity, and direct imaging separates planetary light from the much brighter host star.[^2]

Transit depth can constrain a planet's radius relative to its star. Combining transit timing, radial velocity and stellar models can constrain orbit and mass, but every derived property depends on measurement uncertainty and model assumptions. Detection methods also have different selection effects: transits favour aligned orbits and shorter periods, while direct imaging often favours young, hot planets far from their stars.

## Atmospheric characterisation

Transmission spectroscopy compares starlight filtered through a planet's atmosphere during transit with light measured outside transit. Wavelength-dependent changes can support inferences about gases, clouds and haze. Retrieval software combines radiative transfer, parametrised atmosphere models and statistical algorithms to estimate ranges of atmospheric properties.[^3]

These results are posterior inferences rather than direct chemical photographs. Instrument systematics, stellar activity, overlapping molecular features and model choices can change the interpretation. Published spectra should distinguish measured points from a fitted atmospheric model and report uncertainties.[^4] Detecting a molecule does not, by itself, establish biological activity.

## UK capability

UCL groups develop atmospheric spectroscopy, radiative-transfer models and retrieval methods. The UK also leads major parts of ESA's planned Ariel atmospheric-survey mission, including science leadership, payload-consortium work, cooling, optical support and data processing.[^5] Public ESA pages retrieved for this entry gave conflicting launch years, so the durable record describes Ariel as planned without assigning a date.
