Radiometric resolution is the ability of an imaging sensor to discriminate very slight differences in the energy it records, so that a finer radiometric resolution means greater sensitivity to small differences in reflected or emitted energy.[^1]

Image data are recorded as positive digital numbers that run from zero to one less than a chosen power of two, which corresponds to the number of bits used for binary coding. More bits therefore allow more brightness levels, and the tutorial shows this by displaying one scene in grey tones at two coding depths. Black represents a digital number of zero and white the maximum value.[^1]

Missions often state radiometric resolution as a bit depth, and the NASA Landsat 9 page lists it in that form.[^2] A bit depth describes how finely the recorded signal is divided. None of the sources here establishes the calibration accuracy or noise level of any instrument, and a larger bit depth should not be read as a measure of either.

Radiometric resolution is separate from spatial resolution, which concerns the size of the smallest detectable feature, and from pixel size. Sensor design involves a trade-off, as the tutorial describes: a small instantaneous field of view gives high spatial resolution but reduces the energy detected, and widening the wavelength range to recover that energy reduces spectral resolution.[^1][^3]
