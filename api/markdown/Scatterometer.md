A scatterometer is a generally non-imaging active microwave sensor used to make precise quantitative measurements of the amount of energy backscattered from targets.[^1]

Backscattered energy depends on the roughness of the surface and on the angle at which the microwave energy strikes it. Scatterometry over the ocean can be used to estimate wind speed from sea surface roughness.[^1] The European Space Agency describes the ERS wind scatterometer in the same terms: backscatter depends on the size of the ocean ripples, which relates to wind stress and hence wind speed, and on their orientation to the transmitted pulse, which relates to wind direction.[^2]

Wind scatterometers aim for accurate wind vector measurements, with spatial resolution of secondary importance. The ERS instrument had a resolution of 50 km with 25 km grid sampling, and because it used microwave wavelengths its measurements did not depend on weather.[^2] NASA describes the SeaWinds scatterometer as providing all-weather ocean surface wind vector measurements over the ice-free global oceans.[^3]

Scatterometers are grouped with altimeters in the tutorial as profiling devices rather than imaging radars, and notes that ground-based scatterometers characterise the backscatter of materials in a way analogous to spectral reflectance curves in the optical range.[^1] The figures above belong to the named instruments and are not general properties of scatterometers.
