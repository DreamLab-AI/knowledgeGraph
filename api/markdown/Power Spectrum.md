A power spectrum describes how the power of a signal, or the variance of a time series, is distributed over frequency.[^1][^2]

Marchal calls the spectral density function the power spectrum, or more simply the spectrum, and describes spectral analysis as the estimation of that function from a given time series.[^1] Aster and Borchers apply the power spectral density to signals that are unbounded in time or continuous, and they observe that a power spectral density computed from finite data is always an estimate.[^2] The simplest estimate is the periodogram, which truncates the data with a rectangular window.[^2]

SWEET declares the class equivalent to its general Spectrum class, so the ontology does not separate the power form from the wider notion of a spectrum.[^3] The retained sources are general time-series and geophysics notes, and they do not establish any remote-sensing procedure for the power spectrum.[^1][^2]

Background noise at seismic stations is the one application the retained sources describe.[^2] They give no numerical example that this page relies on.
