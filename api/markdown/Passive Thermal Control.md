Passive thermal control maintains spacecraft temperatures through material properties, geometry and unpowered heat transport. It uses no electrical power for the thermal-control function. Coatings, films, multilayer insulation (MLI), radiator surfaces, conductive straps, selected structural interfaces, sunshades, phase-change material and conventional heat pipes are established passive techniques.[^1]

## Radiation, insulation and conduction

Vacuum removes natural convection around a spacecraft. Heat moves through the vehicle mainly by conduction and radiation and leaves for space by radiation.[^1] External surface design therefore uses both solar absorptivity and infrared emissivity. A radiator coating seeks low absorbed solar energy and high infrared emission, while another surface may be deliberately insulating or absorbing.

Optical properties change in service. Atomic oxygen, ultraviolet and particle exposure can alter coatings and films, so beginning-of-life values cannot stand in for an end-of-life hot-case model.[^1] Contamination and handling can also change the result. Analysis should retain the property source, measurement condition, ageing allowance and uncertainty.[^2]

MLI blocks radiative exchange with repeated low-emissivity layers separated to reduce conduction. Compression, seams, penetrations, fasteners and edge paths raise its effective heat leak; trapped gas must be vented.[^1][^2] Small spacecraft can lose proportionally more performance at blanket edges and may lack dispenser clearance. MLI is consequently a manufactured and installed assembly, not a material value applied uniformly to an area.

Conductive paths can be encouraged with straps, heat spreaders and interface materials or reduced with isolating mounts. Contact pressure, surface finish, fasteners and gap thickness affect conductance.[^1][^2] These paths control local component temperatures even when the whole-spacecraft energy balance closes.

## Transport, storage and rejection

A heat pipe evaporates working fluid at a hot end, transports vapour to a cooler region, condenses it and returns liquid through a capillary wick. It moves heat without a mechanical pump, commonly towards a radiator.[^1] Capacity depends on fluid, geometry, temperature, wick and acceleration or orientation during ground testing. Freezing, dry-out, leakage or an unsuitable operating range can interrupt transport.

Radiators reject heat according to area, emissivity, view to cold space and temperature. Increasing radiator area or conductive coupling helps the hot case but can over-cool hardware during eclipse or a low-dissipation mode. ESA's MetOp service-module description shows a mixed design: silvered radiator surfaces, MLI over other faces, conductive isolation and heaters for selected equipment and early operations.[^3]

Phase-change material absorbs a finite heat pulse as latent heat, then must release that energy and reset. It shifts a load in time rather than supplying an unlimited sink.[^1] Thermal storage therefore needs a declared phase range, capacity, cycling life and recovery condition.

## Boundaries and status

Some documents place mechanical thermostats or even heaters in a passive hardware list because the switch acts without software. The heater circuit still consumes electrical power and belongs to active thermal control under the powered/unpowered definition.[^1][^2] The switching element and the heat source should be classified separately.

Development status also matters. An Airbus deployable ammonia heat-pipe radiator underwent shaker testing in 2016.[^4] That is evidence of a tested development article, not of launch or flight deployment.

RAL Space currently lists thermal engineering and MLI among its UK capabilities.[^5] For MicroCarb, RAL manufactured some of the spacecraft's MLI blankets and worked with Thales Alenia Space UK on thermal-vacuum testing before the satellite launched on 26 July 2025.[^6] These are bounded contributions to a deployed mission; they do not imply UK ownership of its complete thermal design.
