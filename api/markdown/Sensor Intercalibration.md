Sensor intercalibration is the post-launch comparison and adjustment of one satellite sensor's radiometric response against other sensors or a common reference so that their measurements agree.[^1][^2]

It is a narrower kind of calibration than radiometric calibration in general. NASA's joint optical guidelines list inter-calibration with other satellite sensors among the post-launch methods, beside vicarious calibration to in-situ reference measurements and calibration to simulated radiances from pseudo-invariant calibration sites.[^1] CEOS advises data producers to coordinate cross-calibration of at-sensor products using uniform reference surfaces to minimise overall biases between sensors.[^2] One technique described in the guidelines removes calibration trends among different sensors and allows cross-calibration to a common reference.[^1]

Differences vary with the sensors and surfaces compared. A CEOS presentation puts the radiometric differences between Landsat 8 and Sentinel-2 at approximately 2%, and says responses can differ by as much as 17% for different surface types if SBAF corrections are not applied.[^2] Those figures come from one presentation and are not a general result.

Uncertainty is the main limit. The guidelines note that rigorous uncertainty analysis and traceability are not always available for common post-launch methods.[^1] NASA Earthdata says only that processed data can carry ancillary radiometric and geometric calibration coefficients and does not mention intercalibration.[^3]
