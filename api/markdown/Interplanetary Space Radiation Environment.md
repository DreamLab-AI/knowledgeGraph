Interplanetary space radiation environment is the natural radiation environment of a region of interplanetary space.[^1]

NASA's Space Life Sciences Ontology lists free space radiation environment as a synonym and places the class under the space radiation environment class, which it defines as a radiation environment outside a commonly defined boundary marking the beginning of space.[^1] A National Academies workshop report names three main natural sources for spacecraft and astronauts: galactic cosmic radiation, solar energetic particles, and energetic particles trapped in a planetary magnetic field, the last of which belongs near planets.[^2]

Galactic cosmic rays originate outside the solar system and consist of high-energy protons, alpha particles and heavier nuclei, and their flux is modulated by solar activity.[^3] A National Academies report states that their intensity is at a minimum when solar activity is at a maximum, and vice versa.[^4] Solar energetic particles are mostly protons and electrons, and ESA describes their intensity and occurrence as difficult to predict.[^3] The two components therefore do not vary in step with one another.

NASA states that energetic protons and heavy nuclei outside Earth's protective atmosphere are a hazard to living organisms, and that the biological damage from energetic nuclei differs from terrestrial radiation, which leaves large uncertainties in health risk projection.[^5] These sources do not give dose figures for a particular mission, which depend on shielding, trajectory and solar conditions.
