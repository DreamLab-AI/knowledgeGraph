
A satellite communications beam is a directional transmit or receive coverage region produced by an antenna system.

A beam describes a property of the radiated or received field and its intended coverage; it is not a separate spacecraft or necessarily a dedicated frequency channel. A radiation pattern describes antenna response by direction, whereas a footprint describes the area served on a surface. Their relation depends on antenna geometry and propagation, so a footprint has no universally sharp edge.[^1]

Beams can be broad, shaped, spot, fixed or steerable, and a payload can form several beams for different locations or links. ITU-R Recommendation S.672-4 gives design-objective patterns for specified geostationary satellite applications; its shapes depend on design and operational parameters. ESA’s Hylas ground-segment account notes eight beams for that particular historical system, not a standard beam count.[^1][^4]

Spatially separated beams can enable frequency reuse and increase capacity, subject to interference control, antenna discrimination, coordination and payload constraints. ESA’s MARIS project describes reuse and feeder-link architecture considerations; this is a network design principle, not a guarantee of interference-free or authorised use in any band.[^2]

Electronic beamforming controls phase and amplitude across antenna elements to shape or steer a beam. ESA materials describe a beamformer-chip project, a demonstrated Galileo uplink antenna panel and Q-EPA development. These sources show distinct prototype or development statuses; they do not establish universal performance, simultaneous-beam capacity or commercial availability. Feeder-link beams may serve gateway stations and user-link beams may serve customer or mission terminals, but payload and network designs vary and need not use a transparent bent-pipe architecture.[^3][^5][^6][^4]
