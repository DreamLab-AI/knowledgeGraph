Orbital period is the elapsed time for an orbiting object to complete a specified revolution or return event in a stated reference frame.

For a bound two-body ellipse, Kepler’s relation is T = 2π√(a³/μ), where a is semimajor axis and μ = G(M + m) is the system gravitational parameter for masses M and m. Treating the central body as fixed neglects the orbiting body's mass, an excellent approximation for most spacecraft but not every binary. Current distance from the central body replaces semimajor axis only for a circular orbit. A parabolic or hyperbolic trajectory is an open path and has no repeating orbital period.[^1236][^1237][^1238][^1240][^1242]

“Period” can describe several different return events. Sidereal period measures a return relative to inertial space; anomalistic period measures periapsis to periapsis; draconitic period measures successive crossings of the same node. A ground-track repeat is defined in a rotating body-fixed frame and may take several revolutions. These values separate when the orbit’s node or periapsis precesses. Geosynchronous orbit matches Earth’s rotation period; geostationary orbit adds a circular equatorial orbit and prograde direction.[^1234][^1241][^1250][^1251]

Mean motion n gives T = 2π/n in the fixed Kepler problem. Under perturbations, mean motion may refer to osculating elements at an epoch or an averaged element theory; those are not interchangeable with a measured recurrence time. A period inferred from a fitted state should state the event definition, observation interval, frame, time scale and element convention.[^1239][^1244][^1241]

In low Earth orbit, atmospheric drag removes orbital energy and usually lowers semimajor axis. For circular orbits, that lower radius corresponds to a shorter period and higher speed, even though the spacecraft has lost energy. Atmospheric density varies with solar activity, while spacecraft attitude, area and drag coefficient affect the force, so the period evolves with environmental and vehicle uncertainty. Other perturbations, including oblateness and third-body gravity, can also shift the return events over time.[^1251][^1252]

An ideal period calculated from semimajor axis is a model value; an operational estimate depends on the dated state, force model and propagation method. Covariance and validity interval help show how far the estimate can be trusted. A two-line element mean motion follows SGP4 conventions. University of Surrey research provides relevant UK evidence that SGP4 is fast and widely used while remaining less precise than high-precision propagation, so its period should be interpreted within its intended model.[^1234][^1241][^1249]
