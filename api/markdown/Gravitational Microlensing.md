Gravitational microlensing is the temporary magnification of light from a distant star when a foreground star passes very close to the line of sight and acts as a lens.[^1]

Lens splitting produces two typically unresolved images, magnified by an amount depending on the angular separation of lens and source; relative motion yields time-variable magnification that defines a microlensing event.[^1]

If the foreground star hosts a planet with projected separation near the image paths, the planet perturbs the images and leaves a short-lived signature. The review describes the technique as sensitive to low-mass planets with separations just beyond the snow line, orbiting stars throughout the Galactic disk and foreground bulge.[^1]

NASA describes the Roman Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey as designed to measure exoplanet demographics by microlensing, complementary to Kepler and TESS, including free floating planets.[^2] That is a design statement for a planned survey; the sources used here do not report its results.

[^2]: NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, “Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey (Roman Space Telescope)”. https://roman.gsfc.nasa.gov/science/Galactic_Bulge_Time_Domain_Survey.html
