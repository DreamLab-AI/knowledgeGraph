A geodetic control point is a marked or continuously operating station whose coordinates have been determined and are used as the known starting position for surveying and mapping.[^1][^2]

Geodesists assign coordinates to points on the Earth, and a set of accurately measured points is the basis of a national spatial reference system that keeps different maps consistent with one another.[^2] Ordnance Survey describes the same role as providing the fundamental points of known coordinates from which cartographers and navigators start.[^1] None of the opened sources gives a formal definition of the exact phrase, so this description follows how they word control marks, control stations and measured points.

Practice has changed with the measuring technique. In Great Britain the OSGB36 network was observed by angle measurement between hilltop primary control stations, which gave its shape better than its scale.[^1] Current OS Net stations are coordinated in three dimensions by GNSS in the ETRS89 terrestrial reference system.[^1] In the United States the NOAA CORS Network uses continuously operating GNSS stations acting as fiducial geodetic control, and its data let users align their positions with the national system.[^3]

Published coordinates of a control point come from many observations averaged under assumptions, so they are approximate and belong to a named datum or reference frame.[^1] The NOAA page says enhanced post-processed accuracies at its stations can approach a few centimetres, but that applies to that network and method, not to control points in general.[^3]
