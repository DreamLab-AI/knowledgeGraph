A suborbital spaceflight vehicle is a craft used or designed to carry out a flight that does not complete an Earth orbit.[^1430][^1437][^1453]

Platform and trajectory are different class dimensions: the vehicle is the craft, while suborbital spaceflight is the mission mode. A craft may be rocket-powered or winged, and a spaceplane design may be intended for either suborbital or orbital flight. An aircraft that carries a launch vehicle is a separate platform, while the payload is the equipment or cargo carried by the launch vehicle.[^1434][^1437][^1453]

The UK Space Industry Act uses a broad capability-based scope. It includes a rocket or other craft capable of operating above the stratosphere and a balloon capable of reaching the stratosphere while carrying crew or passengers. UK regulations also distinguish launch vehicles, carrier aircraft, payloads, crew and spaceflight participants. These legal categories describe regulated activities and do not make wings, a particular propulsion system or passenger carriage necessary engineering features of every suborbital vehicle.[^1430][^1437]

Suborbital vehicles support different mission profiles. A crewed vehicle may carry flight crew or spaceflight participants; an uncrewed vehicle may carry a scientific instrument or technology demonstrator. UK regulations define experimental transport to include vehicles designed or modified for research, experimental or scientific purposes. Commercial purpose, scientific use and crew status should therefore be recorded separately from the vehicle class.[^1437][^1457]

UK launch permission depends on the vehicle and activity. The CAA requires Air Navigation Order permission for a specified large rocket that cannot exceed the stratosphere; a rocket capable of operating above it falls under the Space Industry Act licensing framework. The rules are jurisdiction-specific, so a US FAA recognition threshold or the 100 km Kármán-line convention does not determine a UK vehicle's licence requirement.[^1433][^1434][^1457]
