Solar radiation pressure is the non-gravitational force on a spacecraft produced by sunlight striking its surfaces, which perturbs the orbit.[^1][^2]

ESA Navipedia describes the acceleration as produced by photons impacting the satellite surface, depending mainly on the reflective properties and the area-to-mass ratio of the satellite. It also depends on an eclipse factor, so the acceleration is zero while the satellite is in Earth's shadow.[^1]

An ESA orbit-perturbation paper reports that radiation pressure is the dominant non-gravitational perturbation at high altitude, with direct solar radiation prevailing above roughly 500 km to 600 km, the exact level depending on solar activity. It lists albedo re-radiation from Earth, re-emitted Earth infrared radiation and thermal emission from the spacecraft as further contributions.[^2]

Modelling this force is difficult, and in orbit determination part of its effect is estimated as stochastic acceleration parameters. The sources here give no coefficients or residual error figures, so the page makes no claim about achievable accuracy. Radiation pressure from photons is also a different thing from the solar wind, which SWPC describes as protons and electrons in a plasma.[^1][^3]

[^2]: ESA/ESOC, H. Klinkrad and B. Fritsche, “Orbit and Attitude Perturbations due to Aerodynamics and Radiation Pressure”. https://swe.ssa.esa.int/TECEES/spweather/workshops/proceedings_w1/SESSION1/klinkrad_orbit.pdf
