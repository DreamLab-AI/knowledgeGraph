Celestial mechanics is the study of the motions of celestial bodies under gravity.

A two-body model describes the relative motion of a pair of bodies under their mutual attraction. Its ideal bound trajectories are conic sections, and Keplerian elements provide a compact geometric description at a stated epoch. In the relative problem, the gravitational parameter is mu = G(m1 + m2); when the orbiting body's mass is negligible, a test-particle approximation uses the primary body's parameter alone.[^1399]

A real solar-system trajectory is influenced by other bodies and, where relevant, by non-gravitational forces. Numerical models integrate a specified force model, while orbit solutions are fitted to observations and carry uncertainty. An osculating element set describes the instantaneous conic associated with a state at one epoch; it does not mean that the perturbed trajectory repeats as that conic.[^1404]

A state is incomplete without its origin, coordinate frame, epoch and time scale. The International Celestial Reference System is realised by frames such as ICRF3. JPL's planetary ephemeris work uses ICRF-aligned axes and TDB as its time argument; terrestrial applications require transformations and Earth-orientation information under the relevant conventions.[^1387][^1388][^1404]

Model choice sets the meaning and limits of a prediction. Record the bodies and forces included, adopted gravitational parameters, initial-state source, integration or fitting method, validity interval and uncertainty. A numerically accurate integration can still miss the observed motion if its force model or input state is inadequate.[^1404]
