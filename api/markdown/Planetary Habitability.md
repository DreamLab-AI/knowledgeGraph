Planetary habitability is the degree to which a world has conditions that could support life, judged mainly by whether liquid water can exist there.[^1][^2]

NASA defines the habitable zone as the distance from a star at which liquid water could exist on the surfaces of orbiting planets. Every star has one, its location depends on the star's size and brightness, and it is wider for hotter stars.[^1] Being inside the zone shows only that a surface condition could be met.[^1]

Habitability is not limited to stellar distance. NASA says planetary science uses data to map zones of habitability and study the chemistry of unfamiliar worlds.[^2] Enceladus is reported to have ingredients needed for life and likely hydrothermal vents in its ocean, and ESA describes the oceans of Jupiter's icy moons as possible habitats.[^3][^4]

None of these sources reports a detection of life. They describe conditions and candidate environments, and the term should not be read as a claim that any named world is inhabited.[^1][^3]
