Vibration testing is the environmental test in which flight hardware is shaken, commonly with random vibration applied through its mounting points, to check that it can withstand the launch environment and that its workmanship is sound.[^1][^2]

A JPL practice sheet describes applying broadband, shaped random vibration to the test item through its service attachments, over a band of about 20 Hz to 2000 Hz and in each of three mutually perpendicular axes. The vibration specification is servo controlled at one or more interface points between the test item and the fixture.[^1]

This sheet gives two objectives: verifying the design against the launch vibroacoustic environment with some margin, and screening workmanship. Qualification and protoflight levels are typically raised 3 to 6 dB above flight acceptance levels, and the sheet calls random vibration the most widely adopted dynamics test for spaceflight hardware.[^1]

A paper on the Goddard environmental specification says random vibration is also performed at system level for smaller spacecraft, with an acoustic test performed separately. The current Goddard standard, GSFC-STD-7000, is described as a baseline for demonstrating by test or analysis that hardware performs in the expected mission environments and meets minimum workmanship standards.[^2][^3]

These sources come from two NASA centres and give no test levels for a specific mission. The JPL sheet derives criteria from launch vehicle interface conditions and workmanship minimums, so levels depend on the vehicle and hardware. Neither source describes sine, shock or acoustic procedures in detail.
