A biosignature is a characteristic, substance, structure or pattern that can provide evidence of past or present life. Candidates include fossils and textures, minerals, isotopic ratios, organic molecules, atmospheric gases, surface reflectance and changes through time. NASA defines a *potential* biosignature as something that might have a biological origin but still needs more data or study before a conclusion about life can be reached.[^1]

## From measurement to interpretation

Life detection involves several distinct claims. An instrument first records a signal. Analysts infer which material or process produced it. They then ask whether biology is a better explanation than geology, atmospheric chemistry, contamination or an instrumental artefact. Confidence can fall at any step, so a molecular identification is not automatically a biosignature and a potential biosignature is not a detection of life.

Assessment must include the environment in which a signal occurs. For an exoplanet, that can mean the host star's radiation, planetary mass and temperature, atmospheric composition, climate and geological cycling. A widely used framework treats the result probabilistically by comparing biological and abiotic explanations within an integrated planetary model.[^2] Independent observations and analyses are needed because a retrieval can depend on data processing, molecular reference spectra and model assumptions.

Oxygen illustrates the problem. Photosynthesis produces most of Earth's atmospheric oxygen, yet water loss and photochemistry can also create oxygen without biology. Conversely, geological and chemical sinks can conceal biologically produced oxygen. Measurements that test these alternative mechanisms strengthen or weaken a biological interpretation.[^3]

## Reporting uncertain evidence

Life-detection claims attract unusual public attention. A proposed confidence scale encourages researchers to report incremental progress and expected false starts, but its authors described it as a starting point for discussion rather than a binding standard.[^4] Clear reporting should say what was measured, what was inferred, which alternatives remain and whether another team reproduced the result.

K2-18 b provides a current example. A 2025 study reported 3-sigma evidence for dimethyl sulphide or dimethyl disulphide in a JWST spectrum, while acknowledging molecular degeneracy and the need for more observations.[^5] An independent reanalysis found that the result was sensitive to wavelength binning and unresolved instrumental systematics, concluding that the data contained no statistically significant evidence for biosignatures.[^6] The disagreement leaves the molecular claim unresolved. It neither detects extraterrestrial life nor proves that K2-18 b is lifeless.
