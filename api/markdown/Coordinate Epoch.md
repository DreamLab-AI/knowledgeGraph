A coordinate epoch is the single instant to which a coordinate system or set of positions is referred, so that the effects of the natural motions of the Earth are pinned to one snapshot in time.[^1]

NASA says a useful coordinate system for locating stars, planets and spacecraft must be pinned to a snapshot called an epoch. By convention the standard reference epoch is J2000.0, the mean equator and equinox of the year 2000, nominally noon Universal Time on 1 January, and the J means Julian year. Only the long precession part of the precession and nutation effect defines the mean equator and equinox, and the previous epoch in use was B1950.[^1]

SPICE documentation places the J2000 epoch at Greenwich noon on 1 January 2000 in Barycentric Dynamical Time.[^2] That differs from the nominal Universal Time given by NASA, and the sources do not reconcile the two, so the time scale should be stated with the epoch.

Epochs also apply to catalogue positions. ICRF3 source positions are reported for epoch 2015.0 and need propagating to other epochs for the most accurate work, and Navipedia refers its Earth-centred celestial system to the mean equator and equinox of J2000.0.[^3][^4] The epoch of a position catalogue is therefore not the same thing as the epoch used to define a frame's axes.
