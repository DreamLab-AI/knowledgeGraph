Re-entry is the passage of an orbital object into sufficiently dense atmosphere, where drag and heating alter its trajectory and may cause ablation, fragmentation and impact.[^esa-reentry-prediction][^esa-reentry-safety]

At orbital speeds, atmospheric interaction can break up and destroy much of a spacecraft, but compact structures and high-melting-point components can survive to the ground. The surviving debris can follow different paths from the intact vehicle, so a prediction of the entry interface is not the same as a prediction of breakup location or a fragment’s impact point.[^esa-reentry-prediction][^esa-containment]

## Risk and uncertainty

A casualty-risk analysis considers the entry trajectory, fragment mass, size and material, casualty area, ground footprint and population. It also accounts for uncertainty in atmospheric density, orbit and attitude knowledge, manoeuvre execution and vehicle design. The result depends on the physical models used for aerothermal heating, structural breakup, fragment motion and population exposure.[^esa-reentry-safety][^esa-reentry-prediction]

ESA’s policy sets a maximum casualty risk of 1 in 10,000 for each re-entry event and requires a controlled re-entry when an uncontrolled case exceeds that threshold. This is an ESA requirement, not a universal treaty rule. ESA’s 2026 environmental report also describes a rising aggregate casualty-risk trend from uncontrolled re-entries; that population-level statistic is not an individual mission prediction.[^esa-reentry-safety][^esa-casualty-risk-2026]

Controlled re-entry uses manoeuvres to target an entry time and footprint, often over an uninhabited ocean area. It can reduce public exposure but does not eliminate uncertainty in burn execution, atmospheric conditions, fragmentation or surviving debris. Design for Demise aims to make hardware ablate more completely; containment can reduce the number of independent fragments. Both require system-level assessment because material, geometry, joints and mission constraints affect the result.[^esa-reentry-safety][^esa-reentry-prediction][^esa-containment]

## Forecasts and mission status

Tracking and improved orbit estimates help refine the predicted re-entry window and ground swath as an event approaches. Forecasts remain probabilistic, particularly for an uncontrolled event. ESA’s public re-entry reporting distinguishes controlled, assisted and uncontrolled cases; an exact impact point should not be stated as certain where breakup and atmospheric uncertainty remain.[^esa-reentry-prediction][^esa-aeolus-map]

Aeolus re-entered in July 2023 after ESA used remaining fuel for a series of assisted manoeuvres. The spacecraft had not been designed for controlled re-entry, and ESA reported that the manoeuvres targeted a safer final track. Its post-event map notes that pinpointing the exact location was difficult, illustrating the difference between influencing the track and knowing every fragment’s location.[^esa-aeolus][^esa-aeolus-map]

In the UK, CAA end-of-life guidance requires operators covered by the post-31 January 2026 application rules to maintain a disposal plan; launch and return guidance addresses risk assessment for launch vehicle orbital stages. The licence date and activity determine which condition applies. The guidance supports planning and risk control but does not make a re-entry forecast exact.[^uk-caa-eol][^uk-launch-return-guidance]
