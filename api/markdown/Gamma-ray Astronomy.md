Gamma-ray astronomy is the study of cosmic sources through their gamma rays, which have the smallest wavelengths and the most energy of any wave in the electromagnetic spectrum.[^1]

Gamma rays cannot be captured and reflected by mirrors as optical light and X-rays can. NASA describes detectors that typically contain densely packed crystal blocks, where a gamma ray strikes an electron and loses energy in Compton scattering.[^1]

Space missions carry out the observations. NASA describes the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope as detecting gamma rays and addressing topics from pulsars and cosmic rays to gamma-ray bursts.[^2] ESA describes Integral as the first space observatory to observe gamma rays, X-rays and visible light simultaneously, with principal targets including gamma-ray bursts, supernova explosions and regions thought to contain black holes.[^3]

Gamma rays are also produced on Earth by nuclear explosions, lightning and radioactive decay, according to NASA.[^1] The cited pages do not set a formal energy boundary for the band, so numerical limits should come from the instrument or paper being discussed.
