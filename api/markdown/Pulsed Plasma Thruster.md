A pulsed plasma thruster is an electric thruster that produces thrust by triggering an electric arc between a pair of electrodes, typically ablating a solid propellant such as polytetrafluoroethylene or ionising a gaseous propellant.[^1]

A 2002 NASA Marshall paper distinguishes gas-fed and ablative forms. The ablative form uses a solid propellant such as Teflon, which the paper associates with compactness and ease of system integration, while plume contamination with some solid propellants and lower specific impulse may limit its use.[^2]

According to the NASA survey, pulsed plasma and vacuum arc thrusters are grouped together, with thrust of 4 to 500 micronewtons and specific impulse of 87 to 3,200 seconds listed as their ranges.[^1] The 2002 paper cites simplicity, very small impulse bits, reliability and high specific impulse as benefits, while these thrusters remain among the least efficient electric propulsion systems, at about ten per cent.[^2]

Its main focus is a high-energy two-stage design with a liquid lithium-fed injector, providing evidence for the thruster class and its trade-offs rather than for the performance of any flight unit.[^2] The efficiency figure is dated, and the survey ranges are nominal values for the grouped device types.[^1]
