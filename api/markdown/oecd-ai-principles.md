- ### Definition
  - The OECD AI Principles are the first intergovernmental standard on AI governance, establishing normative commitments to [[Transparency]], [[Accountability]], [[Fairness]], and [[Responsible AI]] that have shaped national [[AI Regulation]] strategies and multilateral policy frameworks.
- ### Relationships
  - The OECD AI Principles are grounded in a [[Responsible AI]] ethical tradition and provide the normative scaffolding for [[AI Governance]] and [[AI Ethics]] discourse. They operationalise commitments to [[Fairness]] and [[Accountability]] as policy obligations for governments and the private sector, and they have directly influenced the architecture of the EU AI Act and national AI strategies. Their emphasis on transparency maps onto [[AI Governance Framework]] requirements and underpins the protection of [[Human Rights]] in automated decision-making.
- ### Content
  - Adopted in May 2019 by the OECD Council at Ministerial level, the AI Principles emerged from deliberations by the OECD Expert Group on AI (AIGO), which included representatives from government, industry, civil society, and academia across 36 member countries. The five principles—inclusive growth; human-centred values and fairness; transparency and explainability; robustness, security, and safety; and accountability—were designed to be technology-neutral and applicable across sectors and jurisdictions.

  - The principles were endorsed by G20 leaders in Osaka in June 2019, giving them political weight beyond OECD membership and signalling a global consensus on foundational AI governance norms. They were updated in 2024 to reflect advances in generative AI and foundation models, adding language on systemic risk, safety evaluations, and the responsibilities of developers of large-scale general-purpose AI systems. This update aligned the principles more closely with the EU AI Act's risk-based approach.

  - The OECD AI Policy Observatory (OECD.AI) was established as the implementation infrastructure, cataloguing over 1,000 national AI policies and strategies and providing tools for comparative policy analysis. The principles serve as a reference point for the Global Partnership on AI (GPAI) and UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of AI, creating a web of interlocking international instruments. Critics note that the principles remain voluntary and aspirational, lacking binding enforcement mechanisms, though their influence on binding regulatory instruments in the EU, UK, and Canada demonstrates their practical normative power.
