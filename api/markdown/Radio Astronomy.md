Radio astronomy measures natural radio emission from objects and processes in space. Antennas convert incoming electromagnetic waves into electrical signals; receivers, timing systems and digital correlators then preserve and combine the measurements.[^1]

## Telescopes and interferometry

A larger collecting area generally improves sensitivity to faint emission. Angular detail depends on wavelength and aperture. Interferometry combines signals from separated antennas so that an array can synthesise a much larger effective aperture, but it requires precise timing, synchronisation and calibration.[^2] Long baselines improve angular resolution without supplying the collecting area of a filled dish of the same diameter, so sensitivity and resolution remain separate design properties.

Interferometry supports continuum imaging, spectral-line studies, pulsar timing and rapid searches for transient sources. It also has limits: an array samples only part of the spatial-frequency plane, and incomplete coverage can suppress or distort structure unless observation and reconstruction are designed accordingly.

## Calibration and interference

Radio data require flagging of radio-frequency interference, correction of instrumental and atmospheric effects, calibration against known sources and careful imaging. e-MERLIN supplies calibrated datasets and initial images, while warning that those images normally need further work before publication.[^3] A pipeline product is therefore a starting point for scientific interpretation, not a finished conclusion.

Protected spectrum and remote sites reduce interference from communications and electronics, but do not remove it. Aircraft, satellites, adjacent-band transmitters and unintended emissions can still contaminate observations.[^4]

## UK capability

e-MERLIN links seven selectable radio telescopes across a 217-kilometre span of Great Britain to its correlator at Jodrell Bank. It supports imaging, very-long-baseline interferometry and fast-transient observations.[^5] The Square Kilometre Array Observatory has its global headquarters on the Jodrell Bank site, while its telescope arrays are being built in Australia and South Africa. The headquarters, the University of Manchester's observatory and the overseas telescope sites are distinct facilities and organisations.
