Ionospheric E layer, the electrically conducting layer of the ionosphere found at about 100 km altitude in the historical account, was the first ionospheric layer to be discovered.[^1]

NOAA JetStream reports that in 1901 Guglielmo Marconi transmitted a signal between Europe and North America and showed that it had to bounce off a conducting layer at about that altitude. Sir Edward Appleton named the layer the (E)lectrical-Layer in 1927, and the conducting layers found later were named alphabetically as D and F.[^1]

E layer, one of three regions into which the ionosphere is divided, exists through solar radiation striking the atmosphere. JetStream states that because the ionosphere exists through solar radiation striking the atmosphere, all three layers are denser during the day and decrease in density at night. NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center adds that the layers arise from the spectral variability of the radiation and the density of atmospheric constituents.[^1][^2]

Retained sources do not give the vertical limits of the E layer, its peak electron density or the behaviour of any sporadic variant. The roughly 100 km figure belongs to the historical demonstration and should not be read as a layer boundary. Both sources are from NOAA, so they count as limited independent evidence.[^1][^2]

[^1]: NOAA, “JetStream Max: The Ionosphere”. https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/ionosphere-max
[^2]: NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center, “Ionosphere”. https://www.spaceweather.gov/phenomena/ionosphere
