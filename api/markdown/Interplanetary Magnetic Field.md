Interplanetary magnetic field refers to the solar magnetic field carried outward through interplanetary space by the solar wind.[^1][^2]

NOAA describes the solar wind as a plasma with the solar magnetic field embedded in it, flowing outward together. Because the Sun rotates, the wind forms a spiral of faster and slower, denser and thinner streams, and the field is organised along with it. A review of the field's large-scale properties treats them as determined by the solar wind velocity structure: high-velocity streams are almost invariably identified with a single magnetic polarity, and most large-scale, regularly recurring patterns in interplanetary space are best organised by relating them to those streams.[^1][^2]

Polarity has practical consequences. NOAA states that a switch in the polarity of the field embedded in the solar wind is a strong indication that Earth has crossed the current sheet, and that space weather impacts depend on the direction of this field as well as on wind speed and density. For geomagnetic storms, NOAA singles out a sustained southward-directed field at the dayside of the magnetosphere, opposite to Earth's own field, as the most effective condition for transferring energy from the solar wind into the magnetosphere.[^1][^3]

Retained sources do not give field strengths, the spiral angle at Earth or a detailed account of sector structure, and the review itself reports long-term deviations from the expected spiral that it says raise difficulties for the usual theoretical treatments. The term should not be confused with the magnetic field at the solar surface, with Earth's magnetic field or with the current sheet, which is a related structure rather than the field itself.[^1][^2]
