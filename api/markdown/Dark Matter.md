Dark matter is invisible material that makes up most of the mass in galaxies and galaxy clusters and is detected through its gravity rather than its light.[^1]

NASA states that dark matter interacts with ordinary matter through gravity but does not seem to interact at all with the electromagnetic spectrum, so it does not absorb, reflect or emit light. It dates the current concept to the early 1930s, when Fritz Zwicky described anomalous galaxy motions in the Coma Cluster, and to Vera Rubin's later work on stars at the edges of spiral galaxies.[^1]

Gravitational lensing is one way dark matter is studied. NASA describes the Bullet Cluster as some of the best direct evidence, with lensing maps showing mass distributed differently from the hot gas, and says researchers think this material is likely dark matter.[^1] ESA says Gaia is probing the distribution of dark matter in the Milky Way by watching the large-scale motion of stars.[^2]

What dark matter is has not been established. NASA lists hypothetical candidates such as weakly interacting massive particles and axions, and ESA states that scientists remain unsure what dark matter actually is.[^1][^3] It is distinct from dark energy, which is associated with accelerating expansion.
