Declination is the angular coordinate on the celestial sphere that corresponds to latitude, measured in degrees north or south of the celestial equator.[^1]

NASA's reference chapter gives the celestial equator as zero declination and the poles as plus and minus ninety degrees, with positive and negative values referring to north and south. Declination is paired with right ascension, the celestial equivalent of longitude, and the two together fix a position uniquely on the celestial sphere in the way latitude and longitude do on the Earth's surface.[^1]

A declination value belongs to a particular reference system and epoch. Precession shifts the apparent positions of stars slowly, and NASA gives this as the main reason for referring to a common epoch such as J2000.0. The chapter also notes that right ascension and declination measurements can be transformed to the International Celestial Reference System, which is compatible with the J2000.0-based system.[^2][^1]

Declination is an angle on the sky and should not be confused with an observer's local elevation above the horizon. NASA treats pointing in hour angle and declination as a different arrangement from azimuth and elevation, which are derived from published right ascension and declination by computer programmes. Ecliptic latitude is a separate coordinate, defined relative to the ecliptic plane rather than the celestial equator.[^1][^3]
