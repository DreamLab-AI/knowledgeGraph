An environmental control and life support system, or ECLSS, maintains the conditions needed for people and other living payloads inside a spacecraft or habitat. Its scope includes atmospheric composition and pressure, ventilation, carbon-dioxide and trace-contaminant removal, oxygen supply, water recovery and quality, waste handling, environmental monitoring, and interfaces to thermal control and fire detection.[^1]

## Operational systems

The International Space Station combines several linked subsystems. Its Water Recovery System processes urine-derived water, humidity condensate and other wastewater through filtration and catalytic treatment. NASA reports that the station recovers about 90% of its water.[^2] Air revitalisation removes carbon dioxide and trace contaminants. The Oxygen Generation System electrolyses water, producing oxygen for the cabin and hydrogen that can be used with captured carbon dioxide in a Sabatier reactor.

These figures describe particular material loops, not one overall recycling efficiency. NASA reports that the present station process recovers about half of the oxygen contained in exhaled carbon dioxide; the remainder still drives a need for resupply.[^3] Consumables, replacement parts, reliability, maintenance time and waste disposal remain part of the system design.

## Closing resource loops

ESA's Advanced Closed Loop System is an ISS technology demonstration that concentrates cabin carbon dioxide, combines it with hydrogen in a Sabatier reactor and returns recovered water to an electrolyser. The same ESA account says methane is vented and only part of the carbon dioxide is recovered.[^4] It is therefore a partial recovery system rather than a materially self-sufficient habitat.

Bioregenerative research adds plants and microorganisms to physicochemical processing. ESA's MELiSSA architecture separates waste breakdown, carbon processing, nitrification, photosynthetic oxygen production, water recovery and food production into controlled compartments.[^5] A peer-reviewed review describes this as a route towards greater recovery for long missions while identifying substantial development and demonstration work still required.[^6] MELiSSA is a research programme, not an operational closed habitat.

## Exploration and UK capability

As distance and mission duration increase, rapid resupply, evacuation and ground-directed repair become less available. Greater loop closure can reduce launched consumables, but it also adds power demand, control complexity, biological instability and maintenance requirements. System design must balance closure against reliability, mass, energy, spares and crew time.

The UK space-exploration roadmap identifies strengths in extreme physiology, medicine, monitoring, miniaturised devices and related life-support technologies.[^7] These are relevant capabilities, but the cited evidence does not establish an independent UK flight-qualified ECLSS. Individual research projects and components should be described by their demonstrated maturity rather than as a complete operational system.
