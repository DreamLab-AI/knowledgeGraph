Radar altimetry is a form of active microwave remote sensing in which a downward-looking radar transmits short pulses and measures the round-trip delay to determine distance to a surface.[^1][^2]

Natural Resources Canada classes altimeters as non-imaging profiling sensors that generally look straight down at nadir. They measure height or elevation only if the altitude of the platform is accurately known, and the tutorial lists aircraft altitude determination, topographic mapping and sea surface height estimation as uses.[^1] A training course from the National Oceanography Centre describes the altimeter as a radar at vertical incidence that sends a thin shell of radar energy, which is reflected back from the sea surface.[^3]

NASA explains that radar altimeters send pulses of microwaves that bounce off the ocean surface, and that the instrument times the return while tracking the satellite's position to derive the height of the sea surface beneath it. Moisture in the air slows the signal and can make the ocean appear higher or lower than it is, so the satellites carry instruments to measure water vapour and account for its effects. NASA also notes that four missions since 1992 have repeated the same orbit every ten days.[^2]

Radar altimetry should not be confused with imaging radar or SAR, which build two-dimensional images. The sources here concentrate on oceans, and they do not establish the accuracy of the correction methods or of altimetry over land or ice.
