Water quality monitoring is the repeated observation of the constituents of water that determine its suitability for particular uses, with satellite data supplying indicators such as chlorophyll and ocean colour.[^1][^2]

NASA states that the physical, chemical, biological and microbiological constituents of water determine its suitability for particular uses, and this is what is meant by its quality.[^1] Factors affecting it include nutrients, pollution, water temperature, food web changes, introduced species and changes in water flow.[^1] NASA calls chlorophyll concentrations in water a key measurement of quality, and lists microplastic concentrations, agricultural impact surveys and ocean surface temperature datasets among the useful data.[^1]

This remote measurement rests on ocean colour. Ocean colour sensors are designed to retrieve the water-leaving radiance, which bio-optical algorithms use to estimate a number of geophysical parameters.[^2] Atmospheric correction is challenging because only about 4% of the radiation measured by a satellite instrument originates from the water surface, so sensors need high signal to noise ratios.[^2] NASA ocean colour data are used to track phytoplankton, detect harmful algal blooms and monitor ocean health.[^3]

These retained sources do not establish how inland or optically complex waters are handled, nor any threshold that turns an observation into a regulatory judgement. A bloom signal from space indicates the presence of phytoplankton and does not by itself show toxicity.[^2][^3]
