A galaxy group is a gravitationally bound collection of galaxies, which NASA describes as about 100 or fewer members held together by their mutual gravity.[^1]

The Milky Way sits in a neighbourhood with over 50 other galaxies called the Local Group. NASA places the Local Group just off the edge of the Virgo cluster and within the Laniakea supercluster.[^1][^2]

Larger collections are called clusters. The Chandra field guide treats groups and clusters of galaxies on one page and describes clusters as the largest gravitationally bound objects in the universe.[^3] The cited sources do not give a sharp boundary between a rich group and a poor cluster, so the member count should be read as indicative.

A group differs from a supercluster in binding. NASA says groups and clusters can be arranged in superclusters, which are not gravitationally bound.[^1] The Laniakea paper adds that the term supercluster is not precise, so statements about what lies inside a supercluster depend on the definition used.[^2]

[^2]: arXiv (Tully, Courtois, Hoffman and Pomarede), “The Laniakea supercluster of galaxies”. https://arxiv.org/abs/1409.0880
[^3]: Chandra X-ray Center, “Field Guide to X-ray Sources: Groups & Clusters of Galaxies”. https://chandra.harvard.edu/xray_sources/galaxy_clusters.html
