Mars orbit radiation environment is the particle field and associated radiation exposure encountered by a spacecraft in orbit around Mars, including the effects of planetary atmosphere, spacecraft shielding and solar conditions.

## Sources and observations

Energetic-particle sources include galactic cosmic rays and solar energetic particles. Mars Odyssey’s Mars Radiation Environment Experiment (MARIE) measured charged particles during cruise and in Mars orbit. Its measurements ended after a major solar event damaged the instrument in October 2003, so its record is historically important but finite.[^1][^2]

Solar-particle events can produce different observations in orbit and at the surface. During the September 2017 event, MAVEN measured energetic protons above Mars while Curiosity’s Radiation Assessment Detector measured dose at the surface. Only the more energetic protons in the illustrated bands penetrated the atmosphere sufficiently to be observed there.[^3]

Mars has no global intrinsic magnetic field, but its ionosphere and local crustal fields interact with the solar wind. This plasma environment varies with solar conditions and should be distinguished from the energetic-particle dose measured by a radiation instrument.[^4]

## Dose, shielding and model limits

Particle flux or spectrum describes the radiation field. Absorbed dose, dose equivalent and effective dose are derived outputs with different meanings. A dose estimate should identify its detector or tissue response, energy range, shielding, trajectory, interval and solar reference conditions.

Surface measurements include atmospheric shielding and cannot be substituted for an orbital measurement. Conversely, an orbit instrument does not directly determine dose in a habitat or crew member. Solar-event frequency and intensity remain important uncertainties in extrapolating a short measurement record to a long mission.[^3][^5]

Radiation transport estimates depend on environmental spectra, material composition and nuclear-interaction models. NASA’s uncertainty documentation flags secondary-particle and thick-shielding uncertainties, and notes that some numerical estimates do not use the current tool version.[^6] Crew protection requirements use specified reference spectra and mission classes; they are design requirements, not universal Mars-orbit dose limits.[^7]
