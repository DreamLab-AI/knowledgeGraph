Ocean colour remote sensing is the measurement of the colour of sunlit sea water from satellite sensors in order to estimate constituents such as phytoplankton chlorophyll.[^1][^2]

Phytoplankton contain chlorophyll, and NASA describes how large blooms change the way the sea surface reflects light, so that concentrations of chlorophyll and other light-catching pigments can be estimated from satellite observations.[^2] An IOCCG working-group report states that phytoplankton pigments affect ocean colour mostly through the absorption spectrum, with fluorescence altering the signal in specific wavebands.[^3]

That report treats accurate atmospheric correction as a requirement for useful retrievals.[^3] ESA describes the Sentinel-3 Ocean and Land Colour Instrument, which pairs ocean colour with atmospheric correction bands, as having 21 bands from 400 to 1020 nm tuned to ocean colour, vegetation and atmospheric correction requirements.[^4]

NASA says chlorophyll-a estimates from ocean colour are used as an indicator of harmful algal blooms, which is an indicator and not a toxin measurement.[^1] The IOCCG report dates from 1998, so it supports the physical principle and the correction requirement rather than the performance of current sensors.[^3]
