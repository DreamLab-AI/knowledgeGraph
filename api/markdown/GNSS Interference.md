GNSS interference is radio-frequency energy that disrupts the reception or use of GNSS signals, and it ranges from jamming, which overpowers authentic signals, to spoofing, which transmits a false signal mimicking an authentic one.[^1]

The FAA Interference Resource Guide, written for United States operators and pilots, describes jamming as the broadcast of a signal that overpowers authentic satellite signals and spoofing as the transmission of a false signal mimicking the authentic one, which may carry altered location or timing data. It also records less frequent unplanned events linked to faulty commercial equipment and to inadvertent re-radiation of GNSS signals near airports.[^1]

A 2015 European GNSS Agency workshop report refers to both intentional and unintentional jamming, and to effects on the signal-to-noise ratio and on timing and navigation data. The GPS performance standard acknowledges that local interference is one of several conditions that can make signals temporarily untrackable.[^2][^3]

These sources describe the phenomenon and aviation consequences rather than a measured rate of occurrence. The FAA guide is a revision-controlled operational document and the workshop report is an archived news item, so neither establishes current incident levels or detection method performance.[^1][^2]
