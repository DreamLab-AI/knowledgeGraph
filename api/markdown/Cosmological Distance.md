Cosmological distance is the distance to far-off galaxies and other extragalactic sources, which astronomers infer from calibrated measurements such as the period and brightness of Cepheid stars and the redshift of galaxy light.[^1]

NASA describes how a relation between a Cepheid's period and its luminosity allowed distances to be measured to Cepheid stars in far-off galaxies. Hubble and Humason then compared galaxy distances determined from Cepheids with galaxy redshifts and found that more distant objects were receding faster.[^1]

How well a distance is known depends on the method and on how far away the target is. ESA reports that Gaia measured the distances of the nearest stars to an accuracy of 0.001%, a figure that applies to nearby stars in the Milky Way and does not carry over to other galaxies.[^2]

Surveys of distant galaxies describe reach in light-years or in the time light has taken to arrive. ESA says the Euclid mission is building a three-dimensional map of the Universe, with time as the third dimension, from galaxies out to 10 billion light-years.[^3] The cited pages do not define separate distance measures such as comoving, luminosity or angular-diameter distance, so a distance quoted in light-years should be read together with the method behind it.
