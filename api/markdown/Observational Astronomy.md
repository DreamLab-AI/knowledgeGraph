Observational astronomy collects and interprets measured signals from astronomical sources. It spans imaging, spectroscopy, photometry, astrometry, polarimetry, timing and particle or gravitational-wave observations across ground-based and space-based facilities.

## From proposal to measurement

An observing programme begins with a scientific question and a measurable requirement. Investigators choose wavelength, angular or spectral resolution, field of view, cadence, sensitivity and observing conditions, then account for the telescope and instrument response. Surveys apply a repeatable selection function across a large sample; targeted observations trade breadth for depth or time resolution.

Raw measurements require calibration, quality assessment and documented processing before physical quantities can be inferred. Optical data may need bias, flat-field, wavelength and atmospheric corrections. Radio arrays need interference flagging, gain calibration and image reconstruction. Initial pipeline images can require further specialist work before they support publication.[^1]

## Inference and uncertainty

Measured position, brightness or spectrum is distinct from a derived physical property. Astrometry, for example, records angular position and its change over time. Distance and radial velocity are also needed to reconstruct an object's three-dimensional motion through space.[^2] Analyses should report uncertainty, selection effects and model assumptions so that later work can reproduce or revise the inference.

Long-lived archives are part of the observing system. ESA lists Gaia as post-operations, but its calibrated catalogues continue to support research after spacecraft observations have ended.[^3] Mission status, data-release status and continuing scientific use should therefore be recorded separately.

## UK capability

The UK Astronomy Technology Centre at the Royal Observatory Edinburgh develops instruments for ground and space telescopes and conducts observational research.[^4] UK teams have also held science, design and hardware roles in international missions. For the James Webb Space Telescope, UK institutions led the European contribution to the Mid-Infrared Instrument with partners including NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory.[^5] This is a substantial national contribution within an international programme, rather than sole ownership of the observatory.
