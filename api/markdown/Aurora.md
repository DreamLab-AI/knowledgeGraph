Aurora is light emitted when energetic electrons, guided by Earth's magnetic field, collide with atoms and molecules in the upper atmosphere.[^1]

NOAA describes electrons energised in the magnetosphere tail and at lower altitudes along auroral field lines that follow the field to the polar regions and strike oxygen and nitrogen. The collisions excite the atoms and molecules, which release the energy as light, and the aurora typically forms 80 to 500 km above the surface.[^1] ESA's Cluster pages describe solar wind particles that enter through the polar cusps and spiral down the field lines, and its magnetosphere graphic names the cusps as the places where charged particles can enter the atmosphere and create the auroras.[^2][^3]

Earth's field guides the electrons so that the aurora forms two ovals approximately centred at the magnetic poles, and NOAA says these expand away from the poles during major geomagnetic storms.[^1] NOAA also says protons cause faint and diffuse aurora that is usually not easily visible to the human eye.[^1]

Both agencies emphasise different parts of the process (the magnetotail and the cusps), and the sources opened here do not apportion the energy between them. Seeing an aurora also needs clear and dark skies, so the occurrence of an event is separate from whether it is visible from a given place.[^1][^2]
