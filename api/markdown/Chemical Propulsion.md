Chemical propulsion converts energy released by a chemical reaction or catalytic decomposition into hot, pressurised gas and then directs that gas through a nozzle to produce thrust. It is used where a spacecraft needs comparatively high thrust or a rapid manoeuvre, provided the available total impulse and the integrated system meet the mission need.[^1]

## System boundary and architecture

A flight propulsion system includes much more than the thruster. Tanks, propellant-management devices, pressurant vessels, regulators or pumps, manifolds, valves, filters, lines, sensors, control electronics and ground-support equipment determine whether propellant reaches the chamber at the required state and whether the system can be loaded, isolated and made safe.[^1][^2][^3] Evidence for a thruster head does not establish the maturity of this complete chain.

Liquid chemical systems are commonly divided by the number of stored propellant streams. A [[Monopropellant Propulsion|monopropellant]] system sends one formulation to a catalyst or other initiation device. A [[Bipropellant Propulsion|bipropellant]] system meters separate fuel and oxidiser streams into a chamber. The feed can be pressure-regulated, blowdown or pump-fed. A regulated pressure-fed system uses a separate high-pressure gas supply and regulation hardware; a blowdown system stores pressurant with the propellant and accepts falling inlet pressure as propellant is expelled.[^4][^5]

Pump-fed spacecraft concepts can reduce propellant-tank pressure and its associated structural mass, but they add electrical demand, controls, moving hardware and pressure ripple. ESA's electric pump-fed MMH/MON study used separate fuel and oxidiser pumps plus dampers to control pressure and mixture-ratio oscillations. At its 25 March 2024 status date it remained ground-development work: an integrated hot-fire campaign and conversion of an off-the-shelf pump into a space-rated design were still future steps.[^4]

## Performance and sizing

Mission sizing starts with required total impulse, thrust, minimum impulse bit, firing modes, thrust direction, response and repeatability. It must also cover duty cycle, restart count, propellant throughput and the consequences of plume impingement.[^2] A quoted specific impulse or thrust is incomplete without the propellant, inlet pressure, mixture ratio where applicable, propellant and catalyst temperatures, pulse or steady operation, test environment and hardware configuration.

NASA's current Small Spacecraft review publishes broad thrust and specific-impulse ranges, but states that they summarise only the devices in that survey and do not define the wider technology's limits.[^1] Those ranges should support early comparison rather than requirements or procurement. Delivered spacecraft performance also includes dry mass for tanks and feed equipment, unusable residuals, thermal-control and electrical demands, and pointing losses.

## Storability, handling and failure

`Storable` means that a propellant can be retained over a mission under defined conditions; it does not mean that it is thermally passive or benign. Material compatibility, seals, contamination, propellant acquisition, pressurant interaction, leakage and temperature history remain part of the design. NASA reports that hydrazine freezes near 1.6 °C. Freeze–thaw can damage lines, fittings, seals and diaphragms, while partially thawed propellant can restrict flow or cause a hard start.[^6]

Chemical systems can fail through external or internal leakage, a valve stuck open or closed, line blockage, regulator or pump failure, contaminant-driven flow decay, catalyst ageing, delayed ignition, mixture-ratio or pressure transients, thermal overstress and liquid slosh. NASA has documented contaminants in nominally high-purity hydrazine that can accumulate in small thrusters and reduce flow and performance.[^7] Redundant isolation, filtration, health instrumentation and operating inhibits address selected hazards, but each adds interfaces and failure modes.

## Qualification and maturity

Verification should exercise the relevant steady burns and pulses, inlet pressures, temperatures, minimum impulse bits, restarts, throughput and lifetime. Integrated testing also covers valve timing, feed transients, thermal-vacuum conditions, vibration, leakage and safeing.[^2] A successful hot-fire, an environmental test, a qualification campaign and a flight demonstration are different evidence levels. Flight heritage remains configuration- and mission-specific: a change of propellant, catalyst, valve, scale, duty cycle or environment can require new analysis and delta-qualification.[^1]

## UK capability

The UK Space Agency's national facility at Westcott opened with chemical-propulsion vacuum-test capacity up to 1,500 N and is operated by Nammo UK.[^9] Nammo's current Westcott record lists cold-gas acceptance work and vacuum hot-firing for monopropellant and bipropellant engines up to 20 N alongside the national facility.[^8] These are established UK test capabilities. They do not by themselves qualify a product; qualification still belongs to a declared article, configuration, campaign and acceptance basis.
