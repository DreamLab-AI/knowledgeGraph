An astrophysical jet is a narrow, fast outflow of gas and particles launched from the neighbourhood of a compact object such as an accreting black hole.[^1][^2]

NASA describes active galactic nuclei as active supermassive black holes that emit bright jets and winds. The jets of such nuclei can be as small as a few light-hours across but stretch up to hundreds of thousands of light-years into space, and some are detected in radio light.[^1]

Near a black hole, material heading inwards may be rerouted into a pair of jets that leave in opposite directions at close to the speed of light. NASA states that this can occur in black holes of all sizes, and that astronomers do not fully understand how the jets work, so mechanism claims should stay tentative.[^2][^3]

Jets and winds can heat the cold gas of a host galaxy and so temporarily stop star formation, and quasars and blazars are described as powered by accretion onto supermassive black holes. How long these effects last, and how jet orientation shapes what is observed, are not settled by the sources used here.[^1][^4]
