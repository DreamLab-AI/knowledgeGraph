Ionospheric F layer, the ionospheric layer that splits into two layers during the day and recombines into one at night, characterises the upper atmosphere ionisation.[^1]

It is one of the three named layers, D, E and F, that NOAA says arise from the spectral variability of solar radiation and the density of atmospheric constituents. Solar EUV and X-ray radiation provides the ionisation, so the electron content follows solar activity and local time.[^1][^2]

Navipedia describes a daytime process of photoionisation and a night-time process in which recombination of electrons with ions reduces electron density. It places the maximum electron density of the ionosphere as a whole at heights of several hundred kilometres, although the page does not attribute that peak to the F layer by name. NOAA adds that HF radio waves normally support long-distance communication by refraction from the upper layers of the ionosphere.[^1][^3][^4]

Retained sources do not name the two daytime sublayers or give altitude ranges, peak densities or critical frequencies for the F layer, so those details are left open. The layer should not be confused with the whole ionosphere.[^1][^2][^3]
