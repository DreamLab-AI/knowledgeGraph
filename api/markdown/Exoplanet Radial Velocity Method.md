This method detects a planet indirectly by measuring changes in the line-of-sight velocity of its host star.[^1]

NASA describes the starlight wavelengths as alternately squeezed and stretched as the star moves slightly closer to, then slightly farther from, the observer, with the motion caused by gravitational tugs from orbiting planets.[^1] ESO explains the same effect through the Doppler effect, which shifts the stellar spectrum towards longer wavelengths as the star recedes and towards shorter wavelengths as it approaches.[^2]

High-precision spectrographs track the position of spectral lines, with instruments such as HARPS spending most nights monitoring stars for radial-velocity signals.[^2] The sources describe a planet as inferred from the shift, not seen, so the method yields evidence of an orbiting body through the star's motion alone.[^1]

These sources do not set detection thresholds, give a precision for other instruments, or discuss other causes of stellar velocity change. The ESO account is of one instrument, and its description should not be read as a general limit of the method.[^2]

[^2]: ESO, “HARPS”. https://www.eso.org/public/teles-instr/lasilla/36/harps/
