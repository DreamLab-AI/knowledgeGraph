Molecular clouds are large, cold clouds of gas and dust in which stars form.[^1][^2]

Most of the gas in these clouds is molecular hydrogen. Cold molecular hydrogen radiates poorly, so astronomers map the clouds through other molecules present in very small amounts, with carbon monoxide the example given for the Planck all-sky image.[^2]

Star formation in the clouds is described in the literature as gravoturbulent fragmentation: supersonic turbulence generates density fluctuations, and gravity takes over in the densest regions. The cited review presents this as the current understanding in that lecture, not as a settled account of every cloud.[^3]

Cooling lets gas clump into dense pockets, and groups of newly formed stars can sit inside a cloud. Material returned by supernovae and other stellar events is described as enriching later clouds. The sources do not give a lifetime or star-formation efficiency for any particular cloud.[^1]
