An arcjet is an electrothermal thruster. An electric arc passes through a flowing propellant, raising its enthalpy. A converging-diverging nozzle then turns part of that thermal energy into directed exhaust momentum.[^1][^2][^5] The exhaust may be partly ionised, but electric or magnetic fields do not provide the main acceleration. That distinguishes an arcjet from Hall-effect and gridded ion thrusters. It also differs from a resistojet, whose propellant receives heat through a resistive element or heat exchanger rather than a free arc.

## System and operation

A flight arcjet includes a propellant tank and feed system, valves, electrodes, an arc chamber and constrictor, a nozzle, a power-conditioning unit and thermal and electrical interfaces. Hydrazine systems first decompose the liquid in a catalyst bed, then feed the hot gaseous products through the arc. Hydrogen, ammonia and nitrogen-hydrogen mixtures have also been tested, with different storage, molecular mass, material and performance trades.[^3][^5]

Power conditioning has to strike the arc and maintain stable current as spacecraft bus voltage, propellant pressure and mass flow change. Its dynamic interaction with the arc is part of the propulsion design. A hydrazine tank in blowdown, for example, supplies falling pressure and flow over life; the PPU must still start reliably and hold the intended power. High current, ignition transients and switching also introduce conducted and radiated electromagnetic-compatibility work.

Specific impulse rises with electrical energy added per unit propellant mass, within electrode, nozzle, chemistry and heat-rejection limits. Lower mass flow can raise exhaust velocity but can also destabilise the arc or increase local heat loading. Power that reaches electrodes, remains in internal molecular modes or is lost through imperfect expansion does not become directed jet power. A 1991 NASA paper reported that roughly a third of input power became thrust power in its typical arcjet context, while facility pressure also degraded measured performance.[^5] That historical observation is not a universal efficiency for other designs.

## Performance with its test conditions

Numbers must retain propellant, electrical input and hardware state. A 1990 ground qualification paper describes a hydrazine system comprising thruster, catalyst bed, power-control unit and cable. At 1.4 kW input it provided at least 450 s mission-average specific impulse.[^4] An earlier development programme reported 400–730 s from a non-optimised hydrazine design while it was still addressing starting, stability, compatibility, erosion and flight-like PPU operation.[^3] The wider range should not be presented as qualified performance.

A 2023 NASA presentation reproduces manufacturer data for the hydrazine MR-510: 222–258 mN and more than 585–615 s at 2 kW input, labelled flight proven.[^6] The power, propellant and product identity are inseparable from those figures. The slide is useful bounded evidence, but it is not an independent mission report and does not define the performance of all arcjets.

## Life, plume and integration

Electrodes and the constrictor face high current density and temperature. Arc attachment can move, raising noise and local erosion; starts and restarts add thermal and electrical stress. Propellant impurities, catalyst-bed behaviour, nozzle deposition, material compatibility, thermal cycling and long burns affect lifetime. A ground endurance result must state total firing time, number of starts, duty cycle and power rather than compressing them into a generic “life”.

Hot neutral gas, radicals, ions and possible electrode or nozzle products form the plume. Spacecraft accommodation addresses impingement, contamination, surface heating, torque and interactions with arrays or instruments. The power unit and arc also require electromagnetic-interference testing with representative wiring and spacecraft loads. Vacuum-chamber pressure can change the nozzle plume and measured thrust, so facility geometry, pumping and corrections belong in a performance report.

Qualification verifies that a controlled design meets specified vibration, thermal-vacuum, performance and life requirements. Acceptance tests screen the delivered article. A laboratory design, even after hundreds of hours, is not automatically qualified; qualification is not flight; a manufacturer heritage statement should be tied to the particular model and mission configuration. ECSS-E-ST-35-01C supplies the functional, interface, environmental, operational and verification framework, subject to project tailoring.[^7]

## Status and UK boundary

Most detailed open arcjet evidence in this source set is historical US work from the late 1980s and early 1990s. It remains useful for the mechanism, power-unit interaction and failure modes, but its hardware, propellant practice and performance cannot be treated as a current catalogue.

No retained source establishes a current UK operational arcjet product or flight programme. UK preference is therefore not a reason to assign one. A future claim would need a directly opened manufacturer or mission source that identifies the UK organisation, hardware, test state and date.
