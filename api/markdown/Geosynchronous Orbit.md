A geosynchronous orbit is an Earth-centred orbit whose period equals the Earth's rotation period.

Its period is approximately 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds, the sidereal day, rather than the 24-hour mean solar day. A geosynchronous orbit need not be circular or equatorial, so a spacecraft can move north and south or east and west in the sky over an orbital cycle. The ITU definition is period-based; ESA gives the nominal geostationary altitude as 35,786 km above the equator.[^1398][^1400]

A geostationary orbit is the special case with a circular, prograde orbit in Earth's equatorial plane. In the ideal geometry the spacecraft remains over one point in the Earth-fixed frame. Real spacecraft are maintained within allowed longitude and latitude ranges, rather than being inertially stationary. Earth's rotation is irregular; precise Earth-fixed comparisons must use Earth-orientation information and distinguish UT1 from UTC.[^1398][^1400][^1389]

Gravitational and other perturbations move spacecraft away from their nominal orbit. Station keeping applies manoeuvres to control the departure; ESA's GEO example shows a north-south manoeuvre restoring inclination. The station box, manoeuvre schedule and propellant budget are mission and operator constraints, not parts of the ideal orbital definition.[^1401]
