Spacecraft autonomy is the capability of a robotic spacecraft to monitor and control itself to some degree without waiting for ground teams, which NASA describes for fault protection and for onboard navigation.[^1][^2]

NASA's Spacecraft Basics gives the reason for this capability: light time prevents an immediate ground response to anomalies, and tightly constrained tracking schedules limit how quickly problems are detected. Fault protection algorithms therefore mitigate a mishap and try to re-establish contact with Earth.[^1] Safing is one response, shutting down or reconfiguring components to prevent damage, and a command-loss timer can trigger a swap to redundant hardware if no command arrives within a set time.[^1]

NASA's Autonomous Systems and Robotics technical area describes new technologies that function as advisors, advanced automation and autonomous agents, with areas of expertise that include adjustable autonomy, automated planning and scheduling, computer vision and human-robot interaction.[^3] A 2024 NASA assessment team defines autonomous onboard navigation as a spacecraft determining its orbit and planning a path while operating independently of external control and without external informational aids. It describes autonomy as a balance between onboard decision-making and human support, recommends a technology demonstration at Level 3, and states that no spacecraft has yet demonstrated autonomous navigation across multiple mission phases.[^2]

Those levels represent that team's framework for navigation and not a general standard, and the retained sources give no single general definition of autonomy. NASA's grouping of robotics and autonomy in one technical area provides a programme description rather than a technical definition of their relationship.[^3]
