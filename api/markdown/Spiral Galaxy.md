A spiral galaxy is a galaxy with spiral arms, usually a rotating disk of stars and a central bulge.[^1][^2]

NASA describes these galaxies as resembling giant rotating pinwheels with a pancake-like disk and a central bulge or tight concentration of stars. Their arms can be wound tightly or loosely, and some cannot be seen from Earth because the galaxy is viewed edge on.[^1] A university textbook lists a central bulge, a halo, a disk and spiral arms as the components, with interstellar material spread through the disk.[^2]

Spirals are surrounded by halos of old stars, star clusters and dark matter, according to NASA, and the youngest stars form in gas-rich arms.[^1] The textbook likewise finds a mixture of young and old stars, with bright emission nebulae and hot young stars especially in the arms, showing that star formation is still occurring.[^2]

Both sources say that many spirals are barred, with a bar of stars, gas and dust across the centre. NASA names the Milky Way and Andromeda as barred spirals and gives two-thirds as the share, while the textbook gives about two-thirds of nearby spirals.[^1][^2] The textbook also describes Hubble’s sequence from tightly coiled arms around a large bulge to loosely wound arms around a small one. That sequence is a classification by shape and does not by itself establish how a galaxy evolves.[^2]

[^2]: OpenStax, *Astronomy 2e*, section 26.2, “Types of Galaxies”. https://openstax.org/books/astronomy-2e/pages/26-2-types-of-galaxies
