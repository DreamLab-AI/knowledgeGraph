Hypergravity is a gravity level above that felt at Earth's surface, produced for research and training mainly by spinning a payload or person in a centrifuge.[^1][^2]

ESA describes a large centrifuge that treats gravity as an experimental variable, carrying cells, plants and small animals under raised gravity alongside physical and materials experiments.[^1] A study of planarians in that facility applied a short static load, and in some runs vibration, to represent a human-rated launch level, which shows how the facility is used for launch-related questions as well as for long exposures.[^3] High-performance aircraft pilots are trained for short periods at high gravity in centrifuges, and the same review covers lower levels of centrifugation as artificial gravity for deconditioning in spaceflight.[^2]

Evidence on people is thin for long exposures. A 2009 paper states that humans have never been exposed to hypergravity for long durations, and that human centrifuge studies are mostly short, so conclusions from animal and cell work should not be carried over to long human stays unchanged.[^4] The sources do not give a formal threshold at which a gravity level counts as hypergravity, and they do not establish how effects scale with level or duration across species.

Hypergravity is distinct from microgravity, the near-weightlessness of orbital flight, although both are used in comparative experiments. It is also distinct from the brief accelerations of launch, which the planarian study treats as a combined static and vibration load.[^3]
