Ecliptic longitude is the angular distance of a position on the celestial sphere measured along the ecliptic from the first point of Aries, in the same direction as right ascension.[^1][^2]

One course text describes it as analogous to right ascension, but measured along the ecliptic rather than the celestial equator.[^2] The first point of Aries is the ascending node of the Sun's path on the equator, the point from which right ascensions are measured.[^1]

An equinox is a direction along the line where the ecliptic and equatorial planes intersect.[^3] Because both planes move, an ecliptic longitude needs a date, and J2000.0 is the conventional standard epoch.[^3][^4] Nutation in longitude, used in Earth-rotation models, is a separate quantity and should not be confused with the longitude of a star.[^3]

Textbook figures show longitude in a figure, so the verbal definition rests on the course page, and neither source states a conversion formula in the passages retained here.[^1][^2]
