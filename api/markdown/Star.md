A star is an astronomical body that is powered by nuclear fusion in its core.[^1][^2]

The SWEET file records the class and its label and gives no physical definition, so the description of what a star does comes from NASA and a textbook.[^1] NASA describes fusion releasing energy that heats the star and produces pressure that pushes against its gravity. A star that fuses hydrogen to helium in its core is called a main-sequence star.[^2] The textbook adds that once a star reaches the main sequence it gets its energy almost entirely from hydrogen fusion in the core.[^3]

Stars differ in brightness, size, colour and behaviour, and NASA notes that some types change into others quickly while others stay almost unchanged for very long periods.[^2] The pace depends on mass, since more massive stars use up their fuel much more quickly.[^3] Later stages include red giants, white dwarfs and brown dwarfs, which NASA treats as types or relatives of stars.[^2]

Astronomers sort stars by spectral class, which measures surface temperature and not mass or age.[^4] The sources used here do not supply a single physical definition that separates stars from brown dwarfs, and NASA says brown dwarfs are not technically stars.[^2]
