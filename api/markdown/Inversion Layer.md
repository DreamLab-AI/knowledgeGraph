An inversion layer is a layer of the atmosphere in which temperature increases with height, the reverse of the usual fall of temperature with altitude.[^1]

The Met Office explains that the troposphere is typically colder the higher one goes, and that a small layer can form where temperature rises with height instead.[^1] It describes this as often happening in areas of high pressure, where air high up sinks, dries out and warms, and the warm layer can then act as a lid and trap cooler air near the surface.[^1] Inversions are most common in winter, when mist and fog become trapped in the cooler air low down, but they can occur all year round.[^1]

SWEET records the class as equivalent to temperature inversion layer and as an atmosphere layer associated with the inversion phenomenon.[^2] The retained sources give no heights, thicknesses or temperature differences, and only one agency source was opened, so the page states no figures.
