An orbiter is a spacecraft designed to travel to a distant planet and enter orbit about it, carrying enough propulsive capability to decelerate at the right moment for orbit insertion.[^1]

NASA describes orbiter missions as the stage that follows initial reconnaissance with in-depth study of each planet. Designing for orbit brings occultations: a planet can shadow the spacecraft, cutting off solar-panel power and exposing it to large thermal variation, and Earth occultations interrupt uplink and downlink communications.[^1]

Once in orbit, science operations are described in two broad categories, exploring a planetary system and mapping a planet. The first involves observing the planet, satellites, rings and magnetosphere over a tour of typically a few years or more, while the second concentrates on the planet's own surface and atmosphere. NASA notes that either activity is an extended encounter period needing continuous or dependably regular support from the flight team and ground networks.[^2]

These pages are NASA overviews built mainly around planetary missions. They do not set a formal definition covering every body orbited by a spacecraft, nor a numerical design requirement for propulsion, power or communications margins.
