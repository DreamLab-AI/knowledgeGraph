A spacecraft solar cell is a semiconductor photovoltaic device that converts sunlight into electricity, and cells are assembled into panels and arrays as one means of spacecraft power generation.[^1]

NASA distinguishes single-junction cells, which are inexpensive but have relatively low efficiency, from multi-junction cells, which stack semiconductor layers each tuned to absorb a different portion of the solar spectrum.[^1] ESA describes gallium arsenide based semiconductors increasingly supplanting silicon because they reach higher power levels, and NASA Marshall lists germanium, gallium arsenide and gallium indium phosphide layers in typical multi-junction cells.[^2][^3]

Space radiation degrades cells. Protons cause displacement damage in the cell lattice that reduces output voltage and current, and multi-junction cells share this challenge with silicon cells.[^3] ESA says cells are covered by thin cover glass for protection and that the junctions of a multi-junction cell must be matched in current, a match that shifts over life because the junctions degrade at different rates.[^2] NASA lists radiation, coverglass or adhesive darkening, contamination and mechanical or electrical failure among common array degradation factors.[^1]

The ESA article dates from 2009 and describes the position at that time, so the NASA page is the better guide to current practice. The sources cited here do not establish a single degradation rate or efficiency for all missions.
