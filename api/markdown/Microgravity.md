Microgravity is a condition in which a spacecraft and its occupants are in continuous free fall and experience very little sustained support acceleration. Gravity remains substantial in low Earth orbit; the apparent weightlessness arises because the vehicle, crew and equipment fall together. Drop towers and parabolic flights reproduce the condition for seconds, while orbiting laboratories provide exposure lasting months.[^1]

## Physiological effects

Removing the normal mechanical load from the body changes fluid distribution, balance, movement and tissue maintenance. Fluids shift towards the head, the cardiovascular system adapts to a smaller effective circulating volume, and muscles used for posture and locomotion receive less loading. Weight-bearing bones lose mineral, while sensorimotor adaptation can affect coordination and spatial orientation. NASA reports average mineral-density losses of about 1–1.5% per month in weight-bearing bones during spaceflight, although the response varies between crew members and skeletal sites.[^1]

Exercise, nutrition and medical countermeasures reduce deconditioning but do not prevent every change. A 2026 review found effects across the musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, immune and other systems, while stressing that evidence combines crew observations with ground simulations and cellular work.[^2] Spaceflight also brings radiation, altered atmosphere, confinement, workload and disrupted sleep, so an observation made in orbit should not automatically be attributed to microgravity alone.

Most direct human evidence comes from low Earth orbit. Bed rest, suspension rigs, isolation studies, parabolic flight and animal experiments each reproduce only part of that environment. Evidence about long stays in lunar gravity or Martian gravity therefore remains limited, and applying ISS results to those settings requires modelling and further experiments.

## UK research

UK Space Agency funding has supported work on muscle loss, spinal health, isolation and related countermeasures, alongside access to ESA parabolic flights, drop towers and the International Space Station.[^3] The University of Liverpool uses ISS experiments to study muscle ageing and human health.[^4] Northumbria University's Aerospace Medicine and Rehabilitation Laboratory combines bed-rest and parabolic-flight campaigns with a variable-gravity suspension system and work on exercise-based countermeasures.[^5]

These programmes include flight research, short-duration reduced-gravity tests and terrestrial analogues. Their status matters: an analogue can test a mechanism or device without reproducing the radiation, isolation and operational constraints of an orbital or deep-space mission.
