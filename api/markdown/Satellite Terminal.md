
A satellite terminal is endpoint equipment that transmits, receives or both over a satellite communications link.

An earth-station terminal may combine an antenna, RF front end, modem or baseband processing, tracking and control equipment. “Terminal” can also mean a narrower radio-and-antenna unit, so the system boundary should be stated. A user terminal connects a user or platform to satellite service; a gateway connects the satellite network with terrestrial infrastructure. Regulatory category and network role are related in some licensing schemes but are not technical definitions.[^1][^2][^4]

User terminals can be fixed or mobile, including land, aircraft and ship installations. An NGSO terminal may need to track satellites that move across the sky and hand service between them; GEO terminals can generally point towards a satellite at a nearly fixed apparent position. Electronically steered arrays are one tracking approach, but handover behaviour and continuity depend on the network and terminal implementation.[^3][^6][^7]

Spacecraft terminals are designed around mission range, data rate, frequency, power, mass and pointing constraints. They may support command uplink, telemetry or science-data downlink and crosslinks. NASA’s small-spacecraft and onboard-systems descriptions provide reference engineering arrangements, not a universal terminal specification.[^4][^5]

Terminals may use RF or optical equipment. Optical terminals can support high-rate links but require precise pointing; Dstl’s reported LEO-to-ground optical downlink is a specific demonstration, not evidence of a routine consumer terminal.[^4][^8]

In the UK, Ofcom authorisations concern radio-spectrum use by the stated earth-station class or network and do not themselves license the satellite operator’s spacecraft. Read authorisations in their jurisdictional and technical scope; a terminal’s capability to transmit is separate from permission to use a frequency.[^2][^3]
