A comet is a small body of frozen gases, rock and dust that orbits the Sun and, when heated near it, releases gas and dust that form a coma and tails.[^1][^2]

ESA describes the solid nucleus as usually several kilometres across, with a hazy coma of gas and dust forming around it when the Sun's heat activates the comet. Material from the coma is swept into tails, and the comet is named for the tail, which the ESA overview calls its defining characteristic.[^2][^3]

Most comets show two tails: a dust tail made of small particles that curves toward the comet's path, and a plasma or ion tail of charged gas that points away from the Sun, aligned with the solar wind.[^3] NASA treats comets as leftovers from the formation of the Solar System about 4.6 billion years ago, made mostly of ice coated with dark organic material, and says there are likely billions of them in the Kuiper Belt and Oort Cloud.[^1][^4]

Commonly called the dirty snowball, a comet's description is a simplification of its actual structure. The sources do not give a count of comets.
