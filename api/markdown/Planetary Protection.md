Planetary protection is the policy and engineering practice used to control biological and organic contamination during missions beyond Earth. Forward protection seeks to avoid harmful contamination of other celestial bodies and to preserve the scientific integrity of searches for chemical evolution or life. Backward protection addresses possible adverse changes to Earth's environment from extraterrestrial material returned by a spacecraft.[^1] It does not refer to asteroid-impact defence.

## Legal and policy basis

Article IX of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty requires states to conduct exploration so as to avoid harmful contamination of celestial bodies and adverse changes to Earth's environment from introduced extraterrestrial matter.[^2] The treaty creates the international obligation. The Committee on Space Research's 2024 Planetary Protection Policy supplies an international, voluntary and non-legally binding standard that helps spacefaring states implement it.[^3]

COSPAR assigns one of five categories to a mission–target combination. The assessment considers the type of encounter and whether introduced material could compromise research into chemical evolution or life's origins. Flybys and orbiters usually present a different pathway from probes and landers. Category V covers Earth-return missions and distinguishes unrestricted from restricted return. Assignment is risk informed and mission specific; a category is not a permanent biological verdict on a planet or moon.

Controls can include a protection plan, cleanroom assembly, an inventory of organic materials, trajectory biasing, measurement and reduction of microbial burden, partial sterilisation, containment and reports before and after launch. The selected controls depend on the category and may be tailored while preserving the protection objective. Their purpose includes preventing terrestrial material from producing a false positive in later life-detection work. Precautionary containment does not mean that a returned sample is known to contain life or pose a hazard.

## UK implementation

The UK Space Agency published its technical framework in May 2025. It applies to UK operator licences for missions under the Outer Space Act 1986 and Space Industry Act 2018 where an encounter with a Solar System body creates a forward- or backward-contamination risk. UK-licensed missions are expected to show how they adhere to COSPAR policy. The framework recommends the ECSS-U-ST-20C engineering standard while allowing an equivalent approach that demonstrates compliance.[^4]

The Civil Aviation Authority is the UK regulator. It may consult the UK Space Agency and the UK Planetary Protection Advisory Panel, whose members bring expertise from academia, industry and regulation. UK research also tests cleanroom monitoring, decontamination and probabilistic microbial-risk methods.[^5] Scientific advice informs the controls, while licensing decisions remain regulatory decisions under the cited national framework.
