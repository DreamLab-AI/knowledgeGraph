A magnetic pole is a place on Earth's surface where the geomagnetic field is vertical, so that a dip needle stands upright and the horizontal field intensity is zero.[^1][^2]

NOAA calls these dip poles and notes that the north and south positions are not antipodal. In principle they can be found by magnetic survey, but the field is vertical on oval loci traced daily, with considerable day-to-day variation. Dip poles also migrate over time, so a stated position needs an epoch.[^1][^2]

Geomagnetic poles are a different construct. They are computed from the first three Gauss coefficients of a main-field model such as the World Magnetic Model or the International Geomagnetic Reference Field, which defines an axis for a dipole at the centre of Earth. That axis is offset from the rotation axis by approximately 11 degrees, so neither kind of magnetic pole coincides with a geographic pole.[^1][^2]

Both sources come from one publisher, and they give no current coordinates that this page repeats. Declination is unreliable near the poles, and a compass does not show direction at a dip pole.[^2]
