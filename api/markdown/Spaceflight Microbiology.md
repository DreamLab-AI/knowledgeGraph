Spaceflight microbiology is the study and monitoring of microorganisms carried by spacecraft, cargo and crew, including their possible effects on crew health and on onboard systems.[^1][^2]

On the International Space Station, microbes arrive on the contents of resupply spacecraft and on arriving astronauts, and NASA has monitored the station's microbial community since its first module.[^2] Crew members sample and culture microbes from the air, surfaces and water, and identification traditionally followed the return of samples to Earth.[^2][^1]

Sequencing methods have been developed to identify organisms in flight. NASA describes direct swab-to-sequencer identification, and its technical brief says culture-independent methods are being developed to save time and resources.[^2][^1] These sources present such methods as developed or demonstrated. They do not show that routine culture has been replaced.[^2][^1]

Such technical briefs state that experiments indicate spaceflight can increase the virulence of certain bacteria and that crew immune responses are altered during flight.[^1] A laboratory lecture describes the station as only semi-closed, with new bacterial and fungal isolates identified regularly.[^3] Since that lecture dates from 2020, lists of isolates drawn from it are a snapshot and not a fixed inventory.[^3]
