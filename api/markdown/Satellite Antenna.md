
A satellite antenna is a radiating structure that transmits or receives signals for a satellite communications link.

Precision requires the antenna’s location and role: it may sit on a spacecraft, gateway or user terminal, and serve an uplink, downlink or crosslink. It is a component of the link equipment, not by itself a transceiver, modem, complete earth station or communications service.[^1][^2]

Aperture, gain, pointing, polarisation and radiation pattern affect link performance and interference. ITU-R Recommendation S.465-6 gives a reference pattern for earth-station antennas in specified fixed-satellite-service coordination contexts and frequency ranges. Recommendation S.672-4 describes design objectives for certain geostationary fixed-satellite-service antenna patterns. Neither is a universal model for every satellite antenna.[^3][^4]

Mechanically pointed dishes and electronically steered phased arrays are different antenna architectures. A phased array adjusts signals across elements to steer or shape coverage without mechanically rotating the aperture. ESA pages describe a beamformer-chip project, a Galileo uplink-antenna test and the Q-EPA antenna development. Their status differs: a test or project page demonstrates particular engineering activity, while ESA reported in October 2026 that the first Ka-band Q-EPA panel was being assembled. That prototype status does not establish a deployed terminal or operational network.[^5][^6][^7]

Optical communication terminals use focused light rather than RF and require precise pointing. Dstl reported a 2026 demonstration from low Earth orbit to a deployable optical ground station in the Mediterranean. This is evidence of a specific demonstration, not routine service or a UK-based optical ground site.[^8]
