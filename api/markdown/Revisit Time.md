Revisit time is the interval after which a remote sensing system can again observe the same area of the Earth's surface.[^1][^2]

The Natural Resources Canada tutorial separates the orbit cycle, the time for a satellite to pass over the same nadir point a second time, from the revisit period. Steerable sensors can view an area off-nadir before and after the orbit passes over it, which makes the revisit time shorter than the orbit cycle.[^1] Another chapter of the same tutorial instead describes the revisit period as the time to complete one orbit cycle and says it is usually several days, so the wording differs between sections and should be read with that in mind.[^2]

Overlap between the swaths of adjacent orbits shortens the effective interval, and in near-polar orbits high latitudes are imaged more often than the equatorial zone because the orbit paths converge towards the poles.[^1][^2] Missions quote their own figures. Sentinel-2 is specified to give a revisit frequency of five days at the Equator with two satellites phased at 180 degrees, and NASA states that Landsat 9 with Landsat 8 observes the same location every eight days.[^3][^4]

Frequent revisits are linked in the tutorial to monitoring short-lived events such as floods and oil slicks and to obtaining views through persistent cloud.[^2] A quoted revisit figure is a design or constellation value at a stated reference. How often usable, cloud-free imagery is actually obtained is not established by these sources.
