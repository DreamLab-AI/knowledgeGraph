Astronomical spectroscopy separates light from an astronomical source by wavelength and measures the resulting spectrum. Emission and absorption features, continuum shape and changes in wavelength allow astronomers to infer composition, temperature, density, motion and magnetic conditions.[^1]

## Instruments and measurements

A spectrograph disperses light across a detector. Its wavelength coverage determines which transitions can be observed, while spectral resolution determines how finely nearby features can be separated. Resolution is distinct from sensitivity: a highly resolving instrument still needs enough collected light to measure a useful signal.

Radial velocity follows the Doppler displacement of spectral features to measure motion along the line of sight. This supports stellar and galactic dynamics and the search for orbiting planets. Transmission and emission spectra can constrain planetary atmospheres, provided instrumental effects, stellar variability and model uncertainty are included in the analysis.[^2]

Integral-field spectroscopy records a spectrum for each spatial cell in a scene. The result is a data cube with two spatial dimensions and one wavelength dimension, allowing the physical and kinematic structure of an extended object to be mapped.[^3]

## Calibration and interpretation

Raw detector counts are not a scientific spectrum. Processing must account for detector response, wavelength calibration, background, atmospheric transmission for ground facilities and the instrument's line-spread function. Inferences should preserve measurement uncertainties and state which physical or statistical model was fitted. A spectral feature can have more than one plausible carrier, and a best-fit model does not remove degeneracy.

## UK capability

UK institutions build and operate major spectroscopic instruments. Oxford, the UK Astronomy Technology Centre and Durham are members of the HARMONI consortium for the Extremely Large Telescope; HARMONI is a planned integral-field spectrograph rather than an operating facility.[^4] The UK ATC also contributed to MOONS at ESO's Very Large Telescope. MOONS achieved first light on 3 September 2026 and then entered further commissioning and calibration, so first light should not be equated with routine scientific operations.[^5]
