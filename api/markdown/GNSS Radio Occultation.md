GNSS radio occultation is a satellite remote sensing technique that uses GNSS signals received by a low Earth orbit satellite to profile the atmosphere and ionosphere.[^1][^2]

As a GNSS satellite rises or sets behind the horizon as seen from the receiving satellite, its signal passes through the atmosphere and ionosphere. The signal is delayed and its path bent, and the receiver observes this as differences in phase and amplitude.[^2] UCAR describes the measured bending and delay as the basis for retrieving profiles of bending angle, refractivity, temperature, pressure and water vapour, and of electron density in the ionosphere.[^1]

The ROM SAF page states that a typical sounding lasts about a minute and that the receiver sees rays with minimum distances to the surface from zero to roughly 100 kilometres.[^2] The technique was first developed in the 1960s for the outer planets and was demonstrated for Earth by the GPS/MET experiment.[^1]

Operational processing for weather and climate is described for the ROM SAF, which handles data from Metop, Sentinel-6 and Metop-SG, but the pages retained here do not give uncertainty budgets or retrieval algorithms, so those details are not covered.[^2]
