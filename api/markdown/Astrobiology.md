Astrobiology studies the origin, evolution and distribution of life in the Universe. It connects biology and chemistry with geology, astronomy and planetary science: how life began on Earth, which conditions allow it to persist, how living systems alter their surroundings, and how those effects might be recognised elsewhere.[^1] No life beyond Earth has been confirmed.

## Evidence and habitability

Earth supplies the only known example of life. Astrobiologists therefore study organisms in extreme environments, reconstruct ancient environments, simulate planetary conditions in laboratories and test instruments at terrestrial analogue sites. An analogue reproduces selected properties, such as cold, salinity, pressure or mineralogy. It does not reproduce an entire planet, whose gravity, radiation, atmosphere and geological history may differ.

Habitability describes whether stated environmental conditions could support some form of life. It is not evidence that the environment is inhabited. Liquid water is central to searches for life as known on Earth, but investigators must also consider energy, chemistry, stability through time and whether a detectable record could be preserved. Astrobiology accordingly includes prebiotic chemistry and the origin of life, studies of Earth's biosphere, the assessment of other worlds and the design of life-detection methods.

## Missions and UK research

Space missions address different parts of this problem. ESA's Juice mission, launched in 2023 and due at Jupiter in 2031, will characterise Jupiter and the ocean-bearing moons Ganymede, Callisto and Europa as planetary objects and possible habitats.[^2] NASA's Europa Clipper, launched in 2024 and due at Jupiter in 2030, is designed to test whether Europa has conditions suitable for life; it is not a mission that can by itself announce life detection.[^3] ESA's Rosalind Franklin rover is scheduled for launch in late 2028 and is designed to drill into the Martian subsurface in search of morphological and chemical signs of past or present life.[^4] These dates describe current plans and should be checked against live mission sources.

UK research spans field, laboratory, modelling and mission work. AstrobiologyOU studies extreme environments, habitability, biosignatures, planetary protection and the ethical questions raised by exploration.[^5] The UK Centre for Astrobiology works on life's limits, ancient biosignatures, subsurface environments, exoplanets and analogue research, including facilities at Boulby Mine.[^6] These programmes help connect observations to testable biological interpretations. A potentially habitable environment, an organic compound or an intriguing structure remains an intermediate result until competing physical and chemical explanations have been tested.
