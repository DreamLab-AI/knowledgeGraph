Space physiology is the study of how the human body responds and adapts to the altered gravity and other conditions of spaceflight.[^1][^2]

NASA describes muscles, bones, inner ear and organs as needing to adjust to the gravity of the station or spacecraft, and again to the Moon, Mars and Earth on return. ESA states that humans adapt remarkably well to microgravity, while adding that the adaptation involves complex short-term and long-term changes that can cause health problems.[^1][^2]

Unloading of the skeleton and muscles is the best documented example: ESA states that exposure to microgravity leads to bone and muscle loss. NASA adds that fluids shift upward to the head, which may put pressure on the eyes and cause vision problems, and describes exercise and other measures as countermeasures. The sources do not agree on a single rate of loss, so no figure is given here.[^3][^4]

Recovery after flight is less settled. ESA says it is not known whether lost bone fully regenerates, while NASA reports that fracture risk is not higher after return. Both statements are hedged by their sources and apply to the cases they describe.[^2][^4]
