Infrared astronomy is the study of astronomical objects through their infrared radiation, which has longer wavelengths than visible light.[^1]

NASA divides the infrared region into near-, mid- and far-infrared. It says many objects in the universe are too cool and faint to be detected in visible light but can be detected in the infrared, and that infrared waves can pass through dense regions of gas and dust with less scattering and absorption.[^1]

Space telescopes carry out much of this work. NASA describes Spitzer as using an ultra-sensitive infrared telescope to study asteroids, comets, planets and distant galaxies.[^2] NASA's astrophysics overview describes the James Webb Space Telescope as observing in near and mid-infrared light and SPHEREx as mapping the entire sky across optical and near-infrared wavelengths.[^3]

No universal wavelength boundary for each infrared band is defined in these sources, and reduced scattering is not complete transparency. Spitzer is described as a completed mission, so its archive and current observatories should be kept separate.
