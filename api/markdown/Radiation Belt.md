Two donut-shaped regions encircle Earth as the radiation belts, in which high-energy particles, mostly electrons and ions, are trapped by Earth's magnetic field.[^1]

NASA describes an inner and an outer belt at different distances above the equator. The source gives its distances in miles and does not establish that the boundaries are fixed, so they are better read as approximate and as varying with conditions.[^1]

Observations from the Van Allen Probes mission included the first data showing a transient third radiation belt, which can form during times of intense solar activity. The mission page does not give the altitude or formation mechanism of that belt, and it is described as transient rather than a permanent feature.[^2]

Trapped belt particles should be kept apart from solar radiation storm particles. NOAA describes the latter as charged particles, mainly protons, accelerated in the solar atmosphere by large magnetic eruptions, rather than as populations held in Earth's magnetic field.[^3]
