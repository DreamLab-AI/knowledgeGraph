Light time is the time a light or radio signal takes to travel between two bodies, such as Earth and a spacecraft.[^1]

NASA mission-operations usage distinguishes one-way light time from round-trip light time. One-way light time varies continuously as the spacecraft's distance from Earth changes, and round-trip light time is roughly, but not exactly, twice it, because the signal covers different distances on each leg while both bodies move.[^1]

In ephemeris computation, a light-time correction finds where a target was when the observed photons left it, and the vector from the observer to that position is called light-time corrected. NAIF notes that the correction depends on the target's motion relative to the solar system barycentre and not on the observer's velocity relative to it.[^2]

NAIF compares a single-iteration solution with a converged one and reports that the difference is often small, giving centimetre-level examples for two named Mars spacecraft. That finding is specific to those cases and should not be treated as a general bound.[^2]
