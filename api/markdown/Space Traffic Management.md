Space Traffic Management (STM) is the coordination of access to, operations in and return from space so that activities can be conducted safely and sustainably.

## Information, tracking and coordination

Space Situational Awareness (SSA) or Space Domain Awareness (SDA) describes the information and assessment needed to understand objects, activity and hazards in space. Space Surveillance and Tracking (SST) is a core technical function: sensors observe objects, a processing system associates observations with tracks, and orbit estimates support catalogues and warning services.[^1][^2]

STM uses those products within wider operating arrangements. It includes coordination between operators, information exchange, mission and debris planning, launch and in-orbit procedures, licensing, and end-of-life activity. A catalogue or conjunction warning supports decisions but does not assign universal right-of-way or resolve an operator conflict by itself.[^1][^2][^3]

## International governance

The UN COPUOS Long-term Sustainability guidelines cover subjects including orbital information sharing, conjunction assessment during controlled flight, pre-launch assessment, debris monitoring and national regulatory frameworks. The UN General Assembly encouraged States and intergovernmental organisations to implement them voluntarily where feasible and practicable.[^3] These guidelines provide agreed international guidance; they are not a binding global STM statute.

## UK services and regulation

The UK National Space Operations Centre (NSpOC) is jointly led by the UK Space Agency and UK Space Command, in partnership with the Met Office. Its civil space-protection work includes conjunction warnings, fragmentation monitoring, re-entry warning, space-weather advice and support for licence monitoring.[^4][^5] NSpOC supplies space-domain information and services; the CAA remains the licensing regulator.

The Monitor Space Hazards portal provides public monthly risk reports. Access to operational services is available to UK-licensed satellite operators and UK Government departments.[^6] NSpOC also supplies UK-licensed satellite-location data to support CAA monitoring.[^4]

The CAA licenses orbital operators under the Space Industry Act 2018 or Outer Space Act 1986 according to where the activity is carried out. Current guidance addresses safety, sustainability, responsible operations and disposal planning.[^7][^8] For UK-licensed launches, the CAA states that Launch Collision Avoidance Analysis (LCOLA) is carried out by the US 18th Space Defense Squadron under an operator sharing agreement with US Space Command; this launch screening is distinct from in-orbit conjunction services.[^9]

UK strategy connects SDA capability with traffic management and long-term access to the orbital environment.[^10] A 2023 consultation described planned UK Conjunction Data Messages and the policy position at that time. It is a historical snapshot; current CAA guidance takes precedence for present licensing requirements.[^11][^8]
