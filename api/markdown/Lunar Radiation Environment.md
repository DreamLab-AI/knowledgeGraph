Lunar radiation environment is the natural radiation environment on or in the vicinity of the Moon, made up of free space radiation, radiation produced by its interaction with the lunar surface, and radiation from naturally radioactive elements in the lunar crust.[^1]

In the Space Life Sciences Ontology it varies with location and time, for example with proximity to the surface, shielding by the Moon itself, the solar cycle and periodic solar particle events, and it can include non-ionising radiation such as visible light, but the term is often used for biologically damaging ionising radiation such as galactic cosmic radiation and solar particles.[^1]

Measurements near the Moon come from the CRaTER instrument on the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. An abstract from the CRaTER team reports that galactic cosmic rays account for most of the absorbed dose near the Moon, with a smaller share from secondary lunar species, mainly albedo protons.[^2] On the surface, the charged-particle environment consists of galactic cosmic rays, a small contribution from anomalous cosmic rays and sporadic solar energetic particles, with secondary albedo particles produced when cosmic rays interact with the regolith.[^3] A National Academies report also notes that secondary radiation, including energetic neutrons, may contribute significantly to the surface radiation environment.[^4]

NASA states that the Moon's weak atmosphere gives no protection from the Sun's radiation.[^5] The dose shares above apply to an instrument in lunar orbit and to one lander site and period, so they should not be read as the dose to a person on the surface.
