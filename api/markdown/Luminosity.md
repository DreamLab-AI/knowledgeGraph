Luminosity is the total amount of energy that a star or other celestial object emits per second, which is its power output.[^1][^2]

When the term is used without a qualifier it refers to emission across all wavelengths, which is called bolometric luminosity. A measurement limited to one band is a different quantity.[^2]

Luminosity is separate from apparent brightness, the amount of a star's energy that reaches a given area each second at the observer. Astronomers distinguish the star's total energy output from the energy that reaches an eye or a telescope, so apparent brightness depends on distance while luminosity does not.[^1]

Stellar luminosities are commonly expressed relative to the Sun, which the textbook illustrates with a comparison to a nearby bright star. If all stars had the same luminosity, differences in apparent brightness would indicate distance, but the sources note that stars differ, so brightness alone does not give distance.[^1]
