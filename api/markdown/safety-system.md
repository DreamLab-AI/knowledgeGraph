- ### Definition
  - A [[Safety System]] is a [[Control System]] architecture that implements [[Functional Safety]] requirements by continuously monitoring process variables, detecting dangerous states, and executing protective actions to bring a system to a defined safe state, certified against standards such as [[IEC 61508]] or [[ISO 26262]].

- ### Relationships
  - Safety systems implement [[Functional Safety]] principles and are certified under frameworks including [[IEC 61508]] for industrial machinery and [[ISO 26262]] for automotive applications. They rely on [[Monitoring System]] components, dedicated [[Safety PLC]] hardware with separated safety and control execution, and [[Embedded Systems]] running [[Real-Time Operating System]] kernels with deterministic response guarantees. In robotics and autonomous vehicles, [[Robot Safety]] constraints and [[Autonomous Vehicle]] perception requirements drive the safety system architecture, with certified sensor redundancy and actuator interlocks.

- ### Content
  - Safety systems arose from industrial process control in the chemical, nuclear, and oil and gas industries during the mid-twentieth century, motivated by catastrophic accidents (Flixborough 1974, Bhopal 1984, Chernobyl 1986) that revealed the inadequacy of unstructured risk management. IEC 61508 (1998, revised 2010) established the concept of the Safety Integrity Level as a probabilistic measure of a safety function's reliability, providing a quantitative framework for design, verification, and validation. Sector-specific standards derived from it include IEC 61511 (process industry), IEC 62061 (machinery), and ISO 26262 (automotive).

  - A safety system architecture is typically structured as a safety lifecycle: hazard and risk analysis identifies dangerous failure modes; safety requirements specify the required Safety Integrity Level and safe state behaviour; the hardware and software design implements redundant and diverse sensing, logic solving, and actuation; systematic verification (formal methods, FMEA, FTA) and functional testing validate correct operation. Safety PLCs execute safety logic in dedicated, isolated channels with periodic self-test and voted-input structures (1oo2, 2oo3) to detect and tolerate sensor and logic failures. Watchdog timers and heartbeat mechanisms detect software lock-up.

  - Safety systems protect against hazards in nuclear power plants (reactor scram systems), railways (automatic train protection), manufacturing robotics (collaborative robot speed and force limiting, safety-rated monitored stop), oil and gas processing (emergency shutdown), aviation (flight envelope protection), and automotive systems (ABS, electronic stability control, autonomous emergency braking). The IEC 61508 SIL 4 rating (highest) requires a probability of dangerous failure on demand below 10^-5 per hour for continuous-mode functions.

  - As of 2024-2025, the integration of AI and machine learning into safety-critical systems is the dominant challenge. ISO/PAS 8800 (2024) addresses AI safety in automotive systems, while IEC TC 65 is developing standards for AI-based safety functions. Formal verification of neural network safety properties using abstract interpretation and satisfiability solvers is an active research area. Functional safety certification for autonomous mobile robots and collaborative robots is accelerating under IEC 63327 and ISO 10218-2 revisions, and safety assurance cases for AI-augmented monitoring systems are being trialled by aerospace regulators.
