Ecliptic latitude is the angular distance of a position on the celestial sphere north or south of the ecliptic, the projection of the plane of the Earth's orbit on that sphere.[^1][^2]

It is the ecliptic counterpart of declination: one course text describes it as analogous to declination but measured from the ecliptic, reaching +90° at the north ecliptic pole and -90° at the south ecliptic pole.[^2] It is paired with ecliptic longitude, and a worked text relates the two systems through a spherical triangle, with the angle between the ecliptic and the equator called the obliquity of the ecliptic.[^1]

Textbook figures show latitude and longitude in a figure rather than defining them in words, so the verbal definition rests on the course page.[^1][^2]

Both the ecliptic and the equator move, so a coordinate system based on them has to carry a date.[^3] By convention the standard reference epoch is J2000.0, the mean equator and equinox of the year 2000.[^4] The sources do not say which epoch a particular ecliptic dataset uses, and they do not give a numerical obliquity.[^3][^4]
