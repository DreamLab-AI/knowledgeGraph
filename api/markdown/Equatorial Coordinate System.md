Equatorial coordinate systems locate a position on the celestial sphere by declination, its angular distance north or south of the extended equatorial plane, and right ascension, its angular distance measured eastward along the equator from a reference point.[^1][^2]

A reference point has traditionally been the equinox, the place where the Sun, in its yearly circuit, crosses the equatorial plane moving from south to north.[^1] It is called the first point of Aries, a name kept although the point now lies in Pisces.[^2]

Because the equator and the ecliptic are both moving planes, such a system has to be specified as the equator and equinox of some date, and J2000.0 is the conventional standard epoch.[^1][^3] The planes are idealisations, since the theories of the Earth's motion that define them are imperfect.[^1]

The ICRS is a different construction. Its axes do not depend on epoch, ecliptic or equator, although they were aligned close to the mean equator and dynamical equinox of J2000.0 for continuity.[^1] Equatorial coordinates of a given date are therefore not the same thing as coordinates in that system.[^1]
