Spaceflight cardiovascular adaptation is the set of changes in heart and blood vessel function that occur in microgravity, with acute responses that include raised preload and stroke volume and chronic changes that include cardiac atrophy, vascular remodelling and autonomic impairment.[^1]

A review in the same field links these adaptations to post-flight orthostatic intolerance, the difficulty of keeping blood pressure stable on standing after return to gravity.[^1] Another review attributes orthostatic hypotension on return to Earth to impaired arterial pressure control, and treats the head-ward movement of body fluid as the trigger for the adaptive changes.[^2]

NASA notes that crew members perform periodic fitness self-evaluations so that researchers can understand the decline in heart function that can occur during spaceflight.[^3] Such wording is hedged by the source, and none of the pages used here establishes the size or the permanence of that decline for any individual.

Cardiovascular adaptation in this sense is distinct from the cardiovascular risks attributed to space radiation, which a review of Mars mission risks treats as a separate hazard.[^2]
