An impact crater is a hole left in a planetary surface when an asteroid, meteoroid or comet strikes it.[^1][^2][^3]

NASA says the Moon’s sparse atmosphere does not impede impacts, and its craters have been preserved for billions of years, giving an impact history for the Moon and other bodies of the inner solar system. Most craters left by ancient impacts on Earth have been erased by erosion and other geological processes, and NASA states that about 190 impact craters are known on Earth.[^1][^2]

The Earth Impact Database at the University of New Brunswick collects images, publications and abstracts about confirmed impact structures. NASA cites the Barringer Meteorite Crater in Arizona, about 0.6 miles across, as one of the most intact craters. Geologists studied it from the 1890s but its status as an impact crater was not confirmed until 1960, which shows that recognising a crater and confirming its origin are separate steps.[^1][^4]

NASA describes the energy of an impact on Mercury as digging a hole and crushing rock, some of which is thrown out and forms bright rays that darken with time. NASA also reports that Mercury may hold water ice in deep craters in permanent shadow and that the Moon’s highest ice concentrations occur within darkened polar craters. The retained sources give no formal size classes such as simple or complex craters, and the crater count is a figure at retrieval that a database can change.[^2][^3]
