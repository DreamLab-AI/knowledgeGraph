A red dwarf is the smallest and coolest kind of main-sequence star, a fraction of the Sun’s size and mass.[^1]

NASA describes convection in these stars as bringing material from the core to the surface, where it cools and sinks back with a fresh supply of hydrogen. On that account a red dwarf can burn through its hydrogen over trillions of years without changing its internal structure, and scientists think the lowest-mass examples outlast the present age of the universe.[^1] A university textbook gives a main-sequence lifetime of some 200 billion years for a star of about 0.4 solar mass, which it notes is longer than the current age of the universe.[^2]

Red dwarfs are born in much greater numbers than more massive stars and live so long that NASA puts them at around 75% of the Milky Way’s stellar population. They are too faint to see with the unaided eye.[^1] That share is NASA’s approximate figure for one galaxy, not a count for the universe.

A red dwarf appears more orange than red.[^1] Spectral classification sorts stars by surface temperature and places the coolest of the seven standard classes at M, so the two ideas overlap but are not the same label.[^3] The sources used here do not give a mass or temperature boundary for the term, and the lifetimes quoted are theoretical expectations, since no star of this kind is described as having left the main sequence.[^1][^2]
