Drought monitoring follows an evolving shortage of water and its effects across the hydrological cycle. Low rainfall may first appear as meteorological drought, then reduce soil moisture, river flow, groundwater recharge, reservoir storage or ecosystem function. Agricultural, environmental and water-supply drought can overlap, develop at different rates and recover at different times. No single satellite image, gauge or index determines the whole condition.

## Measurements and indicators

The UK Water Resources Portal brings rainfall, river flow, soil moisture and groundwater data together from the Met Office, Environment Agency, Scottish Environment Protection Agency, COSMOS-UK and other national providers. It presents raw observations alongside standardised drought indices.[^1] Surface instruments measure particular sites or catchments; gridded rainfall and modelled soil-water products extend coverage but add interpolation and model assumptions.

Daily river flows for more than 900 catchments in England and Scotland appear on the portal at about two days' latency. Wales and Northern Ireland river-flow updates are monthly. Recent data can be unchecked, erroneous or revised after quality assurance, and the portal says provisional observations should not be used directly as the sole basis for decisions.[^2] A national display therefore does not make its inputs simultaneous or equally mature.

Standardised indices describe how unusual a variable is for a place and season. The Standardised Precipitation Index measures rainfall anomaly; SPEI also includes estimated atmospheric demand; SSI describes streamflow; and SGI describes groundwater level. The UK portal calculates several accumulation periods against a 1961-2010 reference.[^3] Short windows respond quickly, while long windows reveal persistent deficits. Indices can disagree because rainfall, soils, rivers and aquifers retain water for different lengths of time. Results also depend on the chosen reference and statistical distribution.

Earth observation contributes spatially consistent proxies. The European Drought Observatory's vegetation indicator uses satellite-measured fraction of absorbed photosynthetically active radiation to flag anomalous vegetation response. Its Combined Drought Indicator integrates precipitation, soil-moisture and vegetation anomalies at a scale of roughly 5 km; its other products extend to coarser scales.[^4] Vegetation stress can arise from heat, disease, management or other causes, so the satellite anomaly is not a direct measurement of crop loss. The combined product classifies hazard, while impact and risk also depend on exposure and vulnerability.

## Operational decisions and outlooks

The Environment Agency manages drought in England through area plans and coordination with water companies, government and other bodies. It considers rainfall, river levels and flows, groundwater, reservoir storage, environmental evidence, demand and observed impacts. Operational classifications include prolonged dry weather, drought, severe drought and recovery.[^5] Indicators and thresholds support expert judgement; they do not make an automatic declaration. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own responsible bodies and terminology.

The UK Hydrological Outlook supplies modelled trajectories for river flow roughly one to three months ahead, normally releasing a new forecast by the fifth working day of each month. It combines approaches based on persistence, historical analogues, ensemble streamflow prediction and meteorological inputs.[^6] The portal exposes ensemble probabilities and skill against climatology. Model bias remains possible, and a storage-deficit layer describes how much unusual rainfall might be needed to recover; it is explicitly not a drought forecast.

## A dated UK example

On 7 May 2025 the Environment Agency reported that February-April had been England's driest such period since 1956. Northern and central river and reservoir levels had fallen early, while groundwater supported more normal flows in parts of eastern and south-eastern England.[^7] The assessment illustrates how the same rainfall deficit can propagate differently by geology, storage and region.

The May 2025 UK Hydrological Summary then recorded drought status in north-west England on 29 May. It reported the lowest combined England and Wales reservoir stocks in a series beginning in 1990 and the lowest May soil moisture at more than half of COSMOS-UK sites.[^8] Those records use different periods: reservoir series, rainfall baselines and individual soil stations do not all begin in the same year. The report also marked seasonal outlook uncertainty and stated that its source hydrological data remained subject to revision.

A drought assessment should name its variable, reference period, accumulation window, issue date and geography. It should also say whether the evidence is observed, interpolated, satellite-retrieved, modelled or forecast. These distinctions preserve the path from a physical measurement to an operational status and prevent a regional anomaly from being mistaken for a local impact.
