Optical remote sensing is remote sensing carried out at visible and nearby infrared wavelengths, acquiring information about a surface without contact.[^1][^2]

The Canadian tutorial defines remote sensing as acquiring information about the Earth's surface without contact, by sensing and recording reflected or emitted energy. It describes the visible range as approximately 0.4 to 0.7 micrometres and treats the infrared as a neighbouring region.[^1][^2] The tutorial does not define optical remote sensing as a term, so its boundaries here follow the bands that optical instruments measure.

Reflected solar energy is available only while the sun illuminates the Earth, whereas naturally emitted energy such as thermal infrared can be detected by day or night if enough energy is emitted.[^3] The energy also interacts with the atmosphere on its way to the target and again on its way to the sensor.[^1]

Spaceborne examples include the Operational Land Imager on Landsats 8 and 9 and the Multi-Spectral Instrument on the Sentinel-2 satellites, each with its own stated repeat cycle and bands.[^4][^5] Those figures belong to the named missions.
