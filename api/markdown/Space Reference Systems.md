Space reference systems are the coordinate and timing conventions adopted so that the locations and motions of observers, natural objects and spacecraft can be identified consistently.[^1]

Observations are made from a rotating, orbiting Earth, so NASA's account stresses that rotation, precession, nutation, polar motion and revolution must be known, and that an epoch pins a coordinate system to a single snapshot in time.[^1] A terrestrial reference frame supplies coordinates of points on the Earth's surface, while a celestial frame is tied to stellar objects. The ITRF site says that Earth orientation parameters connect the two and that the IERS was created to maintain both.[^2]

The IERS Conventions separate a system, which has a theoretical definition, from its realisation as a frame, to which users have access.[^3] Timing conventions form the other half. Navipedia sets out how the rotational scale UT1, the atomic-based UTC and Terrestrial Time are related by stated offsets and by leap seconds.[^4]

These sources describe the conventions in general. They do not establish which frame or time scale a given spacecraft, navigation message or software library uses, and a coordinate or epoch is not fully specified until the system, frame realisation and time scale are named.[^3][^4]
