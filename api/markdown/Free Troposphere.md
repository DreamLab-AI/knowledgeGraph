Free troposphere is the part of the troposphere that lies above the planetary boundary layer, where air is no longer directly shaped by the Earth's surface.[^1][^2]

Princeton University Press adds the adjective free to distinguish this upper part from the boundary layer, which the troposphere also contains.[^1] Boundary layer is defined as the part of the troposphere directly influenced by the Earth's surface.[^2] Air in the boundary layer is turbulent, whereas the free troposphere is characterised by positive static stability, so buoyancy forces resist vertical motion.[^1][^2]

Both layers are coupled rather than sealed apart, because the daily deepening and shallowing of the boundary layer exerts an influence on the free troposphere.[^1] Its lower boundary is therefore not a fixed height, and the boundary layer depth is given only as varying considerably in space and time with a ballpark value of 1 km.[^1]

Retained sources do not establish how the free troposphere is observed or retrieved by satellite instruments, or a standard upper limit beyond the tropopause.
