Ultraviolet astronomy is the study of astronomical objects through their ultraviolet radiation, light with shorter wavelengths than visible light.[^1][^2]

Earth's atmosphere absorbs much of the high-energy ultraviolet radiation, so scientists use data from satellites above the atmosphere to sense ultraviolet coming from the Sun and other objects. Some ultraviolet light does reach sea level, so the absorption is partial and depends on wavelength.[^1][^2]

NASA describes astronomers as dividing the radiation into near, middle, far and extreme ultraviolet. Young stars shine most of their light at these wavelengths, which makes the band useful for studying star formation, and comparing ultraviolet with visible images lets astronomers learn about the structure and evolution of galaxies. The cited pages do not give wavelength limits for the subdivisions.[^1]

The Hubble Space Telescope detects a portion of the ultraviolet range together with visible and infrared light. That statement identifies one instrument and does not establish which other missions cover which ultraviolet bands, nor how sensitive any of them is.[^3]
