A thermal louver is a thermally activated shutter assembly that regulates how much heat a covered surface can dissipate by changing the surface's effective emission.[^1][^2]

As the louvers open, the average infrared emissivity of the surface changes, and so does the heat it dissipates.[^1] The blades are commonly positioned by bimetallic elements similar to those in a thermostat, which drive them open when internal temperatures are high and closed when they are low.[^2] The NASA guidebook describes a louver as a type of thermal switch actuated by a bimetallic spring.[^3]

NASA Science says louvers can reduce the electrical power used for heaters and help adapt to changes in the environment.[^2] NASA's small-spacecraft report says full-sized louvers on larger spacecraft are effective, while integration on small spacecraft is challenging because typical louvers carry greater mass and input power.[^1]

Louvers are commonly defined as active thermal control, yet the same report treats a CubeSat-adapted design as passive because it needs no spacecraft power, so classification depends on the design.[^1] The sources cited here give no performance figures for a particular louver.
