
Delta Differential One-way Ranging (Delta-DOR or ΔDOR) is a differential radio-tracking technique that constrains a spacecraft’s position in the plane of the sky.

Widely separated ground antennas observe a spacecraft signal and a nearby reference radio source, commonly a quasar, and compare the relative signal arrival delays. This VLBI-derived measurement contributes angular or sky-plane information that conventional line-of-sight range and Doppler do not provide as directly.[^1][^2][^7]

Delta-DOR supplements range, Doppler and trajectory modelling during deep-space navigation, particularly when accurate position knowledge is valuable near a planetary approach, flyby or orbit insertion. ESA’s Mars Express account describes it as a way to cross-check the three-dimensional position when ranging alone gives only one dimension.[^1][^3] It is an observation used in a navigation solution, not a standalone exact position fix.

Despite the expanded name, Delta-DOR is not ordinary two-way distance ranging. It uses differential one-way arrival-time observations across separated stations and reference-source geometry. Accuracy depends on station geometry, signal quality, timing, calibration and propagation effects.[^1][^4][^5]

CCSDS 506.0-M-2 is the opened current-issue Recommended Practice for Delta-DOR operations; its document control lists an editorial correction dated September 2024. CCSDS 506.2-R-1 is labelled a draft Red Book, so it can inform architecture discussion but must not be described as an approved standard.[^4][^5] Standards and available station equipment do not themselves guarantee a scheduled cross-agency service or a mission-specific accuracy.[^4][^6]
