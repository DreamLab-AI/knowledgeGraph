- ### Definition
  - An [[ASIC]] (Application-Specific Integrated Circuit) is a semiconductor chip whose logic is permanently optimised at the mask and transistor level for a specific computational workload, yielding peak efficiency in terms of operations per watt and unit cost at production volumes compared to reprogrammable alternatives such as [[FPGA]] or general-purpose [[GPU Compute]]. Because an ASIC cannot be reprogrammed post-fabrication, design decisions are irreversible; the circuit is taped out to a foundry (TSMC, Samsung) once the register-transfer-level design is finalised. ASICs underpin [[Hardware Acceleration]] for AI inference, cryptographic hashing in blockchain mining, and high-speed networking switch fabric.

- ### Relationships
  - ASICs enable [[Hardware Acceleration]], and when designed for AI workloads constitute the physical substrate for [[Neural Processing Unit]] and [[Inference Hardware]] products. They are contrasted with [[FPGA]], which offers reconfigurability at lower efficiency, and with [[GPU Compute]], which sacrifices per-watt efficiency for programmability and flexibility. ASICs are deployed within [[Embedded Systems]] and as discrete [[Hardware Component]] elements in larger system designs.

- ### Content
  - The ASIC industry emerged in the 1980s as design automation tools—hardware description languages (VHDL, Verilog) and electronic design automation (EDA) suites—democratised custom chip design beyond a handful of semiconductor giants. Gate array approaches allowed designers to customise pre-fabricated wafers with metal interconnect layers, reducing non-recurring engineering costs. The development of cell-based ASIC design, where designers compose standard cell libraries rather than laying out transistors manually, further lowered barriers to custom silicon.

  - ASIC development proceeds through RTL design (coding logic in VHDL or SystemVerilog), synthesis (converting RTL to gate-level netlist), place-and-route (mapping gates to foundry-specific physical layout), sign-off verification (timing, power, and design rule checks), and tape-out (submission of final GDSII layout files to the foundry). Non-recurring engineering (NRE) costs for advanced nodes (7nm, 5nm) can exceed tens of millions of pounds, making ASICs economically viable only at sufficient production volume or for applications requiring performance unachievable with programmable alternatives.

  - AI inference has become a major ASIC application domain. Google's Tensor Processing Unit (TPU) is a landmark ASIC designed specifically for matrix multiplication workloads central to neural network inference. Competitors include Cerebras's wafer-scale engine, Graphcore's Intelligence Processing Unit, and a generation of startup accelerators (Tenstorrent, Groq, SambaNova). In cryptocurrency, Bitcoin mining migrated entirely from CPU to GPU to FPGA to ASIC within a few years, as competitive mining dynamics rewarded the most efficient hashing hardware.

  - By 2024–2025, the AI ASIC market has become one of the fastest-growing segments of the semiconductor industry, driven by hyperscaler demand for custom inference and training accelerators. Amazon's Trainium and Inferentia, Microsoft's Maia, and Meta's MTIA chips reflect the trend of large technology companies designing proprietary ASICs to reduce dependence on Nvidia GPUs and optimise for their specific model workloads. Chiplet architectures—integrating multiple dies in a single package—are enabling ASIC designers to combine custom logic blocks with commodity IP, partially decoupling design complexity from foundry node requirements.
