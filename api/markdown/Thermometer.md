A thermometer is an instrument that indicates temperature from a property of its sensing element, most commonly the thermal expansion of a liquid or the change of electrical resistance with temperature.[^1][^2]

Liquid-in-glass types use the expansion of liquids such as mercury and alcohol on heating. NIST describes a move from mercury thermometers to electronic ones that detect the changing resistance of a metal, and advises keeping the enclosure away from buildings and pavement, which can make a reading artificially warm.[^2]

At Met Office synoptic stations air temperature is measured with platinum resistance thermometers, which relate temperature to the electrical resistance of a platinum wire. They sit in a Stevenson screen that shields them from direct sunlight and precipitation while letting air circulate, and liquid-in-glass thermometers are kept for check readings. The Met Office factsheet says the thermometer is calibrated at a fixed interval to give traceability to the national temperature standard.[^3][^4]

Radiometric thermometers operate in the infrared part of the spectrum and are used, among other applications, for temperature measurements from satellites, which is the main route by which the term meets Earth observation.[^1] A radiometric measurement is of emitted radiation, not of air temperature in a screen, and the retained sources do not give a conversion between them.
