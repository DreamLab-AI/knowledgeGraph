Ionosphere, the part of Earth's upper atmosphere ionised by solar extreme ultraviolet and X-ray radiation, creates a layer of electrons that reflects and modifies radio waves.[^1][^2]

Published vertical extents differ by source. NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center places it between 80 and about 600 km, NOAA JetStream gives 60 to 300 km, and ESA Navipedia extends it from about 60 km to well beyond the upper limit of either NOAA figure. These look like differences of definition rather than a measured conflict, and none is adopted here as a standard.[^1][^2][^3]

NOAA says spectral variability of the solar radiation and the density of atmospheric constituents create layers within the ionosphere called the D, E and F layers. JetStream adds that all three are denser by day, that the F layer splits into two layers in daytime and recombines at night, and that the D layer essentially disappears at night. Navipedia describes the underlying balance as ionisation by solar radiation during the day and recombination of electrons with ions at night, which reduces electron density.[^1][^2][^3]

Ionisation also follows solar activity. NOAA states that the photon flux at EUV and X-ray wavelengths varies by nearly a factor of ten over the 11 year solar cycle, and that flares, solar wind changes and geomagnetic storms also affect the ionosphere. Total electron content, the number of electrons along a radio path, together with signal frequency determines the delay a radio wave suffers. The retained sources do not give standard altitude boundaries between layers or profile values for individual conditions.[^1][^4]
