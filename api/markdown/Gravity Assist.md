A gravity assist is a planned close encounter in which a spacecraft’s trajectory changes under a moving body’s gravity, altering its velocity relative to a larger central body such as the Sun.[^1363][^1364]

## Planet-relative and heliocentric motion

In the ideal unpowered two-body flyby model, the spacecraft follows a hyperbola relative to the assisting planet. Far before and after closest approach, its inbound and outbound hyperbolic-excess velocity vectors have equal magnitudes but different directions. The planet-relative energy is unchanged; gravity bends the velocity vector.[^1362][^1363]

Because the planet is moving around the Sun, rotating the same planet-relative vector through the encounter changes the spacecraft’s heliocentric velocity, so heliocentric energy can increase or decrease according to the encounter geometry. Energy is exchanged with the planet and total system energy remains conserved. “Slingshot” is a useful image only if it does not imply energy appears without a source.[^1363][^1364]

A powered flyby adds a manoeuvre, commonly modelled as a periapsis impulse in initial design. It can change the inbound and outbound speed magnitudes as well as direction, but the actual burn has finite duration and must be checked against the spacecraft, body and trajectory constraints.[^1361][^1362]

## Patched-conic design and limits

Patched conics model the interplanetary legs as separate two-body arcs and match their boundary velocities to a planet-centred hyperbola at an encounter. This approach makes trajectory sequence and flyby geometry tractable during preliminary design. It is an approximation: there is no physical sharp boundary at a sphere of influence, and the method does not integrate all bodies’ gravity continuously through the encounter.[^1361][^1362]

A screening solution checks that the required periapsis clears the planet’s surface and atmosphere and that its turn angle is compatible with the inbound and outbound asymptotes. Flight design must then use body ephemerides, a higher-fidelity force model, navigation covariance, finite-burn effects and mission-specific thermal, structural and operational margins.[^1361][^1362]

## Mission evidence and status

Completed examples include Mariner 10’s Venus assist on its way to Mercury and Cassini’s Venus-Earth-Jupiter sequence to Saturn. Cassini’s Titan encounters also illustrate that gravity assists may raise or lower energy about the central planet, not merely propel a spacecraft outward from the Sun.[^1363][^1364][^1366]

As of 2 October 2026, ESA reported that BepiColombo had completed nine planetary flybys and separated its Mercury Transfer Module on 3 September. On 24 September its Mercury Planetary Orbiter performed an 11.4 m/s trajectory correction; Mercury orbit insertion remained planned for 21 November 2026. The UK Space Agency identifies UK hardware and propulsion contributions and University of Leicester leadership of the MIXS instrument. The case study’s last status update is January 2025, so its older “in cruise” wording must not override ESA’s current mission update.[^1367][^1368]
