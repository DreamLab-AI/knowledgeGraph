A supernova is the explosion of a star, produced either by the collapse of a massive star's core or by a runaway thermonuclear explosion in a degenerate stellar core.[^1][^2][^3]

NASA describes the core-collapse case in plain terms: the iron core collapses, rebounds, and sends a shock wave outward through the star, producing the explosion.[^1] A review of explosion mechanisms ties the energy of such events to the gravitational binding energy released in the collapse. In that review the neutrino-heating mechanism, aided by non-radial flows, drives explosions of some progenitors, but those explosions are low in energy, and the most energetic events may require magnetorotational driving.[^2]

Type Ia supernovae are treated separately. A review of their progenitors concludes that such an event must be the runaway thermonuclear explosion of a degenerate carbon-oxygen core, most likely a white dwarf, while stating that the specific progenitor systems and the processes that ignite them have not been identified.[^3]

Spectroscopic classes and explosion mechanisms do not map one to one. A review of supernova remnants notes that Type II events are core collapse, whereas Type I events can be either core collapse or thermonuclear.[^4] The mechanism claims above are therefore specific to the cases the sources cover, and none of them is presented as a settled account of every supernova.[^2][^3]
