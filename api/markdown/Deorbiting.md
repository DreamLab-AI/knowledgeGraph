Deorbiting is the deliberate or naturally produced lowering of an orbit so atmospheric drag eventually brings an object into re-entry.[^nasa-deorbit-systems]

Deorbiting is one disposal route. End-of-life disposal can instead include moving an object to a graveyard orbit, direct retrieval or another planned removal method. Operators should distinguish a powered, controlled re-entry from natural orbital decay: a commanded manoeuvre can shape the entry trajectory, while natural decay gives less control over event time and location.[^nasa-deorbit-systems][^uk-launch-return-guidance]

## Design and operational planning

Disposal planning starts during mission design because the chosen method depends on propulsion, attitude control, power, spacecraft health, tracking and the orbit. The plan should name who controls the spacecraft during disposal, the required subsystems, the phase-start conditions, failure responses and the parameters that trigger review. Passivation reduces stored energy that could cause an accidental breakup; collision risk also needs consideration while the object remains in orbit.[^iadc-debris-guidelines][^uk-caa-eol]

Disposal deadlines and success targets depend on the policy and licence that apply. ESA’s policy for new ESA missions requires safe disposal success probability above 90% and limits residence in protected LEO to five years after end of life. Those are ESA mission requirements, not a universal rule for every operator. IADC guidance sets an international mitigation framework, but it is not itself a worldwide licensing law.[^esa-debris-mitigation][^iadc-debris-guidelines]

## UK licensing context

UK operators must identify the applicable licence under the Space Industry Act 2018 or the Outer Space Act 1986. CAA guidance requires an end-of-life plan for orbital operator licence applications submitted after 31 January 2026; it does not apply to licences granted before that date. The plan must remain current during operations, and licensees must report the start and outcome of disposal. Earlier licences may have different conditions, so the licence itself and current CAA guidance determine the operator’s duties.[^uk-orbital-licensing][^uk-caa-eol][^caa-cap2210]

UK launch and return guidance separately describes disposal of launch vehicle orbital stages, including controlled and uncontrolled re-entry and the need to assess fragment survivability and public risk. Its scope should not be substituted for current orbital-operator licence terms.[^uk-launch-return-guidance]

## Operational examples

ESA lowered ERS-2’s orbit after its 2011 end-of-mission operations, reducing its estimated orbital lifetime from more than 200 years to less than 15; natural decay led to re-entry in February 2024. Aeolus, which was not designed for controlled disposal, received a series of assisted manoeuvres in July 2023 that lowered its orbit and targeted a safer final track. Both cases show that end-of-life actions depend on spacecraft capability and the policy context in which each mission was designed.[^esa-debris-mitigation][^esa-aeolus]

ESA’s Integral spacecraft is a different status case: its active science phase ended in March 2025, and ESA reports that it remains monitored for planned re-entry in 2029 after earlier orbit-adjustment burns. That event remains planned, not completed.[^esa-integral]
