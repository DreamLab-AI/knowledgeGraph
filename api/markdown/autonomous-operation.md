- ### Definition
  - [[Autonomous Operation]] describes the condition in which a system — whether a robot, industrial facility, software platform, or AI agent — continues to fulfil its designated function over extended periods without human operators initiating each action or making routine control decisions. It encompasses the four self-* capabilities articulated in IBM's autonomic computing vision (2001): self-configuration (adapting to environmental changes), self-healing (detecting and recovering from faults), self-optimisation (tuning performance), and self-protection (defending against threats). The scope of autonomy is typically bounded by a defined operational design domain and a set of conditions under which human escalation is required.

- ### Relationships
  - Autonomous Operation requires [[Autonomous Behavior]] as its action-selection substrate and [[Feedback Control]] loops to maintain stable performance. It enables concrete system types including [[Autonomous Agent]] software, [[Autonomous Mobile Robots]] hardware platforms, and [[Agentic Workflow]] software systems. It is contextualised within the broader category of [[Agentic AI]], governed by [[Functional Safety]] standards, and monitored through [[Monitoring System]] infrastructure that detects deviations requiring human attention.

- ### Content
  - The concept of autonomous operation emerged from industrial process control in the mid-20th century, where chemical plants, power stations, and manufacturing processes were progressively automated using programmable controllers and distributed control systems (DCS). The goal was not simply to eliminate manual labour but to achieve tighter control precision, faster fault response, and 24/7 operation without fatigue-related degradation. The aviation industry developed autopilot systems that could maintain cruise flight, altitude, and heading autonomously, with pilots providing supervisory oversight and handling edge cases.

  - The technical enablers of autonomous operation form a hierarchy. Sensing and situation awareness provides the system with real-time knowledge of its own state and environment. Decision logic — rule-based, model-based, or learned — determines appropriate responses. Actuation executes decisions. The feedback loop validates that actions achieved their intended effect and triggers re-planning if not. Fault detection and isolation (FDI) algorithms identify abnormal conditions; fault-tolerant control designs ensure graceful degradation rather than catastrophic failure. In software systems, self-healing mechanisms include circuit breakers, retry policies, auto-scaling, and blue-green deployment patterns.

  - Autonomous operation is deployed across critical infrastructure: power grids balance supply and demand autonomously through energy management systems; telecommunications networks reroute traffic around failures using OSPF and BGP; cloud computing platforms auto-scale workloads in response to demand signals; oil-and-gas facilities use advanced process control to optimise yield without constant operator adjustment. In the military domain, autonomous operation of unmanned air systems over contested airspace is an area of active development and intense ethical debate. Space exploration relies on autonomous operation for robotic missions where communication delays preclude real-time human control.

  - In 2024–2025, the scope of autonomous operation is expanding dramatically due to large language model-driven agents capable of multi-step reasoning and tool use. Agentic AI systems can now autonomously browse the web, write and execute code, manage files, and interact with APIs over hours-long task horizons. This shift raises new governance questions distinct from traditional industrial automation: how to specify, monitor, and constrain the objectives of AI agents operating at cognitive rather than physical levels of abstraction. Regulatory frameworks such as the EU AI Act attempt to classify autonomous AI systems by risk level and mandate human-override capabilities for high-stakes deployments.
