Beyond Low Earth Orbit (BLEO) spaceflight is a mission or trajectory phase conducted outside the near-Earth orbital region conventionally called LEO.[^1438][^1447][^1452]

BLEO is a broad mission label rather than a single orbit or precise altitude boundary. It can include high Earth orbit, cislunar transfer, lunar orbit or surface activity, and interplanetary flight. A spacecraft can leave LEO while remaining bound to Earth; “beyond LEO” does not by itself mean escape, a planetary encounter or landing. A mission can also begin in Earth orbit and continue beyond it.[^1438][^1447][^1454]

Both crewed and robotic missions fit the category. Apollo's lunar flights are historical crewed examples. Artemis I was an uncrewed integrated test that sent Orion from Earth orbit to lunar distance and returned it in 2022. BioSentinel is an uncrewed CubeSat that made a lunar flyby and continued into a heliocentric orbit. The Artemis II press kit was issued before flight in January 2026, so it supports mission architecture rather than later flight status; check current agency reporting before describing a crewed mission as completed.[^1444][^1448][^1449][^1454][^1455]

Distance and destination change the operating environment. Outside Earth's magnetic protection, crews and equipment encounter greater exposure to galactic cosmic rays and variable solar-particle events. Communication lag can reduce real-time ground support, increase crew autonomy and delay medical return. Those conditions differ between high Earth orbit, cislunar flight, the lunar surface and a Mars transfer, so BLEO is not one uniform environment.[^1444][^1445][^1446]

Deep-space missions use networks such as NASA's Deep Space Network for tracking, navigation and communications at the Moon and beyond. Lunar far-side activity may require relay spacecraft because the Moon blocks a direct radio path to Earth. Network choice and performance depend on geometry, antenna capability, relay availability and mission link budgets.[^1447][^1448]

Return is also mission-specific. Orion's Artemis I lunar-return trajectory entered Earth's atmosphere at about 25,000 mph and exposed its heat shield to temperatures near 5,000°F. Those values describe Orion's lunar return, not a generic BLEO or LEO return. The CAA's orbital operator licence can cover outer-space activities beyond Earth orbit. UK-licensed missions with planned encounters with planetary bodies and contamination risk also fall within the UK's planetary-protection framework, assessed by the CAA.[^1435][^1452][^1454][^1455]
