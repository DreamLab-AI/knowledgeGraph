Spaceflight circadian rhythm is the timing of the roughly daily biological cycles of an astronaut, including sleep and wakefulness, as it is altered by the light and dark cycles, isolation and workload of a mission.[^1]

NASA states that astronaut internal clocks might be altered by different dark and light cycles, a small and noisy environment, the stress of prolonged isolation and confinement and, for Mars, an extended day. It also reports that light-emitting diode lighting on the International Space Station is used to help align circadian rhythms and to improve sleep, alertness and performance.[^1]

A review of Mars mission risks cites in-flight studies documenting reductions in sleep duration. It also reports a simulated Mars mission in an isolation chamber in which some crew showed dysregulation of circadian entrainment and sleep difficulties. That result comes from a six-person analogue crew and does not by itself show the same effect in orbit.[^2]

Another review lists disrupted circadian rhythms among factors that, together with isolation, confinement, microgravity and radiation, shape the overall response of a space-exposed organism. The sources used here do not separate the circadian contribution from those other factors.[^3]
