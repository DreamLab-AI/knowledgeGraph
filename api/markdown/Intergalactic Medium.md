Intergalactic medium is the gas and plasma found in the space between galaxies.[^1][^2]

It is very thin, and although it can be extremely hot it is dimmer than the stars and other light-emitting matter inside galaxies, which makes it difficult to detect. Astronomers study it through X-ray emission and through the X-rays it absorbs when it is backlit by jets from supermassive black holes.[^1][^2]

Much of the medium is hydrogen and helium left over from the big bang. Elements made in stars have been added over cosmic history, carried out of galaxies by supernovae and by other processes such as star formation, black holes and galaxy interactions.[^1][^2]

Theory places a large part of the ordinary matter of the universe between galaxies in a warm-hot phase of the medium. The cited pages present this as an expectation that observers are testing, so they do not establish how much of that gas has been detected. They also describe temperature in broad terms rather than as one value for every region.[^2]
