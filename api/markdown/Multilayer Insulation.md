Multilayer insulation (MLI) limits radiative heat exchange between spacecraft hardware and its surroundings. A blanket stacks thin, low-emittance reflective foils with low-conductance spacer material. The reflective surfaces reduce layer-to-layer radiation; spacers keep adjacent foils apart and restrict solid conduction.[^1][^3]

MLI can reduce external heat gain in sunlight or near a hot planet and reduce heat loss during eclipse or cruise. It does not create or reject heat. A radiator is deliberately exposed through a high-emittance surface, so covering required radiator area with MLI would obstruct its function unless the design provides a controlled opening.[^1][^7]

## Thermal performance

Blanket performance is commonly represented by effective emittance or by an area conductance over stated boundary temperatures. Neither is a universal material constant. Layer material and coating, layer count and density, mean temperature, residual gas pressure, compression, spacer construction and contact with the protected surface all change heat flow.[^3]

Adding layers eventually gives little benefit and can make performance worse when packing increases contact. Residual gas adds conduction if vacuum is inadequate. Before complete depressurisation, gas trapped between layers can also load or distort the blanket. Small spacecraft face an acute geometry penalty because edges and attachments occupy a larger fraction of the available area; NASA describes MLI edge “shorts” as a general problem and notes that CubeSats may lack clearance for useful blankets.[^2]

## Installed blanket effects

A flight blanket contains seams, overlaps, cut-outs, vent paths, grounding points, fasteners, wiring and access openings. These create parallel conduction and direct radiation paths absent from an ideal flat coupon. ESA reports that one large suspended blanket performs better than several small blankets over the same area and that real systems with seams and gaps differ from samples of the same lay-up.[^3] NASA seam tests on 20- and 50-layer blankets likewise required corrections for heat carried by instrumentation wires and reported measurement uncertainty.[^4]

Workmanship is therefore part of the thermal design. Local compression, wrinkles, torn foils, reversed outer layers, conductive stitching, poorly placed fasteners and unplanned contact can degrade the blanket. Contamination and handling can change exposed optical properties. Penetrations must preserve sensor and instrument fields of view, thruster clearance, grounding, cleanliness and maintainability.

Mounting also has to survive launch and vent without tearing, ballooning or shifting into mechanisms. ESA's Nebula activity treats attachment, launch load, depressurisation, thermal performance, access and fields of view as one development problem; its stated breadboard and test work is development evidence rather than universal flight qualification.[^6]

## Analysis and qualification

A thermal model should represent the installed zones and major penetrations instead of applying one coupon value to every covered face. Hot and cold cases need the correct external finish, orientation and beginning- or end-of-life optical properties. Test support, chamber pressure, shroud temperature, wiring and instrumentation must be included in the uncertainty and model correlation.[^3][^4]

Thermal-balance or thermal-vacuum testing checks the integrated blanket with representative boundaries. RAL Space lists UK chambers from component to spacecraft scale and an outgassing facility working to ECSS-Q-70-02C, but facility capability is distinct from qualification of any particular blanket.[^8]

BepiColombo supplies a mission-specific example: ESA describes stacked MLI blankets within a thermal architecture that also uses radiator shielding and heat pipes for the Mercury environment.[^5] MetOp uses silvered fluorinated ethylene propylene on radiator areas and MLI on other faces, showing the functional separation between rejection and insulation.[^7] Neither layout is a generic prescription; orbit, attitude, allowable temperature and internal dissipation govern the partition.
