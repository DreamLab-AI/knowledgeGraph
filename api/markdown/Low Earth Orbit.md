Low Earth orbit (LEO) is the family of Earth-centred orbits closest to the planet. NASA and ESA commonly use an upper altitude of 2,000 km.[^1][^2] This is a practical classification boundary rather than a discontinuity in orbital physics. Any dataset or rule should state how it treats an eccentric orbit that crosses the boundary.

## Boundaries and motion

ESA's 2026 space-environment statistics classify an object as LEO when both its perigee and apogee are between 0 and 2,000 km altitude.[^3] Another catalogue might use mean altitude, semi-major axis or instantaneous position. Those alternatives can assign the same eccentric trajectory to different regimes. Altitude also requires an Earth-radius or reference-surface convention; it is not itself a complete orbital coordinate.

There is no fixed physical lower edge. The atmosphere becomes thinner with height but does not end abruptly. ESA notes that operational satellites generally do not fly below about 180 km because drag becomes severe.[^1] The practical limit varies with solar activity, atmosphere, spacecraft area-to-mass ratio, attitude, propulsion and intended lifetime. The value should not be turned into a definitional boundary.

A circular spacecraft near crewed-spaceflight altitudes travels at roughly 7.8 km/s and completes an orbit in about 90 minutes.[^1] These are representative values, not constants for LEO. In a two-body model, period is controlled by semi-major axis. An eccentric orbit therefore has one orbital period even though its instantaneous altitude and speed change around the path.

## Inclination, coverage and perturbations

LEO permits equatorial, inclined, polar and retrograde orbital planes. Polar orbit and Sun-synchronous orbit are important subclasses or overlapping design families, but neither is synonymous with LEO.[^1] Earth observation benefits from short range to the surface, while communications constellations use many moving spacecraft to provide continuing coverage that one low satellite cannot supply.

Atmospheric drag is a defining operational constraint. It changes semi-major axis and period and can lead to re-entry. Density forecasts depend on solar and geomagnetic conditions, while drag acceleration also depends on attitude and ballistic properties. Propagation tools consequently require an explicit atmosphere and drag model where those effects matter.[^4] Lifetime and re-entry dates should be reported as uncertain predictions, with model and space-weather assumptions, rather than exact appointments.

## Classification and provenance

LEO describes a regime, not a quality or mission type. It does not imply a particular resolution, revisit time, latency, radiation dose or disposal outcome. A useful record includes perigee and apogee or semi-major axis and eccentricity, inclination, epoch, frame and time scale, altitude convention, propagation theory and the authority's regime rule. For a decaying orbit, the classification can change with epoch.
