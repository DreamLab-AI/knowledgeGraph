Emissivity is the ratio of the radiance a surface actually emits to the radiance a black body would emit at the same thermodynamic temperature, and Earth observation instruments sensitive to thermal infrared radiation are designed to calculate it.[^1][^2]

The ECOSTRESS algorithm document defines emissivity for an isothermal, homogeneous emitter as that ratio.[^1] NASA Terra explains that the strength of the energy a surface emits depends on both its temperature and how efficiently it can emit radiation, known as its emissivity.[^2]

Emissivity is treated as an intrinsic property of the surface, so unlike surface temperature it does not depend on the angle of the sun or on local weather conditions.[^2] Because radiance depends on both temperature and emissivity, separating them is an underdetermined problem: for the five ECOSTRESS bands there are five measurements and six unknowns.[^1]

NASA Terra states that the emissivity of most natural Earth surfaces ranges between approximately 0.6 and 1.0, that vegetation, water and ice are above 0.95 in the thermal infrared, and that values below 0.85 are typically restricted to deserts and semi-arid areas.[^2] These figures are NASA Terra's summary for the thermal infrared. The retained sources do not establish values for other wavelength ranges, nor how the quantity is stored in a particular product level.
