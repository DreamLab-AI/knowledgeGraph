A life support system is the set of equipment that provides or controls the conditions a crew needs to live in a spacecraft, including atmospheric pressure, oxygen levels, ventilation, fire detection and suppression, waste management and water supply.[^1]

NASA describes the International Space Station system as three key components: a water recovery system, an air revitalisation system and an oxygen generation system.[^1] The water recovery system reclaims wastewater, cabin humidity condensate and water from suit hydration systems, and checks purity before reuse. The air revitalisation system removes trace contaminants and carbon dioxide from cabin air, and the oxygen generation system electrolyses water to produce oxygen for the cabin.[^1]

That description applies to one station at a stated date, and other vehicles may divide or configure the functions differently. The NASA page also states a recovery rate for station water, which is a figure for that system and should not be read as a general property of life support.[^1]

Life support is not the same as radiation protection. Radiation monitoring for crew is described by NASA as the work of a separate group at the Johnson Space Center, using dosimeters and environmental monitoring, so the two concerns are handled by different functions even when both serve crew safety.[^2] The sources do not describe how the two are integrated in any given vehicle design.
