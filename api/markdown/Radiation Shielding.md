Radiation shielding is the use of material around spacecraft electronics and other components to absorb part of the particle radiation that would otherwise reach them.[^1][^2]

Barth, LaBel and Poivey state that shielding material can reduce total ionizing dose by absorbing most electrons and lower energy protons. Shielding effectiveness decreases as thickness increases, because higher energy protons are difficult to slow down. Dose is commonly expressed as a function of aluminium shield thickness, and a solid sphere geometry gives an upper bound for the dose inside an actual spacecraft.[^1]

Shielding is less effective against other effects. Barth's work reports limited effectiveness against displacement damage, and a low-Earth-orbit example in which adding shielding had little effect on high-energy trapped protons; examples from 2004 should not be read as design values.[^1] NASA Goddard test compendia list shielding as one option, with other methods, for mitigating total ionizing dose and displacement damage effects in flight parts.[^2] Shielding thicknesses and materials suitable for a given orbit come from launch provider guidance, and shielding is one input to hardness assurance rather than a substitute for part selection and testing.
