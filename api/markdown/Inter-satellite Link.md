An inter-satellite link is a direct communication path between spacecraft. It may use an optical carrier or a radio-frequency band, and it can connect satellites in the same orbital regime or bridge different orbits. The term describes the physical link; routing, storage and delivery to an end user belong to the wider network.

## Relay and crosslink operation

A crosslink can move observations or communications traffic towards a spacecraft that has ground visibility. This reduces the need for the source spacecraft to wait for its next direct ground-station pass, although total latency still includes contact scheduling, routing, relay capacity and terrestrial delivery.

Terminals need mutual line of sight, compatible waveforms and sufficient link margin. Optical terminals also need fine acquisition, pointing and tracking. Radio-frequency links generally tolerate wider beams but face their own antenna, spectrum and interference constraints. Network design must account for changing range, relative motion, occultation, handovers and possible loss of a relay node.

Europe's European Data Relay System is a deployed example. Its geostationary nodes exchange data with compatible lower-orbit spacecraft through two-way laser terminals, then relay the traffic to Earth by radio. ESA gives an optical inter-satellite capacity of up to 1.8 Gbit/s, while EDRS-A also carries a Ka-band inter-satellite terminal.[^1] EDRS began serving the Copernicus programme in 2016, providing operational rather than merely experimental evidence for this architecture.[^2] The quoted rates are system limits, not guaranteed throughput for every user.

## UK activity and future systems

In March 2026, the UK Space Agency reported the launch of a Spire Global UK 6U spacecraft carrying an optical inter-satellite-link payload. Its purpose is to test compact high-speed laser crosslinks; the announcement says the intended benefits follow once the technology is validated.[^3] Launch therefore demonstrates that the test mission reached orbit, not that an in-orbit crosslink had already succeeded.

ESA's HydRON programme aims to demonstrate an interoperable, multi-orbit optical network with rates in the terabit-per-second range.[^4] It remains a development programme. UK-supported lunar work provides a radio example: Lunar Pathfinder is planned to receive S-band and UHF communications from lunar assets and relay them to Earth in X-band.[^5] Its published launch schedule is volatile, so the architecture should be separated from current mission status.
