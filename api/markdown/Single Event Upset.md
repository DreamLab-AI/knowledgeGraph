A single event upset is a non-destructive single event effect, a disturbance to circuit operation caused by a single ion passing through or near a sensitive node.[^1]

NASA's tutorial lists the upset beside the multiple-bit upset, the transient and the functional interrupt as non-destructive effects, in contrast to latchup, burnout and gate rupture. It records that single event upsets were first postulated in 1962, that satellite anomalies from upsets in flip-flops were reported in 1975, and that upsets were first observed on Earth in 1978 in memory, caused by alpha particles released from uranium and thorium contaminants in packaging material.[^1] A NASA presentation treats soft errors as the terrestrial version of single event upsets.[^2]

Upset consequences depend on where the disturbance lands. One presentation gives the example of an upset in a control register changing the operational mode of a device.[^2] In testing, susceptibility is characterised by a cross section, the number of errors divided by the particle fluence, which is calculated at several values of linear energy transfer.[^3]

A cross section is a test-characterisation quantity and not an in-orbit upset rate. The primer adds that most upset testing of modern complex devices is geared to application-specific information, and that interpreting the data for other applications is challenging.[^3] The retained sources do not describe error-correction or other mitigation, and this page makes no claim about them.
