A supergiant star is a very luminous, evolved star of high initial mass, a class that includes the red supergiants identified as progenitors of some supernovae.[^1][^2]

Observational work on supernova progenitors has directly detected red supergiants as the stars that exploded in Type II-P events. The review of that work puts the minimum initial mass able to produce a supernova near eight solar masses and states that the value depends on the stellar models used.[^1] Other massive progenitor types are discussed there too, including Wolf-Rayet stars for broad-lined Type Ic events, so a supergiant is one stage or type among several routes to a supernova and not a synonym for a supernova progenitor.[^1]

NASA describes one variable supergiant in Camelopardalis as a star that will eventually end its life in a supernova and is likely to leave a black hole behind. That caption concerns a single named star and is not a rule for every supergiant.[^2] For core-collapse events in general, the energy of the explosion is linked to the gravitational binding energy released when the stellar core collapses, although a review of explosion mechanisms reports that the modelling is not settled.[^3]

None of the sources opened here gives a spectral-type or luminosity-class definition of a supergiant, so this page leaves those boundaries to the stellar-classification pages.[^1][^2]
