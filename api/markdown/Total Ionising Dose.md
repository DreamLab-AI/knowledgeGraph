Total ionising dose is the cumulative long-term damage to electronics caused by solar and trapped protons and trapped electrons.[^1]

In microelectronics, the Barth, LaBel and Poivey paper states that this damage causes threshold shifts, leakage current and timing skews. The effect first appears as parametric degradation of a device and can ultimately result in functional failure. NASA lists it alongside single-event effects and total non-ionising dose as radiation effects on electronics, and treats them through radiation hardness assurance, an iterative process that assesses radiation threats and develops mitigation at hardware, software and operational levels.[^1][^2]

Shielding material absorbing most electrons and lower-energy protons can reduce the dose, with diminishing effectiveness as shielding is increased. Testing involves exposing a device to an ionising radiation environment while measuring its electrical performance under varied operating conditions. A NASA small-spacecraft report adds that monocoque structures increase surface area to potentially reduce the dose, which is a hedged design benefit rather than a measured one.[^1][^3]

Dose is a cumulative quantity and differs from single-event effects, which are caused by individual particles. The sources retained here give no dose limits or part ratings, so any tolerance figure for a part or mission needs its own test data and mission environment.[^1][^2]
