A star cluster is a group of stars that share an origin and are held together by gravity for some length of time.[^1][^2]

Two broad categories are described. Open clusters are loosely bound groups that typically contain tens to thousands of stars, and they tend to disperse as they orbit their galaxy. Globular clusters hold far more stars in a roughly spherical, tightly bound system and are described as lasting for billions of years.[^1][^2] The sources give typical ranges for membership, size and age, which should be read as descriptions of typical objects and not as sharp boundaries.

Members of a cluster formed from the same cloud of gas and dust, and NASA describes molecular clouds full of newly formed clusters as stellar nurseries.[^3] Theoretical work treats cluster formation in dense, hierarchically collapsing gas clouds and follows the cluster through to its eventual dispersion.[^4]

A shared origin does not mean a group is still bound. NASA distinguishes clusters from stellar associations, whose stars formed together and still move together but have become gravitationally unbound.[^1] The sources cited here do not set a single numerical threshold for when a group counts as a cluster.
