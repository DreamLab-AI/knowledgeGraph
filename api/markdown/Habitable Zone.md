Habitability depends on distance from a star at which liquid water could exist on the surfaces of orbiting planets; this distance range is the habitable zone.[^1]

NASA says every star has such a zone and that its position depends on the size and brightness of the star. Zones potentially able to host life-bearing planets are wider for hotter stars, whereas red dwarfs have tighter zones close to the star. Planets in a red dwarf zone are exposed to extreme levels of X-ray and ultraviolet radiation, and powerful flares could sterilise closely orbiting planets.[^1]

Climate models determine the boundaries. Kopparapu and co-authors updated an earlier one-dimensional model and obtained water loss and maximum greenhouse limits for the Solar System at 0.99 and 1.70 astronomical units, which places the present Earth near the inner edge. They propose using stellar flux incident on a planet rather than equilibrium temperature. Their model omits the radiative effects of clouds, so they say the actual boundaries may extend further in both directions.[^2]

A position inside the zone is a condition on orbital distance and stellar energy. NASA frames it in terms of what could exist and what conditions might be right, and neither retained source treats it as evidence that a planet is habitable or inhabited. NASA adds that large gaseous worlds such as Jupiter seem far less likely to offer habitable conditions. The sources do not address habitability of subsurface oceans away from the zone.[^1][^2]

[^1]: NASA Science, “The Habitable Zone”. https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/habitable-zone/
[^2]: Kopparapu et al. (arXiv), “Habitable Zones Around Main-Sequence Stars: New Estimates”. https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.6674
