A sea ice lead is a linear opening in sea ice that forms where floes diverge or shear apart, exposing a surface layer of water.[^1][^2] SWEET describes it as a surface layer of a water body formed when surface ice fractures and moves apart, and NSIDC calls leads narrow, linear cracks.[^1][^2]

NASA Earth Observatory notes that as pack ice drifts, leads continually open and close between ice floes.[^3] NSIDC gives a width that varies from a couple of metres to over a kilometre, and SWEET adds that leads are generally wide enough for navigation by surface vessels.[^2][^1] The term is generally applied to linear features, and SWEET says that a very large open area may be called a polynya although the usage of the two terms is under debate.[^1] NASA describes polynyas as persistent openings maintained by strong winds or ocean currents.[^3]

Open water in a lead is much darker than the surrounding ice, so in summer it absorbs more solar energy, while an open winter lead releases heat and moisture to the atmosphere.[^2] NASA reports that the positive phase of the Arctic Oscillation increases the size of leads in the ice pack.[^3]

Neither a width threshold separating a lead from a polynya nor a satellite detection method appears in the retained sources.
