Electromagnetic compatibility testing is the verification of space hardware against limits on the electromagnetic interference it emits and on its susceptibility to emissions from itself or other sources.[^1]

A NASA Goddard guideline sets design and test requirements for payloads, subsystems and components and organises verification into conducted emissions, conducted susceptibility, radiated emissions and radiated susceptibility. It states that most of its requirements are taken from the widely used MIL-STD 461, 462 and 463 EMC specifications and tailored for spaceflight.[^1]

Test facility choice matters as well. A 1992 Goddard paper on upgrading an EMC test chamber reports that the enclosure was to absorb internally reflected radio-frequency energy and reduce external power-line noise.[^2]

Both sources are NASA Goddard documents, one a Shuttle-era guideline and one a facility paper, so they show practice at one centre at a particular time. They do not establish current requirement values, and later revisions of the referenced military standards are outside what they cover.[^1][^2]

[^2]: Dyer, NASA Goddard, *Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) standard test chamber upgrade requirements for spacecraft design verification tests*, AIAA Paper 92-1175, February 1992. https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19920050669
