Urban heat monitoring is the measurement of how much warmer built-up areas are than their natural surroundings, using surface temperatures from satellites and aircraft and air temperatures from ground stations.[^1][^2]

Urban heat islands arise because buildings, roads and other infrastructure absorb and re-emit the sun's heat more than natural landscapes such as forests and water bodies.[^1] Surface and air temperatures behave differently: surface temperatures vary more than atmospheric air temperatures during the day, but they are generally similar at night.[^1] A monitoring design therefore has to say which of the two it measures.[^1]

EPA explains that instruments mounted on satellites and aircraft can provide better geographic coverage than those used for recording air temperatures.[^2] Satellite data are collected only when a satellite passes over a city and are available only for clear weather conditions.[^2] EPA therefore recommends combining satellite surface temperatures with data from monitoring stations or traverses for air temperatures to obtain the most complete picture of a city's heat island.[^2]

Both retained sources come from the US Environmental Protection Agency and describe United States practice, so the retained sources do not establish how other national agencies define or report urban heat. No temperature differences are carried into this page because none was checked against an independent source.[^1][^2]
