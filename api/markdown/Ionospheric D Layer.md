Ionospheric D layer, the lower, denser region of the ionosphere, is present mainly in daylight and essentially absent at night.[^1][^2]

NOAA states that X-ray and extreme ultraviolet radiation from a solar flare ionises the lower layers of the ionosphere on the sunlit side of Earth. High frequency radio waves normally support long-distance communication by refraction from the upper layers. When a strong enough flare produces ionisation in the D layer, radio waves interacting with electrons there lose energy through more frequent collisions, so HF signals can be degraded or completely absorbed. NOAA calls this a radio blackout and says it primarily affects the 3 to 30 MHz band.[^2]

NOAA's D-Region Absorption Prediction product correlates flare intensity with the strength and spread of D-layer absorption. Because the layer depends on sunlight, blackouts are a sunlit-side effect, consistent with NOAA's general statement that the night side of Earth has much less ionisation than the day side.[^2][^3]

Retained sources do not give an altitude range for the D layer, so its boundaries are left unstated here. The layer should not be confused with the whole ionosphere, and it should not be confused with the radio blackout, which is the absorption effect that occurs there.[^1][^2][^3]
