A planet passing between its star and observer dims the star's light slightly, revealing its presence through this transit method.[^1]

A transit reveals a planet through the dimming rather than by direct sight of the planet, and the dimming appears in a light curve, a graph of light received over time.[^1] NASA describes Kepler as detecting planets by observing tiny dips in the brightness of a star when a planet crosses in front of it.[^2]

NASA states that the size of the orbit follows from the orbital period and the star's mass, and the size of the planet from how much the star's brightness falls.[^1][^3] Light that passes through a planet's atmosphere during a transit can also be analysed to learn about atmospheric content, a technique NASA calls transit spectroscopy.[^1][^3]

None of these sources give detection limits or a current discovery total, and transits only occur when the orbit aligns with the observer, a condition not quantified in the sources.[^1]
