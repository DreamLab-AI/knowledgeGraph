A Lissajous orbit is a generally quasi-periodic trajectory near a libration point, formed by combining oscillations in different directions whose frequencies do not usually share one period.[^1356][^1357]

## Shape and relation to halo orbits

In the linear neighbourhood of a collinear point, a spacecraft can oscillate both within and out of the primaries’ plane and perpendicular to it. When the frequencies differ, the three-dimensional path does not close after one cycle; selected projections can resemble mathematical Lissajous figures. In a simplified construction, a halo orbit is a special periodic case in which the relevant oscillations are synchronised.[^1357]

This dynamical distinction is more reliable than describing one path as simply “smaller” than the other. Mission and outreach pages may use the names broadly, while the strict dynamical literature distinguishes periodic halo solutions from quasi-periodic Lissajous motion. Record the mission’s own orbit designation, model and frame; the real spacecraft follows an ephemeris shaped by the full Solar System and non-gravitational forces.[^1356][^1371]

## Maintenance and mission examples

Lissajous paths near collinear points can drift away from a reference under unstable modes and perturbations. Orbit determination, covariance and station-keeping design control that departure. Required manoeuvres depend on the chosen trajectory, tracking, force model, spacecraft attitude and mission limits. A 1993 comparative simulation did not find a statistically significant difference in mean two-year station-keeping costs between its halo-type and Lissajous cases, which is a result for that study rather than a general cost rule.[^1356]

ESA describes Gaia’s former Sun-Earth L2 trajectory as a Lissajous orbit with an approximately 180-day period. ESA mission documentation records executed station-keeping manoeuvres during Gaia’s operational era. Gaia left L2 for a heliocentric retirement orbit and was passivated on 27 March 2025, so it is a historical example, not a current L2 mission.[^1353][^1354][^1370]

ESA’s orbit comparison labels Gaia’s trajectory Lissajous and JWST’s halo. These are mission designations supported by flight-dynamics records, not a claim that either ephemeris is an exact solution of the circular restricted model. Surrey research includes maintenance of quasi-periodic orbits; Strathclyde researchers have studied solar-sail control at quasi-periodic libration-point trajectories. These papers establish UK research activity, not deployment of the proposed sail-control method.[^1358][^1359][^1371]
