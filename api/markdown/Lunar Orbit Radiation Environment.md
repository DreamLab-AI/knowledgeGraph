Lunar orbit radiation environment is the time-varying particle and photon field encountered by a spacecraft orbiting the Moon, together with the shielding that modifies that field.

## Radiation field

Galactic cosmic rays (GCRs) and solar energetic particles (SEPs) are the principal deep-space particle sources. GCRs provide a persistent background that varies with solar modulation; SEPs arrive episodically and can produce much higher fluxes during an event.[^1][^2] Lunar-orbit measurements and models must therefore state the solar conditions and time interval they represent.

LRO’s Cosmic Ray Telescope for the Effects of Radiation (CRaTER) measures GCRs and SEPs with tissue-equivalent material behind different thicknesses of material. This helps characterise transport through shielding, but the instrument’s response is not a direct measurement of every crew member’s organ dose or every spacecraft component’s exposure.[^1]

## Shielding and dose outputs

The Moon blocks part of the incoming field from a given orbital position. Spacecraft structure and mission shielding further change the transmitted particle spectrum, and interactions in material can create secondary particles. An orbiting vehicle does not have the same radiation geometry as a person or detector on the surface.

Absorbed dose, dose equivalent or effective dose, particle spectra and single-event effects describe different outcomes. A useful estimate records the measured or modelled quantity, detector or tissue response, shielding material and thickness, trajectory, integration interval, and solar reference conditions.[^1][^3]

Transport and biological-risk models retain uncertainty from source spectra, particle transport and nuclear physics. NASA’s OLTARIS documentation specifically cautions that secondary-particle distributions and thick shielding are difficult cases, and that some quoted uncertainty estimates were not calculated with the tool’s current code version.[^3]

NASA crew-system requirements use specified reference spectra and apply to defined mission classes. They are design requirements, not universal dose thresholds for every lunar orbiter.[^4] The LRO CRaTER south-pole maps, in turn, are a preliminary assessment of primary dose at surface sites; they should not be read as global orbital-dose measurements.[^2]
