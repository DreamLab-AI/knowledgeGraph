An electrospray thruster is an electric propulsion device that produces thrust by electrostatically extracting and accelerating ions or droplets from a low-vapour-pressure, electrically conductive liquid propellant.[^1][^2]

In the colloid form described by NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, a large potential applied to a room temperature liquid such as an ionic liquid forms a Taylor cone, and ions and droplets leave the cone and are accelerated through a strong electric field.[^2] The NASA small-spacecraft survey groups the technologies by propellant: ionic-liquid electrosprays, and field emission devices that use low-melting-point metals stored as a solid and heated before operation. Thrusters that mainly emit droplets are called colloid thrusters.[^1]

Propellant can be fed actively with pressurant gas or passively by capillary forces.[^1] Operating issues reported in the sources include water absorbed by the ionic liquid, which can form bubbles in the feed system, and plume interactions with the extractor electrode, which are described as limiting the operational lifetime of ionic-liquid systems.[^2][^3]

According to the survey, droplet emission can degrade specific impulse and thrust efficiency even in devices that mainly emit ions, and claimed performance derived from plume characteristics needs verification in a relevant environment.[^1] The two research items are a conference abstract and a preprint abstract, showing the named issues rather than general limits for all designs.[^2][^3]
