Radar foreshortening is the compression of slopes that face a side-looking radar, which occurs because the beam reaches the base of a tall tilted feature before it reaches the top.[^1]

Radar measures distance in slant range, so returns from a large slope area arrive at about the same time and the slope appears shortened and bright in the image. The severity varies with the slope and the incidence angle of the beam, and it is greatest when the beam is perpendicular to the slope, so that the slope, base and top are imaged together.[^1][^2]

Foreshortening is one of three key distortions in synthetic aperture radar images, with layover and shadow. Layover is described as a similar effect in which the top of a feature is reached before its base, and both lead to radar shadow. Natural Resources Canada notes that foreshortening and layover are consequences of relief displacement.[^1][^2]

Radiometric terrain correction can correct foreshortened areas using a digital elevation model. Areas affected by layover or shadow have no returns to correct, and the product guide supplies a layover and shadow mask to identify them. The sources opened here do not quantify how well a correction performs, which depends on the elevation model and the product.[^2]
