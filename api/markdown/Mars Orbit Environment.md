Mars orbit environment is the set of gravitational, atmospheric, plasma, illumination, thermal and impactor conditions experienced by a spacecraft orbiting Mars.

## Gravity and nearby bodies

Mars’s uneven terrain perturbs spacecraft motion through variations in the planet’s gravity field. Mission analysis must use an appropriate gravity solution and propagate the orbit over the mission interval; a nominal orbit label does not describe its long-term evolution.[^1]

Phobos and Deimos are natural bodies near Mars. Their ephemerides and gravity matter for trajectories passing close to them. During a close Phobos flyby, Mars Express experienced a small velocity deflection caused by the moon’s gravity.[^2]

## Atmosphere and drag

Mars has a thin carbon-dioxide-dominated atmosphere, but it can still produce significant drag during low-periapsis passes. Aerobraking uses repeated atmospheric passes to lower orbital energy and reshape an arrival orbit; it is a controlled mission phase, not a condition that affects every Mars orbiter equally.[^1][^3]

Upper-atmosphere density varies by latitude, local solar time, season and atmospheric circulation. Dust storms can heat lower atmospheric layers and raise density at aerobraking altitude, changing drag between passes. Aerobraking plans therefore need density margins and operational monitoring.[^4][^5]

## Plasma, thermal and impactor conditions

Mars has no global internally generated magnetic field, although its crust has local magnetic anomalies. The solar wind interacts with the ionosphere to create an induced magnetosphere and electric currents, so the near-Mars plasma environment changes with solar-wind conditions.[^6]

Spacecraft thermal balance depends on solar flux at Mars, reflected sunlight and infrared emission from the planet, spacecraft-generated heat, orientation, eclipse history and radiative cooling. Mars surface temperatures are not the orbiter’s thermal boundary conditions; mission-specific radiance, albedo and orbit geometry are needed.[^7]

Atmospheric dust storms affect the Martian atmosphere and drag. Meteoroid impacts are a separate orbital hazard. NASA’s MEM documentation says its interplanetary submodel is not intended for a spacecraft inside the sphere of influence of a significant body such as Mars, so a Mars-orbit design should not substitute that submodel for a validated local flux model.[^8]
