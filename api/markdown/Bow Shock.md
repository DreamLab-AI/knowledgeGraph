Bow shock is the region sunward of Earth where the supersonic solar wind is decelerated to subsonic speed before being deflected around the planet.[^1]

NOAA describes the magnetosphere as the region where Earth's magnetic field dominates over that of interplanetary space.[^2] ESA says the shock is created when the supersonic solar wind is suddenly slowed as it approaches that magnetic shield, and likens it to the sonic boom of an aircraft passing through the sound barrier.[^3] NASA adds that most solar wind particles are heated and slowed at the shock and detour around the Earth in the magnetosheath.[^4]

ESA's magnetosphere graphic places the bow shock where the solar wind begins to plough into the magnetosphere, with the magnetosheath as the chaotic region between the shock and the magnetopause.[^5] NASA explains the name by analogy with the bow wave of a fast boat and notes that a bow shock occurs in three dimensions and also forms around other fast-moving objects in space.[^6]

Analogies used above are explanatory and do not make the shock a solid surface. Sources opened here give no single distance for the shock from Earth, and the ESA Cluster pages are no longer updated, so figures for its position or thickness should come from current measurement papers instead.[^1][^3]
