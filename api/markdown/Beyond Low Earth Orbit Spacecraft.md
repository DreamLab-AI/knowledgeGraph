A beyond-Low Earth Orbit spacecraft is a platform designed or operated for a mission phase outside the near-Earth region conventionally called LEO.[^1447][^1452]

Possible platforms include crew vehicles, scientific probes, communications relays, orbiters, landers and rovers. The mission regime and vehicle class are separate, and a mission may pass through more than one regime. BioSentinel continued in a heliocentric orbit after its lunar flyby, while the Lunar Pathfinder case study describes a planned commercial lunar relay. Beyond LEO does not necessarily mean interplanetary flight or escape from Earth's gravity.[^1447][^1449][^1450]

Robotic and crew-capable platforms face different requirements. NASA's BioSentinel CubeSat studied radiation after a lunar flyby and continued on a heliocentric trajectory. Orion is a human-rated vehicle; Artemis I tested it without crew on a lunar-distance mission and return. Neither case makes crew carriage a defining property of beyond-LEO spacecraft.[^1449][^1455][^1456]

Design depends on where and how long the spacecraft operates. Beyond Earth's magnetic protection, radiation includes galactic cosmic rays and variable solar-particle events. Greater distance can bring longer communication delays, more autonomous fault response and longer waits for ground support. Lunar assets on the far side may need a relay to reach Earth, while a planetary encounter can add mission-specific planetary-protection constraints.[^1445][^1446][^1447][^1450][^1452]

UK involvement spans different maturity levels. The UK Space Agency's 2025–26 annual report describes the UK's investment in ESA's Moonlight lunar communications programme and commercial connectivity development. Its Lunar Pathfinder case study, last updated in December 2024, described a UK-built commercial relay spacecraft planned for 2026 and outlined its navigation and radiation-monitor experiments. That older case study establishes the planned design and UK contribution, not current launch or operational status.[^1450][^1451]

Return-capable spacecraft need thermal protection and recovery systems matched to the trajectory. NASA reports that Orion returned from Artemis I at nearly 25,000 mph and that lunar-return entry is faster and hotter than return from LEO. The CAA's orbital operator licence can cover outer-space activities beyond Earth orbit. UK-licensed missions with planned planetary encounters and contamination risk must also address the UK Planetary Protection Technical Framework; this obligation is scoped to those missions, not every spacecraft above LEO.[^1435][^1452][^1455][^1456]
