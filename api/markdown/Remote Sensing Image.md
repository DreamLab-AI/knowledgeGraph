A remote sensing image is a two-dimensional array of pixels, arranged in rows and columns, in which each pixel represents an area of the Earth’s surface and records what a sensor measured for that area.[^1][^2]

NASA describes the pixels of satellite imagery as representing the relative reflected light energy recorded for that part of the image, and an imaging radiometer as an instrument that scans to give a two-dimensional array of pixels from which an image may be produced.[^2] Radar images follow the same pixel logic but are built differently: SAR amplitude products are focused images stripped of phase information and multi-looked to give pixels of approximately square size.[^3]

Imagery can come from aerial cameras, radar, lidar and satellite sensors on drones, aircraft, balloons and satellites, and the spatial properties of an image refer to the area of the Earth’s surface covered by one pixel.[^4][^1]

An image in this sense is the pixel array and not a statement about its processing state. NASA reserves derived geophysical variables at the resolution and location of the source data for Level 2, so a pixel grid may hold raw counts or derived quantities depending on the level.[^5] The retained sources do not establish a single formal definition of the term shared across agencies, and they say nothing about image file formats.
