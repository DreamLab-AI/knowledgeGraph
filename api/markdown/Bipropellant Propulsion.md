Bipropellant propulsion stores a fuel and an oxidiser separately and meters both into a thrust chamber. The two-stream architecture permits energetic combustion and often higher specific impulse than a comparable monopropellant system, but it adds tanks, plumbing, valves and control of mixture ratio and arrival timing.[^1]

## Propellants and ignition

Mixed oxides of nitrogen (MON) or nitrogen tetroxide with monomethylhydrazine (MMH) or hydrazine are established storable spacecraft combinations. They are hypergolic: fuel and oxidiser ignite when they meet, so the chamber does not need a separate spark or torch igniter.[^5][^6][^7] This simplifies one function while making unintended contact, leakage and residual propellant particularly hazardous.

Other pairs need an ignition system or use catalytic decomposition of one fluid to initiate the second. An ESA peroxide/kerosene programme tested catalysts, injectors, cold starts, pulses and long burns but closed in 2016 at TRL 3–4. Catalyst lifetime, propellant grade, injector and ignition behaviour, thermal design and compatibility still required work.[^9] It is evidence of ground development rather than an available flight-qualified system.

## Feed and chamber architecture

A pressure-fed system uses gas pressure to drive both liquids from their tanks. Regulation can keep inlet conditions comparatively stable; a blowdown design accepts falling pressure and the associated changes in thrust, mixture ratio and minimum impulse bit. NASA's current Transit Habitat study examined regulated and blowdown MMH/MON3 concepts with redundant isolation, health instrumentation and propellant-management devices, but the vehicle and its propulsion requirements remained conceptual.[^4]

Pump-fed designs trade lower tank pressure against electrical power, moving parts and dynamic control. ESA's ongoing EPFB activity modelled separate electric fuel and oxidiser pumps with pressure dampers because pump ripple can disturb mass flow and mixture ratio.[^3] At the recorded March 2024 status, correlated models and component-level test evidence existed, while an integrated hot-fire campaign and a space-rated pump remained future work.

Injector design determines atomisation, mixing, heat release and wall loading. Valve timing must prevent harmful lead, lag or trapped propellant. Chamber and nozzle materials must tolerate high temperature, oxidising species and repeated thermal cycling. Feed design must also keep pressurant out of the chamber flow and prevent fuel and oxidiser from crossing into each other's storage or pressurisation paths.[^2][^4]

## Performance with conditions

Nammo specifies its UK-made LEROS 1b dual-mode MON/hydrazine engine at 635 N at nominal inlet pressure and a minimum specific impulse of 317 seconds. Its listed operating envelope is 15–21 bar inlet pressure and an oxidiser-to-fuel mixture ratio of 0.8–0.9.[^7] These numbers describe that engine and propellant configuration. They are not a general bipropellant range.

Nammo also reports qualification demonstrations of a 2,520-second single firing, 20,500 seconds cumulative firing and 70 restarts with the chamber initially hot.[^7] These are manufacturer qualification claims. Their value lies in the declared configuration and test conditions; they should not be transferred to another chamber, valve set, propellant pair or duty cycle.

## Handling, qualification and failure

Storable hypergolic liquids remain toxic, corrosive and reactive. Loading, transport and ground test require controlled materials, protective equipment, remote operations, leak detection and verified inhibits. Flight design must address leakage, a valve stuck open or shut, regulator or pump failure, pressure ripple, injector blockage, mixture-ratio excursion, hard starts and thermal damage.[^1][^2][^4]

Qualification covers steady and pulsed firing, restarts, throughput, inlet and mixture-ratio extremes, thermal conditioning, valve cycles, leakage, vibration and vacuum operation.[^2] A thruster with flight heritage does not make a new tank, feed and control system flight-proven. Integrated-system evidence must match the intended propellant, scale, environment, lifetime and fault policy.

## Deployed examples and UK capability

Orion's European Service Module provides an operational pressure-fed example. Two tanks hold MON and two hold MMH; two high-pressure helium tanks, pressure-control equipment, sensors, shut-off valves and filters feed three engine classes.[^5] Its published capacities and ratings belong to that spacecraft architecture rather than to bipropellant propulsion as a class.

The LEROS 1b has manufacturer-recorded flight use on missions to Jupiter, Mercury and Mars, and Nammo states that its apogee engines are hot-fire tested at Westcott.[^7] A later Westcott- and Dublin-developed LEROS 4-ET performed trans-lunar injection, lunar-orbit insertion and descent-orbit insertion for Blue Ghost before the lander's successful 2 March 2025 landing.[^8] The UK Space Agency-hosted account was written by Nammo UK, so the specific industrial contribution is well identified but remains company-authored.
