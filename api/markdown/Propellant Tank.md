A propellant tank is the vessel in a propulsion system that stores propellant and, together with feed lines, valves and pressurisation devices, forms part of the system that delivers it to the thrust chamber.[^1]

In a pressure-fed liquid engine the tank pressure drives the propellant to the chamber, and a NASA Marshall chapter states that such systems are limited to relatively low chamber pressures because high pressures make the vehicle tanks too heavy. With a turbopump the tank pressure is described as practically independent of chamber pressure, although the tanks still need pressurisation to keep the pump inlet from cavitating.[^1]

In low gravity the tank must also deliver liquid free of vapour. NASA's small-spacecraft survey states that small spacecraft have historically used bellows or membrane tanks to ensure propellant delivery and expulsion, and that propellant management devices separate the liquid and vapour phases inside the tank in any gravitational or thermal environment where those tank types are not used.[^2] The survey also reports diaphragm tanks and additively manufactured tank shells under development for small spacecraft.[^2]

These sources describe liquid propulsion systems and do not establish requirements for solid or cold-gas storage, nor sizing rules for any particular tank. Flight heritage statements on propellant management devices in the survey are tied to the propellants listed there.[^2]
