An ecliptic coordinate system is a celestial coordinate system whose fundamental plane is the ecliptic, the plane of the Earth's orbit around the Sun, with longitude measured along the ecliptic and latitude measured north or south of it.[^1][^2]

NASA describes the ecliptic plane as inclined 23.4 degrees to the celestial equator, and as the great circle along which the Sun and planets appear to travel. In the solar ecliptic system defined by the NASA National Space Science Data Center, the Z axis is normal to the ecliptic plane and northward, the X axis points towards the vernal equinox, and the Y axis completes the right-handed set.[^1][^2]

Ecliptic longitude increases from zero in the X direction towards Y, and that latitude runs to plus ninety degrees towards the north ecliptic pole and minus ninety degrees towards the south pole. That system is heliocentric, so a geocentric version would change the origin and not the plane, and the sources here do not describe a geocentric ecliptic system in detail.[^2]

Vernal equinox direction moves slowly, so the equinox epoch matters. The NASA page lists B1950, mean of date and J2000 as commonly used choices, and the reference chapter explains why a single snapshot epoch is adopted. Ecliptic coordinates differ from equatorial right ascension and declination, which are tied to the celestial equator. Both pages in this set come from NASA, so no independent second source was opened for the definition.[^2][^3][^1]
