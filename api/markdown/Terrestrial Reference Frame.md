A terrestrial reference frame is the realisation of a terrestrial reference system, through the realisation of its origin, orientation axes and scale and their time evolution, by a set of points with precisely determined coordinates.[^1]

The IERS Conventions distinguish the system, which has a theoretical definition, from the frame to which users have access.[^1] For the International Terrestrial Reference System, the IERS gives the centre of mass of the whole Earth, including oceans and atmosphere, as the origin, the metre as the unit of length, an initial orientation from the BIH and a no-net-rotation condition with regard to horizontal tectonic motions for its evolution.[^2]

The International Terrestrial Reference Frame is the realisation of that system, estimated from station coordinates and velocities observed by several space geodetic techniques.[^2] The ITRF site says its realisations include station positions and velocities, which is why they can be used to compare observations from different epochs.[^3] Such a frame follows the Earth's diurnal rotation and is not an inertial system.[^4]

Several ITRF realisations exist, so a coordinate is complete only when the realisation and epoch are stated. The sources opened here do not cover national datums or the relationship of other frames, such as those used by navigation satellite systems, to the ITRF.[^2][^3]
