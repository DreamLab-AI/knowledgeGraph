Coordinated Universal Time is the time scale produced by the BIPM that runs at the same rate as International Atomic Time and differs from it only by a whole number of seconds, and that serves as the basis of civil time in most countries.[^1][^2]

UTC is obtained from atomic time by adding or removing a leap second as needed, so that it stays aligned with the Earth's rotation. A leap second is announced when the difference between UTC and UT1 approaches 0.9 seconds, and the IERS is responsible for the decision to insert one.[^2][^3][^1]

UTC alone does not give the Earth's orientation. Users who need the rotation angle apply observed or predicted values of UT1 minus UTC supplied by the IERS to UTC, and a broadcast value of that difference is coarser than the values in IERS products.[^1][^3]

UTC is described as a scale, not a clock reading or a time zone. They also record that the 27th General Conference on Weights and Measures kept the existing process for aligning UTC with the Earth's rotation while envisaging a larger tolerance in future, with a new process expected from 2035. The ITU text is a 2023 overview, so the planned arrangement should not be read as the rule now in force, and the sources opened here do not give current leap-second or GNSS time offsets.[^2]
