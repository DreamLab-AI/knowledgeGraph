A liquid rocket engine is a rocket engine in which stored liquid fuel and liquid oxidiser are pumped into a combustion chamber, burned, and expelled as hot exhaust through a nozzle to produce thrust.[^1]

NASA Glenn describes the thrust as depending on the mass flow rate through the engine, the exit velocity of the exhaust and the pressure at the nozzle exit, all of which depend on the nozzle design. Because the oxidiser is carried on board, the engine can produce thrust in a vacuum.[^1] That page is an introductory schematic and does not cover engine cycles or injector design.[^1]

Propellants reach the chamber through a feed system, which a NASA Marshall chapter classes as pressure-fed or pump-fed according to how the propellants are pressurised.[^2] Choice between them is a design trade at engine and vehicle level, and the chapter ties pump-fed systems to high-pressure, high-performance applications.[^2]

In small spacecraft, liquid propulsion includes monopropellant and bipropellant systems. A NASA survey notes that Earth-storable propellants have dominated in-space chemical propulsion for decades, with hydrazine for monopropellants and nitrogen tetroxide with a hydrazine fuel for bipropellants.[^3] That survey covers spacecraft below a stated mass limit, so it does not describe launch-vehicle engines.[^3]
