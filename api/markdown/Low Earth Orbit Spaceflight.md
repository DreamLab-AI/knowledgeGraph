Low Earth orbit (LEO) spaceflight is a mission or trajectory in which a spacecraft completes an Earth-centred orbit in the near-Earth region, conventionally extending to about 2,000 km altitude.[^1438][^1441]

LEO is a family of orbits rather than one path or altitude. NASA's SCaN glossary gives a range of 80–2,000 km, while ESA describes 160 km as a practical minimum for a spacecraft to orbit Earth. Atmospheric drag and required orbital lifetime make the lower edge dependent on the mission and vehicle; the commonly used upper boundary is a taxonomy convention, not a physical or legal discontinuity.[^1438][^1439][^1441]

Orbital status depends on the trajectory and velocity, not altitude alone. UK regulations define “stable orbit” for a launch vehicle in their regulatory context as an orbit with minimum perigee of 130 km after allowing for natural influences, capable of continuing for at least one Earth orbit. That specific legal definition should not be treated as the universal lower boundary of LEO.[^1437][^1440]

LEO spacecraft move rapidly and revisit the same orbital geometry in short periods. NASA gives about 17,000 mph at 242 km and roughly 90 minutes for one LEO revolution as an educational example; exact speed and period vary with orbit. The regime includes crewed stations, robotic observatories and Earth-observation spacecraft; UK funding also supports commercial LEO connectivity development. The orbit does not identify whether a mission is crewed, scientific, public or commercial.[^1439][^1440][^1441][^1451]

Operations must account for atmospheric drag, orbital debris and radiation. NASA's debris review describes LEO as extending from Earth's surface to 2,000 km and notes that debris can threaten both human and robotic missions. Its debris quantities refer to January 2020 and should not be repeated as a current inventory. Radiation also varies with orbit and shielding; LEO retains some protection from Earth's magnetic environment but is not radiation-free.[^1441][^1444]

Communications can use direct-to-ground links, relays and space networks as the spacecraft moves through available coverage. NASA's Artemis II architecture assigns its Near Space Network to launch, Earth-orbit and return phases, while DSN support is used beyond LEO. This is an example architecture rather than a requirement for every LEO mission.[^1447][^1448]
