Lunar surface radiation environment is the natural radiation on or adjacent to the surface of the Moon, made up of free space radiation partly shielded by the Moon, albedo radiation from the lunar crust and particles from the decay of radioactive elements in the crust.[^1]

Three contributions are listed in the ontology definition: free space radiation that the body of the Moon partly shields, albedo radiation produced when that radiation interacts with the lunar crust, and decay products of crustal radioactive elements. The parent definition of the lunar radiation environment adds that it varies with location and time, for example with shielding by the Moon itself and with solar particle events.[^1]

Measurements from the surface are few. The Lunar Lander Neutrons and Dosimetry instrument on the Chang'e-4 lander reported the first surface measurements of exposure to both charged and neutral particles, at a single far-side site. The authors describe galactic cosmic ray exposure as generally a low dose rate, compared with sporadic and sometimes very intense solar particle events.[^2]

Dose rates in that work are measured in silicon detectors and converted to quantities comparable with biological exposure, so silicon dose rate, water dose rate and dose equivalent should not be mixed. The sources do not establish a lunar-wide dose rate, and a value from one site and period should not be read as the exposure of a crew with different shielding.[^2]
