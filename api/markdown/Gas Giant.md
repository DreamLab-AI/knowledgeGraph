A gas giant is a large planet mostly composed of helium and/or hydrogen.[^1]

NASA says such planets, like Jupiter and Saturn, have no hard surfaces and instead have swirling gases above a solid core. Its overview of the solar system places Jupiter and Saturn in this class and notes that the giant planets of the outer solar system lack hard surfaces.[^1][^2]

NASA reports that gas giant exoplanets can be much larger than Jupiter and much closer to their stars. Those near their stars are often called hot Jupiters, with temperatures in the thousands of degrees. NASA outlines three hypotheses for how they reach such orbits and says it could be more likely that gas giants develop beyond the snow line, where solid cores form more easily. Spitzer observations reported in 2007 were taken as evidence that gas giants form within the first 10 million years of a Sun-like star’s life, or not at all.[^1]

NASA treats Uranus and Neptune as a separate class, the ice giants, which are mostly hydrogen and helium but also contain water, ammonia and methane. The retained sources give no mass or radius threshold for a gas giant and do not settle how the formation hypotheses divide among individual planets.[^2][^3]
