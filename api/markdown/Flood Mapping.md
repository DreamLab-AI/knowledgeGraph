Flood mapping locates land affected by water or describes the probability, depth, velocity or consequences of flooding. The name covers several products with different meanings: an image-derived outline of an event, a forecast of what may happen, a modelled risk zone, or a planning scenario. A useful map therefore states its source, acquisition or valid time, spatial scale and intended decision.

## Observations and models

Satellite, aircraft and drone imagery can show water visible to a sensor at one moment. The Environment Agency describes its rapid remotely sensed estimates as raw and unvalidated during incident use. Different sensors and resolutions can yield different outlines, and a snapshot is unlikely to capture the peak. A mapped extent also does not establish that an individual property flooded internally. Area teams later compare candidate outlines with marks left by the water, local knowledge and other evidence before accepting a recorded outline.[^1]

Hydrological models estimate how rainfall and catchments generate flow; hydraulic models represent water moving through channels, floodplains and built areas. Their outputs depend on terrain, boundary conditions, structures, defences, climate assumptions and grid resolution. Environment Agency guidance requires current inputs, sensitivity testing, records of uncertainty, and calibration against gauge data or validation against independent flood information.[^2] National mapping can guide an assessment, but a recent national model may still lack the scale or detail needed at a particular site.

## UK operational use

The Flood Forecasting Centre combines Met Office meteorology with Environment Agency hydrology for river, tidal, coastal and extreme-rainfall hazards in England. Related partnerships operate in Scotland and Wales. The Met Office reports that five-day Flood Guidance Statements are distributed daily to emergency responders.[^3] UKCEH models support these services and public-warning systems, with forecasts ranging from hours to seasons. Its HYRAD system combines rainfall radar, gauges and forecasts for operational analysis.[^4]

Forecast guidance is evidence about a possible future state, rather than a direct observation of every flooded location. Published national risk products are also designed for particular planning and public-information purposes. The Environment Agency says they do not determine risk at individual-property level and are updated as new data and methods become available. Local evidence can take several months to review and incorporate, while a site-specific flood-risk assessment may refine the national picture.[^5]

## Satellite coverage and limits

Synthetic-aperture radar can observe through cloud and at night, which makes Sentinel-1 valuable during storms. Copernicus Global Flood Monitoring incorporated data from the operational Sentinel-1C satellite on 28 October 2025. The additional acquisitions shortened revisit intervals and provided more recent baselines for change detection.[^6] Revisit remains periodic, however, and fast-moving floods can change between passes.

Radar does not reveal every flooded surface. Buildings, vegetation and sensor geometry can obscure or alter the return, while wind-roughened water and other dark surfaces can complicate classification. A 2025 Copernicus example in Brittany showed floodwater missed beneath canopy and in an urban settlement even near the flood peak. The EXFLOS service combines observed water, a terrain model and hydraulic simulation to extend depth estimates into concealed areas under a 30-minute processing requirement.[^7] Those concealed-area values are modelled estimates constrained by the observation and terrain; they are not direct measurements beneath each tree or building.
