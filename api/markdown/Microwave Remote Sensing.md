Microwave remote sensing is the acquisition of information about the Earth's surface, without contact, by sensing reflected or emitted energy in the microwave part of the spectrum.[^1][^2]

Tutorials describe the microwave region as roughly 1 cm to 1 m in wavelength and state microwave sensing includes both active and passive forms.[^2] Passive instruments detect energy emitted from the natural environment, while active instruments emit energy and collect data based on changes in the return signal.[^3]

Longer microwave wavelengths can penetrate cloud cover, haze, dust and all but the heaviest rainfall, because they are less affected by the atmospheric scattering that acts on shorter optical wavelengths.[^2] That property depends on wavelength, and the source names the heaviest rainfall as an exception. ESA's SMOS mission is an example, making global observations of soil moisture over land and salinity over oceans with an L-band radiometer.[^4]

Sources used here give only a short account of active microwave systems such as radar and do not set out their processing. Wavelength limits are approximate tutorial figures.
