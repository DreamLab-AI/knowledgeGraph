A supernova remnant is the expanding structure left by a supernova explosion, consisting of ejected stellar material and the interstellar gas that the blast wave heats and sweeps up.[^1][^2]

NASA describes a remnant as the remains of a supernova explosion and says the shock wave heats and stirs the interstellar material it meets, producing a shell of hot material.[^1] ESA's XMM-Newton caption adds that the expanding shell contains swept-up interstellar material as well as ejecta, and that X-ray observations of remnants allow astronomers to identify the abundance and distribution of elements formed late in the star's life.[^2]

A review of X-ray observations reports differences by explosion type. Type Ia remnants tend to be regularly shaped with stratified ejecta, whereas core-collapse remnants have patchy ejecta distributions. The same review describes remnant shocks as among the best Galactic examples of high Mach number, collisionless shocks.[^3] These are tendencies described by one review, not rules for every remnant.

A remnant is the extended expanding structure and not the compact object that may be left at the explosion site. The ESA caption notes that a neutron star or black hole may remain at the centre but be hidden by the shell, which keeps the two things distinct.[^2] The pages opened here do not give quantitative figures for remnant lifetimes or cosmic-ray yields.[^1][^3]
