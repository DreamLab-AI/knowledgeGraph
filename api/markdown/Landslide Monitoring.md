Landslide monitoring is the observation of slopes and the conditions that precede or follow their failure, so that past events can be mapped and hazard assessed.[^1][^2]

USGS defines a landslide as a wide variety of processes that move slope-forming material, including rock, soil and artificial fill, downward and outward.[^1] Because the movement types differ, a method suited to one type is not necessarily suited to another, and the sources do not rank the methods.

NASA says landslides may be initiated by rainfall, snowmelt, earthquakes or volcanic activity, or by mining and construction.[^2] NASA Earthdata describes data on past landslides, including an open global landslide inventory, and data on erosion and fluvial processes in sloped areas that help in developing warning systems. It also presents satellite rainfall data as an aid in assessing landslide and flood hazards.[^2]

These sources, opened here, do not establish detection thresholds, forecast skill or the use of radar interferometry for individual slopes, so none is claimed. Rainfall is one input to assessment and does not by itself show which slope will fail.[^2][^1]

[^2]: NASA Earthdata, “Landslides”. https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/topics/human-dimensions/natural-hazards/landslides
