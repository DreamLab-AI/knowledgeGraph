Lunar orbit environment is the set of gravitational, illumination, thermal, particle, plasma and dust conditions experienced by a spacecraft while it orbits the Moon.

## Gravity, terrain and orbit evolution

The Moon’s gravity field is uneven. Mass concentrations beneath impact basins and visible terrain such as mountains and craters both contribute to local gravity variations, which can perturb low-altitude spacecraft orbits.[^1][^2] Topography also controls surface visibility and shadow geometry, so a terrain model and a gravity solution answer related but different mission questions.

Earth and the Sun affect motion and eclipse geometry in the Earth–Moon system. Solar gravity perturbs the Moon’s orbital parameters, and Earth’s oblateness also contributes; spacecraft trajectory work therefore needs an epoch-specific propagation model rather than a timeless Keplerian description.[^3] Resonance and low-altitude stability are mission-dependent.

## Illumination and thermal conditions

An orbiter alternates between direct sunlight and shadow. Lunar eclipses interrupt solar input to a spacecraft in Earth’s shadow and can cool its hardware; LRO’s thermal and battery records show how this response depends on the vehicle and operations.[^4][^5] Surface temperatures are a separate boundary condition: the Moon’s thin exosphere cannot distribute heat as an atmosphere does, so sunlit and shaded terrain can differ sharply in temperature.[^6]

Spacecraft thermal balance also includes reflected sunlight from the Moon, infrared emission from the Moon, heat generated onboard, and heat radiated to space. Orbit altitude, spacecraft attitude, absorptivity, emissivity, thermal mass and eclipse duration all affect the result.[^5][^7] Surface temperature alone does not specify spacecraft temperature.

## Plasma, dust and impactors

The Moon moves between the solar wind and Earth’s magnetotail, changing the plasma and magnetic context encountered around the Moon. It spends only part of its orbit inside the magnetotail, so this is a time-varying exposure rather than permanent Earth-like shielding.[^8]

LADEE’s Lunar Dust Experiment measured impacts from dust around the Moon and mapped dust populations by local time and the Moon’s position relative to Earth’s magnetosphere.[^9][^10] These observations support a sparse dust-exosphere description; they do not establish a uniform, dense dust shell. A spacecraft also faces sporadic meteoroid impacts. NASA’s meteoroid model treats lunar-orbit focusing and lunar/Earth shielding separately from its interplanetary model.[^11]

## Scope

For a given mission, environmental conditions depend on orbit altitude, inclination, epoch, local time, spacecraft attitude, eclipse history and the model versions used for gravity, thermal and meteoroid analysis. Lunar surface dust, surface radiation and surface thermal conditions should be linked to this page, but kept distinct from the conditions measured by an orbiter.
