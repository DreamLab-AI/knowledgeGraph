Comparative planetology examines the same physical and chemical processes across several planets, moons or small bodies. Differences in mass, composition, atmosphere, distance from the Sun and geological history act as natural experiments. Comparing their effects helps explain why related worlds followed different paths and tests whether an explanation developed for one body works elsewhere.

## Evidence across worlds

Researchers combine remote sensing, in situ instruments, laboratory experiments, numerical models, meteorites and returned samples. Atmospheres can be compared through their composition, circulation, clouds, weather and escape. Surface images and spectra reveal cratering, volcanism, erosion and mineralogy. Gravity, magnetic fields and seismic measurements constrain interiors, although features such as buried oceans remain model-based inferences rather than direct observations.

Earth offers accessible field sites and ground truth. Planetary analogues reproduce selected features, allowing researchers to test instruments and interpretations before applying them to distant observations.[^1] Their limits matter: an Earth desert or ice sheet does not reproduce another world's gravity, radiation, atmosphere or full geological history. Comparisons are strongest when the measured property and the unmatched conditions are both stated.

Samples supply another line of evidence. Meteorites record early Solar System materials and the conditions in which their minerals formed. The Natural History Museum studies meteorites and curated grains returned from the asteroid Bennu to investigate planet formation and the delivery of water and organic compounds.[^2] Such compounds may be ingredients for life, but are not evidence that life produced them. Returned samples also have a better documented collection history than meteorites exposed to Earth's environment.

## UK contributions and missions

UK groups work across this comparative chain. UCL's Mullard Space Science Laboratory analyses targets including Mars, Venus, comets, Saturn and its moons, and contributes instruments and scientists to international missions.[^3] The University of Leicester compares atmospheres using spacecraft, space telescopes and ground observatories, linking composition and dynamics to planetary formation and climate.[^4] Imperial College London leads the J-MAG magnetometer on ESA's Juice mission; magnetic induction measurements will help constrain the properties of oceans thought to lie beneath icy moons.[^5]

Juice, launched in 2023 and due at Jupiter in 2031, will compare the planet and the ocean-bearing moons Ganymede, Callisto and Europa.[^6] ESA's BepiColombo was still approaching Mercury on 1 October 2026. Orbit insertion was planned for 21 November 2026 and the science phase for April 2027, so its main comparative dataset was not yet available.[^7] Mission status and dates should be checked against current operational sources.
