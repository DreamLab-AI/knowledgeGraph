Exoplanet direct imaging is the detection of a planet orbiting another star by capturing light from the planet itself, as though photographing it, rather than inferring it from indirect effects.[^1][^2]

ESO states that the main obstacle is that the light of a star is so powerful that a nearby planet is swamped by it. Its SPHERE instrument addresses this in three stages: a system that corrects for atmospheric turbulence, a coronagraph that blocks starlight, and differential imaging that exploits differences in colour or polarisation between planetary and stellar light.[^2]

NASA states that direct images so far have been mainly of giant planets still so hot from recent formation that they remain self-luminous, and that next-generation space telescopes would use a coronagraph, with starshade designs still being refined.[^1] Light captured directly from an exoplanet is described as too faint a signal to show surface features, though it can provide profiles of atmospheres.[^1]

ESO notes that only a few planets have been detected directly compared with the many found by other techniques.[^2] The cited pages do not give detection limits, contrast ratios or a catalogue of directly imaged planets, so those are outside what this page states.
