A resistojet is an electrothermal thruster. Electrical current heats a solid resistive element or heat exchanger; propellant takes up that heat and expands through a nozzle to produce thrust.12 The propellant need not be ionised. This separates the mechanism from Hall-effect and gridded ion thrusters, which accelerate charged particles with fields, and from an arcjet, which transfers electrical energy through an arc in the gas.

Heating raises exhaust velocity above the value from the same unheated gas, reducing propellant mass for a given impulse. Performance remains limited by input power, heat-transfer area, material temperature, propellant molecular mass, nozzle expansion and losses. A low specific impulse relative to plasma thrusters can accompany useful simplicity, small impulse bits or safe propellant storage. Each comparison needs the complete system and mission duty cycle.

Hardware and propellants

A flight resistojet combines a tank, pressure regulation, valves and feed lines with the heater or heat exchanger, temperature sensing, nozzle, power switching and thermal interfaces. A liquid propellant such as water also needs reliable acquisition and vaporisation before superheating. Heat leaking through the mount or radiating towards the spacecraft affects the thermal design.

Hydrogen, ammonia, nitrogen, carbon dioxide, methane, hydrazine-decomposition products, nitrous oxide, butane, water and water-alcohol mixtures have appeared in research or flight-demonstration programmes.3456 This does not make one heater compatible with all of them. Propellant chemistry, decomposition, deposits, storage pressure, freezing, valve materials and heater oxidation change the design.

Specific impulse, thrust and efficiency must retain heater power, mass flow, inlet state and temperature. A thrust value without power cannot establish thrust-to-power ratio; a hot laboratory point cannot establish long-duration performance. SSTL reports a UK-DMC-1 experimental water system with a total mass of 188 g including 2.06 g of water and an average in-orbit thrust of 3.3 mN.5 The mission record does not provide a complete operating-point efficiency, so none should be inferred.

Lifetime and control

Heater temperature and material compatibility often set life. Thermal cycling drives fatigue and creep; local hot spots can distort a coil or heat exchanger. Deposits or reaction products can change thermal resistance and flow. Valve life, feed stability and sensor drift matter alongside the heater.

Historical NASA work illustrates both endurance and its limits. Experimental rhenium units ran on ammonia for more than 8,000 hours and one hydrogen unit exceeded 6,000 hours under a defined cyclic schedule.6 The same review identified heat-exchanger temperature distribution and creep as concerns. These were ground tests on old experimental hardware, not qualification of a current water or CubeSat product.

Water adds a phase-change component. NASA vaporiser tests reached as much as 500 hours and 250 thermal cycles, but different porous designs developed pressure-drop increases from 29% to 147% over their reported runs.7 Stable heater operation therefore does not prove stable mass flow. A flight design must test start-up, two-phase transients, freezing, residual water, scaling or oxidation and repeated thermal cycles.

Plume, power and spacecraft integration

Hot neutral gas dominates the plume, but dissociation, contamination and charged fractions depend on propellant and temperature. Integration covers impingement, surface heating, contamination, torque, sensor fields of view and interaction with other thrusters. Power switching, temperature control and wiring require conducted and radiated electromagnetic-compatibility assessment.

A NASA space-station technology programme reported 10,000 hours of resistojet life testing together with plume characterisation and electromagnetic-interference work.8 That is strong evidence that these concerns apply even to a nominally simple electrothermal device. It is a dated ground programme, not evidence for a present small-satellite product.

Vacuum chambers can change nozzle back-pressure, plume expansion and heat loss. Tests should record facility pressure, pumping, mounting, propellant condition and thrust-stand calibration. Qualification covers the controlled design and specified mechanical, thermal-vacuum, performance and endurance requirements; acceptance checks the delivered unit. Flight demonstration establishes behaviour only for the flown configuration and operations.

UK flight evidence and its limits

The University of Surrey reports that UoSAT-12 carried a Surrey-developed nitrous-oxide resistojet. Its live page writes “NO2” beside that mission but uses N2O elsewhere; nitrous oxide is N2O, whereas NO2 is nitrogen dioxide.4 The chemistry should be corrected without discarding the mission claim.

SSTL reports two further demonstrations. UK-DMC-1’s water resistojet generated measurable in-orbit torque from its reported 3.3 mN thrust. STRaND-1 launched in 2013 carrying WARP DRiVE, a water-alcohol resistojet experiment, but the same mission entry records patchy communications.5 Carriage and launch do not establish that WARP DRiVE fired successfully. These cases support UK flight-demonstration heritage, not a claim that a currently available product is qualified or operational.

ECSS-E-ST-35-01C places electric propulsion within a system-engineering framework covering functions, interfaces, environment, quality, operations and verification, subject to project tailoring.9 That controlled evidence remains necessary even when the mechanical layout appears simple.
